## A condition you impose once and never again

Normalization is a condition you impose once, at $t=0$. Nothing after
that moment re-imposes it: the dynamics keep the total probability at 1
on their own, and that is a claim you can check against a live numerical
engine rather than take on faith. This lesson turns $\int|\psi|^2dx=1$
into a working tool, then watches the norm hold under real evolution.

## Density, in the same sense as mass density

$|\psi(x)|^2$ is a probability density in the same sense as a mass density or a
population density: to get an actual probability (a plain number between
0 and 1), you integrate the density over a region. The *total* probability
integrated over all of space must be exactly 1, because the particle is
*somewhere* with certainty.

## Rescaling, regions, and why the norm holds

### Normalization

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1
$$

Any function with finite $\int|\psi|^2\,dx = N < \infty$ can be
normalized: replace $\psi \to \psi/\sqrt{N}$, since
$\int|\psi/\sqrt N|^2 dx = (1/N)\int|\psi|^2 dx = N/N = 1$.

### Probability of a region

The probability of finding the particle in $[a,b]$ is

$$
P(a \le x \le b) = \int_a^b |\psi(x)|^2\,dx
$$

which is what `Wavefunction1D.probabilityDensity()` returns
pointwise, summed (as a Riemann sum, `probability * dx`) over the
relevant grid points. The `probabilityLeftAndRightOf`
function used by the Wavefunction Explorer's <Term id="quantum-tunneling">tunneling</Term> comparison is
this same integral, split at the barrier.

### Why normalization survives time evolution

This is not a separate assumption. It's forced by the dynamics postulate.
Time evolution is $|\psi(t)\rangle = U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$ for a <Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>
$U(t)$ (derived from Hermiticity of $H$ in the Time Evolution lesson of the
last course, and reproven directly for position space two lessons ahead).
Unitary operators preserve inner products exactly:

$$
\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\psi(0)\rangle = 1
$$

using $U^\dagger U = I$. So if $\psi(0)$ starts normalized, it stays
normalized at every later time: not approximately, not on
average, but as an identity. The Wavefunction Explorer's split-operator time
evolution is built from three factors that are each individually a pure
phase (multiplication by a unit-modulus number, either pointwise in
position space or via a unitary Fourier transform), so the simulator's
`Norm ‖ψ‖` readout staying at $1.0000$ for as long as you let it run isn't
a coincidence or good luck. It's this unitarity theorem, holding to
floating-point precision.

## Norm conservation is particle conservation

Norm conservation is probability conservation: the particle doesn't
appear or disappear, get created or destroyed, as time passes. Any
numerical method that lets the norm drift is quietly violating this, one
of the reasons the split-operator method, built from exact-phase factors,
is the right tool here. A naive explicit finite-difference scheme does not
preserve the norm on its own, and can diverge outright.

## A Gaussian, normalized and then integrated

**$\psi(x) = A\,e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ for a real constant $\sigma$. Find
$A$, and find $P(-\sigma \le x \le \sigma)$.**

  explanation="The 68% rule applies to the density's standard deviation, and the density here is |ψ(x)|² ∝ e^(−x²/σ²), whose standard deviation is σ/√2, not σ. The σ in the wavefunction's exponent is not the σ of the probabilities it predicts."
/>

$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}\,dx = \sigma\sqrt{\pi}$ (a
standard Gaussian integral), so normalization requires
$|A|^2\sigma\sqrt\pi = 1$, giving $A = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$. The region
probability $P(-\sigma\le x\le\sigma) = \int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}|\psi|^2 dx$
evaluates to $\mathrm{erf}(1) \approx 0.843$.

The width mismatch flagged in the prediction is worth stating once more in
symbols, because it is the most common slip in this calculation. The
wavefunction carries $\sigma$ in its exponent, but the density carries
$\sigma/\sqrt2$: squaring $e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ gives
$e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}$, which is a Gaussian of standard deviation
$\sigma/\sqrt2$. Integrating out to $\pm\sigma$ therefore covers
$\pm\sqrt2$ standard deviations of the density, and $0.843$ is the
familiar $68\%$ figure evaluated at $\sqrt2$ rather than at $1$.

## What squaring is for, and what cannot be normalized

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to square before integrating.** Normalization uses
$|\psi(x)|^2$, not $\psi(x)$. For a complex $\psi$, that's
$\psi(x)^*\psi(x)$, not $\psi(x)^2$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any function can be normalized.** If $\int|\psi|^2 dx$
diverges (e.g. $\psi(x)=1$ for all $x$, a plane wave), no rescaling fixes
it. Such states aren't normalizable at all and require special
handling (briefly touched on in the Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson).
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $\psi(x) = A$ on $[-2,3]$ and $0$ elsewhere, find $A$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $A = 1/\sqrt5 \approx 0.447$.

   The only thing the normalization integral sees is the *length* of the
   support, not where it sits:

   $$
   \int_{-2}^{3}|A|^2\,dx = |A|^2\,\big(3-(-2)\big) = 5|A|^2 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}
   $$

   taking $A$ real and positive.

   The error this question is built to catch is treating the interval as
   having length $3$, reading the upper limit as the width and getting
   $A=1/\sqrt3\approx0.577$. The interval runs from $-2$, not from $0$, and
   its width is $b-a=5$. Translating the same top-hat anywhere along the
   line changes nothing about $A$: $[0,5]$, $[-2,3]$ and $[100,105]$ all
   give $1/\sqrt5$, because probability does not care where the origin was
   put.

   A second check: $|\psi|^2 = 1/5 = 0.2$ everywhere on the support, and
   $0.2\times5 = 1$. The density is a flat $0.2$ per unit length, and it
   is the area under it, not its height, that is a probability.

   </details>

2. Write out the chain
   $\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger U|\psi(0)\rangle=1$
   in this lesson's position-space integral notation, and say precisely
   which equality in it is the one $U^\dagger U=I$ licenses.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In integrals, the chain reads

   $$
   \int|\psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = \langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|\psi(0)\rangle = \int|\psi(x,0)|^2\,dx = 1
   $$

   and the equality $U^\dagger U=I$ licenses is the *third* one, the step
   from $\langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger U|\psi(0)\rangle$ to
   $\langle\psi(0)|\psi(0)\rangle$. Everything else in the chain is
   definition or bookkeeping.

   Worth separating out what each of the other steps is doing, because
   only one of them is a theorem.

   The first equality is the definition of the inner product in position
   space: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int\phi(x)^*\psi(x)\,dx$, so
   $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ is $\int\psi^*\psi\,dx = \int|\psi|^2dx$. No
   physics, just what the bracket means once the basis is a continuum.

   The second is the substitution $|\psi(t)\rangle = U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$
   together with the adjoint rule $\langle U\phi| = \langle\phi|U^\dagger$.
   Again no physics: this is how adjoints are defined.

   The third is the only place a property of $U$ enters, and it is the
   whole argument. The last is the initial condition, imposed by hand at
   $t=0$ and never again.

   The instructive slip is to think unitarity has to be re-checked at each
   instant, or that "the norm is conserved" is an approximate statement
   about slowly varying states. It is neither. The chain has no $t$-
   dependence left in it once the third step is taken, so
   $\int|\psi(x,t)|^2dx = 1$ holds identically, at every real $t$, for
   every initial state, with no smallness assumption anywhere. That is why
   the simulator's norm readout sitting at $1.0000$ indefinitely is not
   evidence of a well-behaved run: it is a consequence the method was built
   to guarantee, which is why the next question's scenario is
   diagnostic.

   </details>

3. If a numerical simulation of a normalized state showed
   $\|\psi(t)\|=0.998$ after some evolution, what would that most likely
   indicate about the *method* used, given the exact-unitarity argument
   above?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   That the numerical evolution operator being applied is not unitary. The
   physics cannot lose $0.2\%$ of a particle, so the loss belongs to the
   integrator, not to the state.

   The argument above leaves no room for a physical explanation. Exact
   Schrödinger evolution preserves the norm identically, so a drift is a
   discrepancy between the discrete map the computer applies and the
   continuous map the equation specifies. Three concrete causes account for
   nearly every real instance.

   The scheme is not norm-preserving by construction. A naive explicit
   finite-difference or forward-Euler step is $I-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\Delta t$
   truncated, which is not unitary for any finite $\Delta t$; its norm
   drifts every step and the drift compounds. The split-operator method
   this platform uses avoids this by being built from three factors that
   are each exactly a pure phase, so its norm is preserved regardless of
   $\Delta t$.

   Probability has run off the edge of the grid. A finite grid is not the
   infinite line, and a spreading or travelling packet that reaches the
   boundary either wraps around or is clipped, depending on the boundary
   condition. The norm computed over the retained region then falls
   honestly, and the fix is a wider grid or a shorter run, not a better
   integrator.

   The non-unitarity was put in deliberately. Absorbing boundary layers and
   complex "optical" potentials are standard tools for simulating a
   particle leaving the region of interest, and they work precisely by
   making $H$ non-Hermitian so that norm is lost on purpose. A drift then
   means the model is behaving as designed.

   What the number does *not* tell you is how accurate the run was. The
   lesson on numerical evolution makes the point in the opposite direction:
   a method can hold the norm at exactly $1.0000$ while getting the energy
   wrong by a factor of thirty. Norm conservation and accuracy are separate
   guarantees, so $0.998$ is evidence of one specific defect, and $1.0000$
   is evidence of the absence of that one specific defect and nothing more.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\int|\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1$ is normalization; $\int_a^b|\psi(x)|^2 dx$ is
  the probability of being found in $[a,b]$.
- Any wavefunction with finite $\int|\psi|^2 dx$ can be normalized by
  rescaling.
- Unitary time evolution preserves the <Term id="inner-product">inner product</Term> exactly, so a
  normalized state stays normalized forever: a theorem, not an
  approximation, and one the simulator's exact norm conservation
  demonstrates directly.
