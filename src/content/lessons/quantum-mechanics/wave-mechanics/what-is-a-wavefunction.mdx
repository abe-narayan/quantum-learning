<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Ask where an electron in a hydrogen atom actually is, and quantum mechanics won't hand you a single point — it hands you a function.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

That function is $\psi(x)$, and this lesson is about where it comes from
and what it means. A finite list of amplitudes is enough to describe a
qubit, or three qubits, or any number of qubits: $\mathbb{C}^2$,
$\mathbb{C}^8$, always some finite dimension with one number per basis
state. Position refuses that treatment — a particle isn't confined to finitely many discrete
spots, it can sit at *any* real number $x$, so no finite list of
amplitudes can label a continuum of possibilities one at a time. The
postulates from the last course (state, observable, measurement,
dynamics) survive this move with their content untouched; only their
*representation* changes, and pinning down exactly how is this lesson's
entire job.

## Intuition

Don't start from continuous $x$ directly. Start from what you already
know. Chop space into a fine grid of positions $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N$
spaced $\Delta x$ apart (this is *exactly* the grid this course's numerical
simulator uses internally). A particle's state on this grid is an ordinary
finite-dimensional state vector, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i|x_i\rangle$,
with $\sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1$ by the Born rule you already know. Then ask what
happens to this description as $\Delta x \to 0$.

## Mathematical Development

### The continuum limit

On the grid, $P(\text{at } x_i) = |c_i|^2$. As the grid refines
($\Delta x \to 0$, $N \to \infty$), the probability of being in any single
*point* must go to zero: a continuous particle isn't "at" one exact point
with nonzero probability, any more than a continuous random variable is.
What survives the limit is the probability of being in a small *interval*
$[x, x+\Delta x]$. For this to have a sensible, $\Delta x$-independent
limit, the coefficients must scale as

$$
c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}
$$

for some function $\psi$. The $\sqrt{\Delta x}$ is exactly what's needed
so that $|c_i|^2 = |\psi(x_i)|^2 \Delta x$ stays finite and proportional to
the interval width as $\Delta x \to 0$. The normalization condition
becomes

$$
\sum_i |c_i|^2 = \sum_i |\psi(x_i)|^2 \Delta x \;\xrightarrow{\Delta x \to 0}\; \int |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1
$$

a Riemann sum turning into an integral, nothing more exotic than that.
The state itself, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |x_i\rangle = \sum_i
\psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}\,|x_i\rangle$, becomes (informally, dividing
through the bookkeeping of the limit)

$$
|\psi\rangle = \int \psi(x)\,|x\rangle\,dx
$$

with $\langle x|x'\rangle = \delta(x-x')$ (a Dirac delta) replacing the
discrete $\langle x_i|x_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$, the continuum's version
of an orthonormal basis. **This platform's numerical simulator is this
same construction, deliberately left at finite $\Delta x$**: `Grid1D`
*is* $\{x_1,\ldots,x_N\}$, and a `Wavefunction1D`'s `amplitudes` array
*is* $\{\psi(x_1),\ldots,\psi(x_N)\}$. The discretization this lesson
derives as a limit is the platform's literal implementation strategy, not
an unrelated approximation.

### Why $\psi(x)$ is a density, not an amplitude

The scaling $c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}$ is the crux: $\psi(x)$ itself
is **not** a probability amplitude in the same sense $c_i$ was. It's an
**amplitude density**, with units of (probability)$^{1/2}$ per unit
length. $|\psi(x)|^2$ is a probability *density*: $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$ is a
probability, but $|\psi(x)|^2$ alone is not (it can be larger than 1, and
routinely is, wherever the wavefunction is tightly localized). This is a
direct, forced consequence of the continuum limit, not a separate
postulate bolted on afterward.

### What changed, and what didn't

Revisit the four postulates from the last course:

- **State**: still a normalized vector in a Hilbert space, just now an
  infinite-dimensional one ($L^2(\mathbb{R})$, square-integrable
  functions), with $\psi(x)$ as its components in the position basis.
- **Observable**: still a Hermitian operator (position and momentum
  operators are built explicitly in later lessons).
- **Measurement**: still Born's rule, reinterpreted as a *density* for a
  continuous outcome, exactly as derived above.
- **Dynamics**: still $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt = H|\psi\rangle$; position
  space just makes $H$ a differential operator instead of a matrix (a
  later lesson).

Nothing here is new physics. It's the same postulates, in a basis with a
continuum of labels instead of finitely many.

## Physical Interpretation

A finite-dimensional qubit's amplitudes told you how much of $|0\rangle$,
how much of $|1\rangle$. $\psi(x)$ tells you the analogous thing for
every possible position simultaneously, a function's worth of amplitude
information, which is exactly why continuous quantum mechanics needs
calculus where finite-dimensional quantum computing needed only linear
algebra.

## Worked Example

**A particle's wavefunction is $\psi(x) = A$ for $x \in [0, L]$ and $0$
elsewhere. Find $A$.**

Normalization requires $\int_0^L |A|^2\,dx = 1$, so $|A|^2 L = 1$, giving
$A = 1/\sqrt{L}$ (taking $A$ real and positive, a free choice: see the
Superposition, Interference, and Phase lesson on why an overall phase
never matters).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating $\psi(x)$ itself as a probability.** It isn't. It can be
negative, complex, or larger than 1 in magnitude. Only $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$
is ever a probability.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $dx$.** $|\psi(x)|^2$ alone has units of probability
*per length*. Comparing it directly to a plain probability, without the
interval width, compares two different kinds of quantities.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Chopping space into a fine grid and taking $\Delta x \to 0$ forces
  $c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}$, the unique scaling that keeps
  normalization finite in the limit.
- $\psi(x)$ is an amplitude *density*; $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$, not $|\psi(x)|^2$
  alone, is a probability.
- The four postulates from the last course are unchanged in content:
  only their representation (functions instead of finite vectors) is new.
- This platform's numerical `Grid1D`/`Wavefunction1D` types are this
  lesson's discrete construction, deliberately left un-taken-to-the-limit.
