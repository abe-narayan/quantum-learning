## Why a list of amplitudes stops working

The function quantum mechanics hands you is $\psi(x)$, the
<Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term>,
and this lesson is about where it comes from
and what it means. A finite list of amplitudes is enough to describe a
qubit, or three qubits, or any number of qubits: $\mathbb{C}^2$,
$\mathbb{C}^8$, always some finite dimension with one number per <Term id="basis">basis</Term>
state. Position refuses that treatment. A particle is not confined to
finitely many discrete spots; it can sit at *any* real number $x$, so no
finite list of amplitudes can label a continuum of possibilities one at a
time. The postulates from the last course (state, <Term id="observable">observable</Term>,
measurement, dynamics) survive this move with their content untouched.
Only their *representation* changes, and pinning down how is this lesson's
whole job.

## Chop space into a grid first

Don't start from continuous $x$ directly. Start from what you already
know. Chop space into a fine grid of positions $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_N$
spaced $\Delta x$ apart (the same grid this course's numerical simulator
uses internally). A particle's state on this grid is an ordinary
finite-dimensional <Term id="quantum-state">state vector</Term>, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i|x_i\rangle$,
with $\sum_i |c_i|^2 = 1$ by the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> you already know. Then ask what
happens to this description as $\Delta x \to 0$.

## Taking the grid spacing to zero

### The continuum limit

On the grid, $P(\text{at } x_i) = |c_i|^2$. As the grid refines
($\Delta x \to 0$, $N \to \infty$), the probability of being in any single
*point* must go to zero: a continuous particle isn't "at" one exact point
with nonzero probability, any more than a continuous random variable is.
What survives the limit is the probability of being in a small *interval*
$[x, x+\Delta x]$. For this to have a sensible, $\Delta x$-independent
limit, the coefficients must scale as

$$
c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}
$$

for some function $\psi$. The $\sqrt{\Delta x}$ is what makes
$|c_i|^2 = |\psi(x_i)|^2 \Delta x$ stay finite and proportional to the
interval width as $\Delta x \to 0$. Any other power fails: a constant
$c_i$ leaves $\sum_i|c_i|^2$ growing without bound as $N$ increases, and
$c_i \propto \Delta x$ sends the whole sum to zero. The normalization condition
becomes

$$
\sum_i |c_i|^2 = \sum_i |\psi(x_i)|^2 \Delta x \;\xrightarrow{\Delta x \to 0}\; \int |\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1
$$

a Riemann sum turning into an integral, nothing more exotic than that.
The state itself, $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i |x_i\rangle = \sum_i
\psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}\,|x_i\rangle$, becomes (informally, dividing
through the bookkeeping of the limit)

$$
|\psi\rangle = \int \psi(x)\,|x\rangle\,dx
$$

with $\langle x|x'\rangle = \delta(x-x')$ replacing the discrete
$\langle x_i|x_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}$, the continuum's version of an
<Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term>.

Both $\delta$s are worth stopping on, because later lessons use them
without ceremony. The **Kronecker delta** $\delta_{ij}$ is just a compact
way of writing "orthonormal": it is $1$ when $i=j$ and $0$ otherwise, so
$\langle x_i|x_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}$ says each grid state has norm one and
is orthogonal to every other. The **Dirac delta** $\delta(x-x')$ is its
continuum counterpart, and it is not an ordinary function: no ordinary
function is zero everywhere except at one point and still integrates to
$1$. Define it instead by the only property anything ever uses it for,
namely what it does inside an integral:

$$
\int f(x')\,\delta(x-x')\,dx' = f(x)
$$

It sifts out the value of $f$ at the single point where its argument
vanishes, collapsing an integral to one number, the same way $\delta_{ij}$
collapses a sum $\sum_j c_j\delta_{ij}$ to the single term $c_i$. That
sifting is the whole of it, and it is the step that turns a double
integral into a single one in every position-space calculation from here
on.

**This platform's numerical simulator is this
same construction, deliberately left at finite $\Delta x$**: `Grid1D`
*is* $\{x_1,\ldots,x_N\}$, and a `Wavefunction1D`'s `amplitudes` array
*is* $\{\psi(x_1),\ldots,\psi(x_N)\}$. The discretization this lesson
derives as a limit is the platform's literal implementation strategy, not
an unrelated approximation.

### Why $\psi(x)$ is a density, not an amplitude

The scaling $c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}$ is the crux: $\psi(x)$ itself
is **not** a probability amplitude in the same sense $c_i$ was. It's an
**amplitude density**, with units of (probability)$^{1/2}$ per unit
length. $|\psi(x)|^2$ is a probability *density*: $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$ is a
probability, but $|\psi(x)|^2$ alone is not (it can be larger than 1, and
routinely is, wherever the wavefunction is tightly localized). This is a
direct, forced consequence of the continuum limit, not a separate
postulate bolted on afterward.

### What changed, and what didn't

Revisit the four postulates from the last course:

- **State**: still a normalized vector in a Hilbert space, just now an
  infinite-dimensional one ($L^2(\mathbb{R})$, square-integrable
  functions), with $\psi(x)$ as its components in the position basis.
- **Observable**: still a Hermitian operator (position and momentum
  operators are built explicitly in later lessons).
- **Measurement**: still Born's rule, reinterpreted as a *density* for a
  continuous outcome, as derived above.
- **Dynamics**: still $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt = H|\psi\rangle$; position
  space turns $H$ into a differential operator instead of a matrix (a
  later lesson).

Nothing here is new physics. It's the same postulates, in a basis with a
continuum of labels instead of finitely many.

## A function's worth of amplitude

A finite-dimensional qubit's amplitudes told you how much of $|0\rangle$,
how much of $|1\rangle$. $\psi(x)$ tells you the analogous thing for
every possible position simultaneously: a function's worth of amplitude
information rather than a list's. That is the reason continuous quantum
mechanics needs calculus where finite-dimensional quantum computing needed
only linear algebra.

## Normalizing a top-hat

**A particle's wavefunction is $\psi(x) = A$ for $x \in [0, L]$ and $0$
elsewhere. Find $A$.**

Normalization requires $\int_0^L |A|^2\,dx = 1$, so $|A|^2 L = 1$, giving
$A = 1/\sqrt{L}$ (taking $A$ real and positive, a free choice: see the
Superposition, Interference, and Phase lesson on why an overall phase
never matters).

## Two ways to misread psi(x)

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating $\psi(x)$ itself as a probability.** It isn't. It can be
negative, complex, or larger than 1 in magnitude. Only $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$
is ever a probability.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $dx$.** $|\psi(x)|^2$ alone has units of probability
*per length*. Comparing it directly to a plain probability, without the
interval width, compares two different kinds of quantities.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why must $c_i$ scale as $\sqrt{\Delta x}$, specifically, rather than as
   $\Delta x$ or as a constant, for the continuum limit to work?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the Born rule constrains $\sum_i|c_i|^2$, which squares the
   scaling, and only a square root survives that squaring as a single
   factor of $\Delta x$: exactly what a Riemann sum needs.

   Track the sum under each candidate.

   With $c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}$, the normalization sum is

   $$
   \sum_i |c_i|^2 = \sum_i |\psi(x_i)|^2\,\Delta x \;\xrightarrow{\Delta x\to0}\; \int|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
   $$

   a textbook Riemann sum with the width $\Delta x$ sitting where a
   Riemann sum requires it. The limit is finite and independent of how
   finely you chopped, which is the whole requirement.

   With $c_i$ a constant $c$ instead, $\sum_i|c_i|^2 = N|c|^2$, and $N$
   grows without bound as the grid refines. Normalization would force
   $|c|^2 = 1/N$, so $c$ is not a fixed number at all and there is no
   function left in the limit; refining the grid changes the answer, and a
   description that depends on your bookkeeping is not a description of
   the particle.

   With $c_i = \psi(x_i)\Delta x$, the sum is
   $\sum_i|\psi(x_i)|^2\Delta x^2 = \Delta x\sum_i|\psi(x_i)|^2\Delta x
   \to 0\cdot\int|\psi|^2dx = 0$. Total probability collapses to zero, and
   normalization becomes impossible.

   Stated as a rule, the exponent is forced by counting: the sum has
   $N \propto 1/\Delta x$ terms, so each term must scale as $\Delta x^1$ to
   keep the total finite, and since each term is a *squared* coefficient,
   the coefficient itself scales as $\Delta x^{1/2}$. Any other power
   diverges or vanishes.

   The wrong turn is to look for a physical reason for the square root, as
   though nature had chosen it. It is bookkeeping, forced by the fact that
   probabilities come from squared amplitudes, and it is precisely what
   makes $\psi$ an amplitude *density* rather than an amplitude. The
   $\sqrt{\Delta x}$ carries the units, and once it is stripped out, what
   is left over has units of (probability)$^{1/2}$ per unit length.

   </details>

2. For the top-hat wavefunction above with $L=4$, what is $A$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $A = \tfrac12$.

   The wavefunction is constant on an interval of length $4$, so

   $$
   \int_0^4|A|^2\,dx = |A|^2\cdot 4 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |A| = \frac{1}{\sqrt4} = \frac12
   $$

   taking $A$ real and positive, which costs nothing: an overall phase on
   the whole wavefunction is unobservable, as the last course established.

   Two checks are worth doing every time, because they catch most errors
   for free.

   Units. $A$ has units of (length)$^{-1/2}$, matching $\psi$'s status as
   an amplitude density. If your answer came out dimensionless, you
   normalized $\psi$ rather than $|\psi|^2$.

   Direction. Widening the region must *lower* $A$, since the same total
   probability is being spread thinner. $A=1/\sqrt L$ does that. A common
   slip produces $A=\sqrt L$, which rises with $L$ and predicts a state
   that becomes more concentrated the wider its support, which is
   backwards.

   Note that $|\psi|^2 = \tfrac14$ here, comfortably under $1$, but that is
   an accident of $L=4$. Shrink the support to $L = 0.01$ and $A = 10$,
   with $|\psi|^2 = 100$: a probability *density* of $100$ per unit length,
   which is not a probability and is not capped at $1$. The probability of
   finding the particle anywhere in that support is still exactly $1$,
   because $100\times0.01 = 1$.

   </details>

3. Exactly one of the four postulates needs its *statement* changed, not
   merely its representation, when moving from qubits to continuous
   position. Which one is it, what changes, and why do the other three
   survive word for word?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Postulate 3, the Born rule, is the one. Its sum becomes an integral and
   its probability becomes a probability *density*.

   As written for qubits, Postulate 3 says: measuring $A$ with spectral
   decomposition $A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ gives outcome
   $\lambda_i$ with probability $P(\lambda_i)=|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$.
   Every clause of that sentence assumes one eigenvector per outcome and a
   countable list of them. Position has neither. The outcomes form a
   continuum, so the sum over $i$ becomes an integral over $x$; and the
   probability of any single exact value is zero, so
   $P(\lambda_i)$ has to be replaced by $|\psi(x)|^2$, a density, with
   $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$ carrying the probability. The deeper reason is that
   $\hat x$ has no normalizable eigenvector at all: $|x\rangle$ satisfies
   $\langle x|x'\rangle=\delta(x-x')$ rather than $\langle x|x\rangle=1$,
   so the postulate's $|e_i\rangle$ does not exist to be substituted in.

   The other three survive because each is stated in terms that never
   mentioned dimension.

   Postulate 1 says the state is a normalized vector in a complex Hilbert
   space. $L^2(\mathbb{R})$, the square-integrable functions, is a complex
   Hilbert space. The sentence is unchanged; only which space it names has
   changed, and "normalized" now reads $\int|\psi|^2dx=1$ rather than
   $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$, which is the same condition in the new basis.

   Postulate 2 says an observable is a Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues
   are the possible outcomes. $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ are Hermitian
   operators, and the sentence stands. In fairness, infinite dimensions do
   introduce genuine technical care here that finite dimensions never
   needed: these operators are unbounded, so they are defined only on a
   dense subspace rather than everywhere, and the property that actually
   matters becomes self-adjointness rather than plain symmetry. That is a
   refinement of what "Hermitian operator" means, not a change to what the
   postulate asserts.

   Postulate 4 says evolution is unitary, equivalently
   $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt=H|\psi\rangle$. That equation is reproduced
   character for character in position space; all that changes is that $H$
   is a differential operator instead of a matrix.

   The wrong answer to guard against is nominating Postulate 1, on the
   grounds that "the state is now a function, not a vector." A function
   *is* a vector, in exactly the sense the Vector Spaces lesson defined:
   you can add two wavefunctions and scale one by a complex number. That
   lesson built the abstraction precisely so this step would need no new
   postulate, and it does not.

   </details>

## Summary

- Chopping space into a fine grid and taking $\Delta x \to 0$ forces
  $c_i = \psi(x_i)\sqrt{\Delta x}$, the unique scaling that keeps
  normalization finite in the limit.
- $\psi(x)$ is an amplitude *density*; $|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$, not $|\psi(x)|^2$
  alone, is a probability.
- The four postulates from the last course are unchanged in content:
  only their representation (functions instead of finite vectors) is new.
- This platform's numerical `Grid1D`/`Wavefunction1D` types are this
  lesson's discrete construction, deliberately left un-taken-to-the-limit.
