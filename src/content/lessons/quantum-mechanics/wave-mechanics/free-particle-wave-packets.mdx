## Motivation

The plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ from two lessons ago is the momentum
operator's eigenfunction, but $\int|e^{ipx/\hbar}|^2dx = \int 1\,dx$
diverges. A plane wave literally cannot be normalized: it isn't a valid
physical state, only a useful mathematical building block. A real free
particle needs a *superposition* of plane waves, which is exactly what a
wave packet is.

## Intuition

Superposition, interference, and phase (the last course's own lesson)
already established that combining amplitudes with the right relative
phases builds new, structured states. A wave packet superposes plane
waves across a narrow band of momenta centered on $p_0$, with phases
arranged so they interfere *constructively* in one localized region and
*destructively* everywhere else, producing a normalizable, localized
"lump" that still carries a well-defined average momentum.

## Mathematical Development

### Building the packet

Take $\phi(k)$ (momentum-space amplitude) to be a Gaussian centered at
$k_0=p_0/\hbar$ with width $\Delta k$:

$$
\phi(k) \propto e^{-(k-k_0)^2/(4\Delta k^2)}
$$

Applying the inverse Fourier transform from two lessons ago gives the
position-space wavefunction. A calculation whose result (the Gaussian
transforms to a Gaussian, worked out explicitly in that lesson) is

$$
\psi(x,0) \propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\,e^{ik_0 x}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{1}{2\Delta k}
$$

an envelope localized near $x=0$ with width $\sigma$, modulated by a
plane wave carrying the average momentum $p_0=\hbar k_0$. This is exactly
`Wavefunction1D.gaussianPacket(grid, {center, width, momentum})`'s
construction, generalized to an arbitrary center.

### Group velocity

The envelope's peak moves at the **group velocity**, not the phase
velocity of the underlying plane wave. For the free-particle dispersion
relation $E(k)=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ (from $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m$, restated in
terms of $k=p/\hbar$),

$$
v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0} = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m} = \frac{p_0}{m}
$$

exactly the classical velocity $p_0/m$. This matches directly:
$\langle x\rangle$'s rate of change under Schrödinger evolution equals
$\langle p\rangle/m$ (Ehrenfest's theorem, a short calculation deferred to
the Wave Packet Dynamics lesson, but numerically verified here already),
so the packet's average position advances exactly like a free classical
particle.

## Physical Interpretation

A wave packet is the quantum object closest to "a particle with a
definite position and momentum." It has *some* spread in both (bounded
below by $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, from the Momentum Space lesson),
but for a macroscopic object that spread is utterly negligible, which is
exactly why classical mechanics ever looked like a good approximation:
Ehrenfest's theorem says a wave packet's average position obeys Newton's
laws.

## Worked Example

**A packet has $p_0=2$ (natural units, $m=1$). After $\Delta t=3$, by how
much should $\langle x\rangle$ have advanced, if the packet started at
$x_0=-15$?**

$$
\langle x(t)\rangle = x_0 + v_g\,t = -15 + (2)(3) = -9
$$

directly checkable against the simulator's live $\langle x\rangle$
readout after playing that preset forward to $t=3$ (this exact check is
also one of `timeEvolution.test.ts`'s engine tests, verified to match this
formula numerically).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Trying to normalize a bare plane wave.** It cannot be done. $\int
1\,dx$ diverges no matter how you rescale. Plane waves are useful
idealizations for defining momentum eigenstates, never themselves
physical states.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing phase velocity and group velocity.** For matter waves these
differ by a factor of 2 ($v_{\rm phase}=\hbar k/(2m)=v_g/2$ for a free
particle). It's the *group* velocity, the envelope's speed, that matches
the classical particle velocity.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A single plane wave isn't normalizable; a Gaussian superposition of
  plane waves across a narrow momentum band builds a genuine, localized,
  normalizable wave packet.
- The packet's envelope moves at the group velocity $v_g=dE/dp=p_0/m$,
  the classical velocity, recovered from purely quantum machinery.
- This is exactly what `gaussianPacket` constructs, and exactly what the
  Wavefunction Explorer's Free Gaussian Packet preset animates.
