<LessonHook eyebrow="A valid state that cannot exist">
  A momentum eigenstate is a perfectly valid piece of mathematics and a
  physically impossible state: it can never be <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term>. A real
  particle needs a superposition of many of them.
</LessonHook>

## The integral that refuses to converge

The plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ from two lessons ago is the momentum
operator's eigenfunction, but $\int|e^{ipx/\hbar}|^2dx = \int 1\,dx$
diverges. No rescaling repairs that, so a plane wave is not a physical
state at all, only a useful mathematical building block. A real free
particle is instead built as a *superposition* of plane waves, and that
superposition is what a wave packet is.

## Add plane waves until they cancel everywhere else

Superposition, interference, and phase (the last course's own lesson)
already established that combining amplitudes with the right relative
phases builds new, structured states. A wave packet superposes plane
waves across a narrow band of momenta centered on $p_0$, with phases
arranged so they interfere *constructively* in one localized region and
*destructively* everywhere else, producing a normalizable, localized
"lump" that still carries a well-defined average momentum.

## Building the packet, then clocking its speed

### Building the packet

Take $\phi(k)$ (momentum-space <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term>) to be a Gaussian centered at
$k_0=p_0/\hbar$ with width $\Delta k$:

$$
\phi(k) \propto e^{-(k-k_0)^2/(4\Delta k^2)}
$$

Applying the inverse Fourier transform from two lessons ago gives the
position-space wavefunction. That lesson already worked the transform out:
a Gaussian transforms to a Gaussian, so the result is

$$
\psi(x,0) \propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\,e^{ik_0 x}, \qquad \sigma = \frac{1}{2\Delta k}
$$

an envelope localized near $x=0$ with width $\sigma$, modulated by a
plane wave carrying the average momentum $p_0=\hbar k_0$. This is the
construction `Wavefunction1D.gaussianPacket(grid, {center, width,
momentum})` implements, generalized to an arbitrary center.

### Group velocity

The envelope's peak moves at the **group velocity**, not the phase
velocity of the underlying plane wave. For the free-particle dispersion
relation $E(k)=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ (from $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m$, restated in
terms of $k=p/\hbar$),

$$
v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0} = \frac{1}{\hbar}\frac{dE}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0} = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m} = \frac{p_0}{m}
$$

which is the classical velocity $p_0/m$. The agreement is not a
coincidence: $\langle x\rangle$'s rate of change under Schrödinger
evolution equals $\langle p\rangle/m$ (Ehrenfest's theorem, a short
calculation deferred to the Wave Packet Dynamics lesson but numerically
verified here already), so the packet's average position advances the way
a free classical particle would.

## The closest quantum mechanics comes to a particle

A wave packet is the quantum object closest to "a particle with a
definite position and momentum." It has *some* spread in both (bounded
below by $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, from the Momentum Space lesson),
but for a macroscopic object the spread is far below anything measurable.
Take a $1$ g grain of sand localized to $\Delta x = 1\ \mu\mathrm{m}$.
The bound gives $\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x) \approx
5.3\times10^{-29}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$, so
$\Delta v \gtrsim 5\times10^{-26}\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Left alone for the entire
age of the universe, that velocity spread would blur the grain's position
by about $20$ nm, a fiftieth of the micron you started with. That is why
classical mechanics ever looked like the whole story.
<Term id="ehrenfest-theorem">Ehrenfest's theorem</Term> says a wave packet's average position obeys
Newton's laws, and for a grain of sand the average is all anyone can
measure.

## How far the centre has moved after three time units

**A packet has $p_0=2$ (natural units, $m=1$). After $\Delta t=3$, by how
much should $\langle x\rangle$ have advanced, if the packet started at
$x_0=-15$?**

$$
\langle x(t)\rangle = x_0 + v_g\,t = -15 + (2)(3) = -9
$$

directly checkable against the simulator's live $\langle x\rangle$
readout after playing that preset forward to $t=3$. The same check runs as
one of `timeEvolution.test.ts`'s engine tests, which asserts the numerical
result against this formula.

## Phase velocity is the other one

<Callout type="warning">
**Trying to normalize a bare plane wave.** It cannot be done. $\int
1\,dx$ diverges no matter how you rescale. Plane waves are useful
idealizations for defining momentum eigenstates, never themselves
physical states.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing phase velocity and group velocity.** For matter waves these
differ by a factor of 2 ($v_{\rm phase}=\hbar k/(2m)=v_g/2$ for a free
particle). It's the *group* velocity, the envelope's speed, that matches
the classical particle velocity.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why is $\Delta k$ (momentum-space width) inversely related to $\sigma$
   (position-space width) for a Gaussian packet? Connect this to the
   <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term> from two lessons ago.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\psi(x)$ and $\phi(k)$ are one state written in two bases
   related by a Fourier transform, and the Fourier transform trades width
   in one domain for narrowness in the other. For the Gaussian the trade is
   exact: $\sigma = 1/(2\Delta k)$, so the product is fixed.

   The construction in this lesson shows it in one step. Starting from
   $\phi(k)\propto e^{-(k-k_0)^2/(4\Delta k^2)}$ and transforming gives
   $\psi(x,0)\propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}e^{ik_0x}$ with
   $\sigma=1/(2\Delta k)$. The transform sends the exponent's
   coefficient to its reciprocal, so the two widths cannot both shrink: a
   narrower band of $k$ is a broader envelope in $x$, and conversely.

   The mechanism, in words rather than integrals, is interference. The
   packet is localized because the plane waves in the superposition
   reinforce near one point and cancel elsewhere. Cancelling over a wide
   region requires many components spread across a wide range of $k$, so
   they can get out of step quickly as you move away from the centre. A
   narrow band of $k$ contains waves that stay nearly in step over a long
   distance, so they cannot cancel until far away, and the envelope is
   correspondingly broad.

   The connection to the uncertainty principle is that these are the same
   fact, not two facts that agree. With $\hbar=1$, $\Delta p = \Delta k$,
   so $\sigma\Delta k = \tfrac12$ reads $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2$:
   the Gaussian saturates the bound. The general theorem
   $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ was proved from the commutator, with no
   mention of Fourier transforms; the Fourier statement is what that
   theorem looks like once the operators are written in position space.
   Two routes, one result, which is the pattern this whole course runs on.

   The wrong turn is to describe this as a measurement limitation, as
   though a cleverer apparatus could beat it. Nothing was measured here. A
   Gaussian in $k$ simply *is* a Gaussian in $x$ of reciprocal width, the
   way a short pulse in time simply is a broad band in frequency. The
   constraint is a property of the state, present before any instrument is
   pointed at it.

   </details>

2. For $p_0=-3$ and $m=1$, what is the group velocity, and which
   direction does the packet move?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $v_g = p_0/m = -3$, so the packet moves in the $-x$ direction, at speed
   $3$.

   The formula derived above carries the sign through untouched:
   $v_g = dE/dp = p_0/m$, and a negative $p_0$ gives a negative velocity. A
   packet built with $k_0<0$ is a superposition of leftward-travelling
   plane waves, and its envelope goes left.

   Checking against the mean-position formula makes it concrete. Starting
   at $x_0$, $\langle x(t)\rangle = x_0 - 3t$, so after $t=3$ the centre
   has moved $9$ units to the left of where it began. The worked example's
   packet, with $p_0=+2$, moved $6$ units to the right over the same
   interval.

   Two distinctions to keep clean.

   Velocity has a sign; speed does not. "Group velocity $-3$" and "speed
   $3$, leftward" are the same statement, and reporting $|v_g|=3$ without
   the direction throws away half the answer to this question.

   The *width* does not care about the sign. $\sigma(t)$ depends on
   $\sigma_0$ and $|t|$, not on $p_0$, so the leftward packet spreads at
   exactly the rate the rightward one does. Momentum sets where the packet
   goes; the momentum *spread* sets how fast it smears, and those are
   independent knobs. The next lesson makes that separation exact.

   Finally, note what a negative $p_0$ does not change: the energy. Since
   $E = p_0^2/2m$ is even in $p_0$, packets with $p_0 = +3$ and $p_0 = -3$
   have identical energy, $4.5$ in these units. Energy fixes the speed and
   leaves the direction undetermined, which is why $\sin(kx)$, an equal
   mix of both signs, has a sharp energy and zero mean momentum.

   </details>

3. Explain in one or two sentences why Ehrenfest's theorem (packet
   averages obey classical mechanics) is *consistent* with, not a
   violation of, the underlying quantum uncertainty.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because Ehrenfest's theorem is a statement about *averages*, and an
   average obeying a classical equation says nothing about the spread
   around it. Uncertainty constrains the spread, and the two claims never
   compete for the same quantity.

   Spelled out: $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p\rangle/m$ constrains one
   number, the mean position, and ties it to another number, the mean
   momentum. The uncertainty relation constrains $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$,
   which are second moments about those means. A distribution can have a
   mean that marches along a perfect straight line while being as broad as
   you like around it, and a free wave packet is exactly that: its centre
   advances at $p_0/m$ forever while its width grows without bound.

   The concrete picture is the grain of sand above. Its $\langle x\rangle$
   follows Newton's laws to any precision anyone can measure, and it also
   carries $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ the whole time; both are true
   because the spread is around $10^{-26}$ m/s in velocity, far below what
   the classical description was ever resolving.

   The seductive wrong reading is that Ehrenfest's theorem shows quantum
   mechanics "reduces to" classical mechanics, so the uncertainty must
   somehow be washing out. It does not, and there are two separate reasons
   to resist it.

   First, the theorem in its exact form is $d\langle p\rangle/dt =
   -\langle V'(x)\rangle$, the average of the force over the packet, and
   Newton's law would require $-V'(\langle x\rangle)$, the force at the
   average position. These agree only when $V'$ is linear in $x$, which
   means $V$ at most quadratic: free, uniform force, harmonic. For any
   other potential the classical trajectory and the quantum mean genuinely
   part company once the packet is wide enough to sample different forces,
   and the next lesson's derivation is where that shows up.

   Second, even where the means do follow classical equations exactly, the
   *predictions* are not classical. A single measurement returns a value
   scattered around the mean by $\Delta x$, not the mean itself. Classical
   mechanics is recovered as a statement about averages, not as a statement
   about outcomes.

   </details>

## Summary

- A single plane wave isn't normalizable; a Gaussian superposition of
  plane waves across a narrow momentum band builds a localized,
  normalizable wave packet.
- The packet's envelope moves at the group velocity $v_g=dE/dp=p_0/m$,
  the classical velocity, recovered from purely quantum machinery.
- That construction is what `gaussianPacket` builds and what the
  Wavefunction Explorer's Free Gaussian Packet preset animates.
