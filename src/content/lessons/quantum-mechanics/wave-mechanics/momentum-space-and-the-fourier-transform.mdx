## A basis change you have already done once

Mathematical Foundations proved that changing basis in a finite-dimensional
<Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term> re-expresses the same vector in different coordinates,
with the new coefficients found by taking inner products against the new
basis vectors. Momentum space is that same move, with the momentum
eigenfunctions $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ from the last lesson as the new
"basis." One thing changes: the basis is now a continuum, so the
change-of-basis sum becomes an integral. That integral is the Fourier
transform.

## How much amplitude at each momentum?

$\psi(x)$ answers "how much <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> at each position?" $\phi(k)$ (using
wavenumber $k=p/\hbar$, this platform's convention with $\hbar=1$) answers
the *same physical state's* "how much amplitude at each momentum?" They
are two different-basis descriptions of one vector $|\psi\rangle$.
Neither is more fundamental, in the same way that $|+\rangle$ is no more
fundamental than $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$.

## The transform pair, and what it preserves

### The transform pair

Expanding $|\psi\rangle$ in the momentum eigenbasis, by direct analogy
with any finite-dimensional basis expansion (coefficient = inner product
with basis vector), gives

$$
\phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad
\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk
$$

(natural units, $\hbar=1$, so momentum equals wavenumber $k$, the
convention this whole course uses, consistent with the energy formulas
already derived in the last course). The $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is not arbitrary.
It is the factor that makes the transform **<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>**, so that moving to
momentum space is a change of description rather than a distortion of the
state. Parseval's theorem, next, is that statement made checkable.

### Parseval's theorem: the transform preserves norm

$$
\int|\psi(x)|^2\,dx = \int|\phi(k)|^2\,dk
$$

This is the continuous version of "changing basis preserves length" (a
unitary matrix satisfies $\|Uv\|=\|v\|$). Total probability is 1 whether
computed from $|\psi(x)|^2$ or from $|\phi(k)|^2$, because they describe
the same state. This platform's `fourier.test.ts` verifies the identity
numerically against the FFT-based implementation, not only algebraically.

### How the platform computes this

A literal integral over infinite continuous $x$ cannot run on a computer.
The platform's `fourier.ts` module implements the **discrete** Fourier
transform on the finite grid `Grid1D` already introduced: a hand-written,
tested radix-2 FFT (`fft`/`ifft`), with the continuous formula above
recovered as the grid spacing $\Delta x\to0$. The engine carries the
normalization convention derived above into the discrete case (a factor of
$\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$ on top of the raw discrete transform), which is
what makes the *numerical* Parseval identity hold to machine precision.
The test suite checks that numerically rather than assuming it.

### Momentum-space uncertainty

Since $\hat p$ acts as multiplication by $\hbar k$ in momentum space
(the Fourier-transform mirror of $\hat x$ acting as multiplication by $x$
in position space, provable, though not proven here, by transforming the
eigenvalue equation from the last lesson), the momentum-space variance
$(\Delta p)^2 = \langle p^2\rangle - \langle p\rangle^2$ is computed from
$|\phi(k)|^2$ by the same recipe that produced $(\Delta x)^2$ from
$|\psi(x)|^2$. That recipe is how `Wavefunction1D.expectationMomentum()`
and `.expectationKineticEnergy()` are implemented: transform to momentum
space, then take the same weighted average as before.

## Squeeze in x and k pays for it

Nothing said so far restricts a wavefunction's shape. $\psi(x)$ and
$\phi(k)$ are two coordinate descriptions of one vector, and it is not
obvious from that alone that squeezing the state in $x$ should do anything
in particular to it in $k$. Commit to an answer before reading on.

That trade-off is the whole content of the <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term>. A
wavefunction narrow in $x$ is *necessarily* broad in $k$ and vice versa,
not as a mysterious extra postulate but as a provable fact about Fourier
transforms. The relation derived abstractly in the last course from the
commutator, and re-derived from calculus in the last lesson, is *this* fact
restated in the language of operators.

## The Gaussian's width in the other basis

**A stationary ($p_0=0$) Gaussian packet has position-space width
$\sigma$. What is its momentum-space width?**

Write the packet as $\psi(x)\propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}$, the form in
which $\sigma$ is the position spread itself, $\Delta x = \sigma$. This is
the platform's `width` convention, and it is the one of the two flagged in
the Expectation Values lesson that puts no stray $\sqrt2$ anywhere in what
follows. Its Fourier transform is
$\phi(k)\propto e^{-\sigma^2 k^2}$ (a standard transform pair, verified
directly in `fourier.test.ts` against the platform's own FFT), which is a
Gaussian in $k$ with variance $(\Delta k)^2 = 1/(4\sigma^2)$, so
$\Delta k = 1/(2\sigma)$. With $\hbar=1$, $\Delta p=\Delta k$, giving
$\Delta x\,\Delta p = \sigma\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sigma}=\dfrac12$: the
Gaussian packet exactly saturates the uncertainty bound $\Delta x\Delta
p\ge\hbar/2$, the minimum-uncertainty state referenced (but not derived)
in the last course.

## Two bases, one set of predictions

<Callout type="note">
**Treating $\phi(k)$ as a separate, unrelated function from $\psi(x)$.**
They're the *same state* in two bases. Every physical prediction (energy,
momentum statistics, and so on) computed from either must agree, and the
platform's engine tests check that cross-consistency directly.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the normalization convention when comparing formulas from
different textbooks.** Some references use $2\pi$ instead of $\sqrt{2\pi}$
factors, or define $k$ vs. $p=\hbar k$ differently. The physics is
identical, but the constants only match once conventions are pinned down,
as this lesson pins down the platform's.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. If $\psi(x)$ is real and even (symmetric about $x=0$), what symmetry
   must $\phi(k)$ have? (Hint: consider what happens to the transform
   integral under $k\to -k$.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\phi(k)$ is also real and even.

   Take the two properties separately, since they come from different
   halves of the integrand.

   **Even.** Put $-k$ into the transform and substitute $u=-x$, which
   flips the direction of integration and hands back a minus sign that the
   swapped limits immediately undo:

   $$
   \phi(-k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \psi(x)\,e^{ikx}\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \psi(-u)\,e^{-iku}\,du = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \psi(u)\,e^{-iku}\,du = \phi(k)
   $$

   the third equality being where $\psi(-u)=\psi(u)$ is used. So evenness
   of $\psi$ transfers directly to $\phi$.

   **Real.** Conjugate the transform, using $\psi^*=\psi$ because $\psi$ is
   real:

   $$
   \phi(k)^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \psi(x)^*\,e^{ikx}\,dx = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int \psi(x)\,e^{ikx}\,dx = \phi(-k)
   $$

   That identity, $\phi(k)^*=\phi(-k)$, holds for *any* real $\psi$. Now
   combine it with evenness, $\phi(-k)=\phi(k)$, and you get
   $\phi(k)^*=\phi(k)$, which says $\phi$ is real.

   A cleaner way to see both at once: split $e^{-ikx}=\cos kx - i\sin kx$.
   Against an even $\psi$ the $\sin$ piece is an odd integrand and
   integrates to zero, killing the imaginary part outright, and what
   survives is $\phi(k)\propto\int\psi(x)\cos(kx)\,dx$, manifestly real and
   even in $k$.

   The Gaussian is the standard illustration: real and even in $x$, and its
   transform is real and even in $k$, which is why the worked example could
   quote $\phi(k)\propto e^{-\sigma^2k^2}$ with no phase attached.

   The trap is assuming realness alone is enough. It is not. A real but
   *shifted* Gaussian, centred at $x_0\ne0$, is real and not even, and its
   transform picks up a factor $e^{-ikx_0}$: complex, with
   $|\phi(k)|$ unchanged. Displacing a packet in position changes only the
   *phase* of its momentum amplitude, never the momentum distribution,
   which is the same statement as a free packet's $\Delta p$ staying
   constant while it moves.

   </details>

2. Using Parseval's theorem, explain why a normalized $\psi(x)$
   guarantees a normalized $\phi(k)$ without recomputing any integral.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because Parseval's theorem is the statement that the two integrals are
   *the same number*, so fixing one fixes the other:

   $$
   \int|\phi(k)|^2\,dk = \int|\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 1
   $$

   There is nothing left to compute. The second equality is the assumption
   that $\psi$ is normalized; the first is the theorem.

   The reason the theorem holds is worth naming rather than treating as a
   coincidence of Fourier analysis. The transform is a *change of basis*,
   from the position eigenbasis to the momentum eigenbasis, and the
   $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ out front is chosen precisely to make that change of
   basis unitary. A unitary map satisfies $\|Uv\|=\|v\|$, so it cannot
   change the length of the vector it acts on; Parseval is that sentence
   written for this particular unitary. The finite-dimensional analogue is
   immediate: writing $|+\rangle$ in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ basis
   instead of its own does not change the fact that its coefficients'
   squared magnitudes sum to $1$.

   Physically the same fact reads: the particle is somewhere with
   certainty, and it also has *some* momentum with certainty, and these are
   not two independent claims to be checked separately. They are one claim
   about one state, asked in two bases.

   The specific misreading to avoid is treating $\phi(k)$ as a second,
   independently-normalizable object, and imagining you might have to
   rescale it after transforming. Rescaling it would break the pair: the
   inverse transform of a rescaled $\phi$ is a rescaled $\psi$, no longer
   normalized. If a numerical transform comes back with the wrong norm,
   the fault is in the discrete normalization convention, not in the state,
   which is why the engine carries an explicit
   $\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$ factor and why its test suite checks Parseval
   numerically instead of assuming it.

   </details>

3. For a momentum-space Gaussian of width $\Delta k = 2$, what is the
   corresponding position-space width, if the state saturates the
   uncertainty bound?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\Delta x = \sigma = 0.25$.

   Saturation means the product sits exactly at the bound, which in these
   natural units ($\hbar=1$, so $\Delta p=\Delta k$) is $\tfrac12$:

   $$
   \Delta x\,\Delta k = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta x = \frac{1}{2\Delta k} = \frac{1}{2(2)} = \frac14
   $$

   Cross-check it against the worked example's relation the other way
   round: it derived $\Delta k = 1/(2\sigma)$, so $\sigma = 1/(2\Delta k) =
   0.25$, and then $\Delta x\,\Delta k = 0.25\times2 = 0.5$. Consistent.

   Two things to keep straight.

   The word "saturates" is doing real work in the question. For a general
   wavefunction the relation is an inequality, $\Delta x\,\Delta k\ge
   \tfrac12$, and knowing $\Delta k$ would then give only $\Delta x\ge0.25$
   with no upper limit. It is the extra information that this state is
   Gaussian, and therefore a minimum-uncertainty state, that turns the
   inequality into an equation and makes a single answer possible. The
   challenge below asks what happens for a top-hat, and the answer is a
   product strictly larger than $\tfrac12$.

   The direction of the relation is the other place to slip. $\Delta k = 2$
   is a *wide* momentum distribution, so the state must be tightly
   localized, and $0.25$ is indeed small. An answer of $\Delta x = 4$,
   obtained by multiplying instead of dividing, would say a broad momentum
   spread accompanies a broad position spread, which is the trade-off
   backwards. Checking the product afterwards catches this every time:
   $4\times2 = 8$, nowhere near $\tfrac12$.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\phi(k)$ is $\psi(x)$ expressed in the momentum eigenbasis, the same
  vector in different coordinates, as in any finite-dimensional basis
  change.
- The Fourier transform pair with $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ normalization is
  unitary: Parseval's theorem guarantees norm is preserved.
- The platform computes this with a real, tested FFT on a finite grid,
  with the continuous integral as its $\Delta x\to0$ limit.
- A Gaussian wave packet exactly saturates $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$,
  the tightest uncertainty allowed.
