## Motivation

Mathematical Foundations proved that changing basis in a finite-dimensional
Hilbert space is just re-expressing the same vector in different
coordinates, with the new coefficients found via inner products against
the new basis vectors. Momentum space is the *exact same idea*, with the
momentum eigenfunctions $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ from the last lesson as
the new "basis" — the only twist is that the basis is now a continuum, so
the change-of-basis sum becomes an integral: the Fourier transform.

## Intuition

$\psi(x)$ answers "how much amplitude at each position?" $\phi(k)$ (using
wavenumber $k=p/\hbar$, this platform's convention with $\hbar=1$) answers
the *same physical state's* "how much amplitude at each momentum?" They
are two different-basis descriptions of one vector $|\psi\rangle$ —
neither is more fundamental, exactly as $|+\rangle$ isn't more fundamental
than $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$.

## Mathematical Development

### The transform pair

Expanding $|\psi\rangle$ in the momentum eigenbasis, by direct analogy
with any finite-dimensional basis expansion (coefficient = inner product
with basis vector), gives

$$
\phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx, \qquad
\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \phi(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk
$$

(natural units, $\hbar=1$, so momentum equals wavenumber $k$ — the
convention this whole course uses, consistent with the energy formulas
already derived in the last course). The $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ isn't arbitrary:
it's exactly what makes the transform **unitary** — a basis change, not a
distortion.

### Parseval's theorem: the transform preserves norm

$$
\int|\psi(x)|^2\,dx = \int|\phi(k)|^2\,dk
$$

This is the continuous version of "changing basis preserves length" (a
unitary matrix satisfies $\|Uv\|=\|v\|$) — total probability is 1 whether
computed from $|\psi(x)|^2$ or from $|\phi(k)|^2$, because they describe
the same state. This platform's `fourier.test.ts` verifies this identity
numerically for the platform's actual FFT-based implementation, not just
algebraically.

### How the platform actually computes this

A literal integral over infinite continuous $x$ can't run on a computer.
The platform's `fourier.ts` module implements the **discrete** Fourier
transform on the finite grid `Grid1D` already introduced — a genuine,
hand-written, tested radix-2 FFT (`fft`/`ifft`), with the continuous
formula above recovered as the grid spacing $\Delta x\to0$. The engine
applies the exact normalization convention derived above (a factor of
$\Delta x/\sqrt{2\pi}$ on top of the raw discrete transform) precisely so
that the *numerical* Parseval identity holds to machine precision — this
is directly, numerically checked in the test suite, not assumed.

### Momentum-space uncertainty

Since $\hat p$ acts as multiplication by $\hbar k$ in momentum space
(the Fourier-transform mirror of $\hat x$ acting as multiplication by $x$
in position space — provable, though not proven here, by transforming the
eigenvalue equation from the last lesson), the momentum-space variance
$(\Delta p)^2 = \langle p^2\rangle - \langle p\rangle^2$ is computed from
$|\phi(k)|^2$ exactly as $(\Delta x)^2$ was computed from $|\psi(x)|^2$ —
which is exactly how `Wavefunction1D.expectationMomentum()` and
`.expectationKineticEnergy()` are implemented: transform to momentum
space, then take the same kind of weighted average as before.

## Physical Interpretation

A wavefunction narrow in $x$ (tightly localized position) is *necessarily*
broad in $k$ (spread across many momenta), and vice versa — not as a
mysterious "principle" but as an ordinary, provable fact about Fourier
transforms (a signal concentrated in time has a broad frequency spectrum).
The uncertainty principle derived abstractly in the last course, and
re-derived from calculus two lessons ago, is *this* fact, restated.

## Worked Example

**A stationary ($p_0=0$) Gaussian packet has position-space width
$\sigma$. What is its momentum-space width?**

The Fourier transform of $\psi(x)\propto e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}$ is
$\phi(k)\propto e^{-\sigma^2 k^2}$ (a standard transform pair, verified
directly in `fourier.test.ts` against the platform's own FFT), which is a
Gaussian in $k$ with variance $(\Delta k)^2 = 1/(4\sigma^2)$, so
$\Delta k = 1/(2\sigma)$. With $\hbar=1$, $\Delta p=\Delta k$, giving
$\Delta x\,\Delta p = \sigma\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sigma}=\dfrac12$ — the
Gaussian packet exactly saturates the uncertainty bound $\Delta x\Delta
p\ge\hbar/2$, the minimum-uncertainty state referenced (but not derived)
in the last course.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating $\phi(k)$ as a separate, unrelated function from $\psi(x)$.**
They're the *same state* in two bases — every physical prediction (energy,
momentum statistics, etc.) computed from either must agree, and the
platform's engine tests check exactly this kind of cross-consistency.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the normalization convention when comparing formulas from
different textbooks.** Some references use $2\pi$ instead of $\sqrt{2\pi}$
factors, or define $k$ vs. $p=\hbar k$ differently — the physics is
identical, but the constants only match once conventions are pinned down,
exactly as this lesson pins down the platform's.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\phi(k)$ is $\psi(x)$ expressed in the momentum eigenbasis — the same
  vector, different coordinates, exactly like any finite-dimensional
  basis change.
- The Fourier transform pair with $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ normalization is
  unitary: Parseval's theorem guarantees norm is preserved.
- The platform computes this with a real, tested FFT on a finite grid,
  with the continuous integral as its $\Delta x\to0$ limit.
- A Gaussian wave packet exactly saturates $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$ —
  the tightest uncertainty allowed.
