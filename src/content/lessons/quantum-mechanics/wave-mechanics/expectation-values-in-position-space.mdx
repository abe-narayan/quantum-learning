## One more observable, taken to the continuum

Take the finite-dimensional average $\langle A\rangle=\sum_i a_i|c_i|^2$
and let the basis label become continuous: the sum over outcomes turns
into an integral over $x$. That is the same continuum-limit move as the
last two lessons, now applied to a specific, extremely useful
observable: position itself.

## Multiply each outcome by its probability, again

If $|\psi(x)|^2$ is the probability density of finding the particle at
$x$, then the *average* position, over many identical measurements, is the
probability-weighted average of $x$: the same "multiply each outcome by
its probability and sum" recipe from the first probability lesson of the
last course, with the sum turned into an integral.

## From the abstract postulate to an integral over x

### Deriving $\langle x\rangle$ from the abstract postulate

Starting from $\langle x\rangle = \langle\psi|\hat x|\psi\rangle$ (with
$\hat x$ the position operator, formally defined in the next lesson as
"multiply by $x$"), inserting the position-basis expansion
$|\psi\rangle=\int\psi(x)|x\rangle dx$ and using
$\langle x|x'\rangle=\delta(x-x')$ collapses the double integral to a
single one:

$$
\langle x\rangle = \int \psi(x)^*\,x\,\psi(x)\,dx = \int x\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
$$

which is the probability-weighted average described above, now derived
rather than assumed. The same derivation with $x^2$ in place of
$x$ gives

$$
\langle x^2\rangle = \int x^2\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
$$

and, more generally, for *any* function $f(x)$ of position,

$$
\langle f(x)\rangle = \int f(x)\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
$$

This is how the engine computes `expectationPotential(V)` (the
case $f = V$) and, via a discretized Riemann sum over the grid,
`expectationPosition()` (the case $f(x)=x$).

### Position variance

As in the finite-dimensional case,

$$
(\Delta x)^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2
$$

measures how spread out the position probability density is.
`Wavefunction1D.variancePosition()` computes this quantity. For the
Gaussian wave packets this course builds constantly, $\Delta x$ *is* the
packet's `width` parameter by construction: a packet built with
`width` $=w$ has `variancePosition()` equal to $w^2$, verified numerically
in the engine's test suite.

Watch that symbol, because two conventions are in play and they differ by
a factor of $\sqrt2$. The engine's `width` is the standard deviation of the
density itself, so its envelope carries $e^{-(x-x_0)^2/4w^2}$. The
analytic Gaussians written out in this course's prose use the other common
convention, $\psi\propto e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$, whose density is
$e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}$ and whose spread is therefore $\Delta x = \sigma/\sqrt2$
rather than $\sigma$. Both are standard, and they name the same packet only
when $w = \sigma/\sqrt2$. Practice question 1 works the second convention
through in full, so compare its answer against $\sigma$, not against `width`.

## What the mean and the spread each tell you

$\langle x\rangle$ is where you'd expect to find the particle on average;
$\Delta x$ is how much any single measurement is likely to deviate from
that average. A tightly localized wavefunction (small $\Delta x$) predicts
measurements clustered near $\langle x\rangle$; a broad one predicts wide
scatter. That's the same interpretation as any probability distribution's
mean and standard deviation, because that is what these are.

Two packets can therefore agree on $\langle x\rangle$ and disagree
completely about what a measurement will return. Both densities below are
centred at $x=0$, so both have $\langle x\rangle = 0$, and no measurement
of the mean alone would separate them:

The narrow packet has one third the spread and, because both integrate to

to the difference; $\Delta x$ is what reports it. This is why a state is
never adequately summarized by its mean, and why the next lesson's
uncertainty relation is a statement about $\Delta x$ rather than about
$\langle x\rangle$.

## Mean and spread of a top-hat

**For the top-hat $\psi(x)=1/\sqrt L$ on $[0,L]$, find $\langle x\rangle$
and $\Delta x$.**

$$
\langle x\rangle = \int_0^L x \cdot \frac1L\,dx = \frac{L}{2}
$$

by symmetry, as expected: the midpoint of a uniform distribution.

$$
\langle x^2\rangle = \int_0^L x^2\cdot\frac1L\,dx = \frac{L^2}{3}
$$

$$
(\Delta x)^2 = \frac{L^2}{3} - \frac{L^2}{4} = \frac{L^2}{12}, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{L}{2\sqrt3}
$$

matching the well-known variance of a continuous uniform distribution,
here derived from the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term>, not imported from classical statistics.

<ParametricCurve
  frames={topHatDensityFrames}

The density is flat, so the marked mean sits in the middle by
symmetry: as much probability weight sits to the left of $\langle
x\rangle$ as to the right, the same "balance point" reading as a center
of mass with uniform density, which is what $\int x\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$
computes.

## Squaring the average against averaging the square

<Callout type="warning">
**Computing $\langle x^2\rangle$ as $\langle x\rangle^2$.** These are
generally different (equal only for a state with zero variance, which no
normalizable wavefunction has). Squaring the average is not the same as
averaging the square.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the weight $|\psi(x)|^2$ isn't $\psi(x)$.** The expectation
integral always carries the *probability density* as its weight, which
for complex $\psi$ means $\psi^*\psi$, not $\psi$ or $\psi^2$.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For the Gaussian $\psi(x) = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$
   centered at the origin, what is $\langle x\rangle$ by symmetry alone,
   with no integral needed?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\langle x\rangle = 0$.

   The integrand is $x\,|\psi(x)|^2$. The density
   $|\psi(x)|^2 = (\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}$ depends on $x$ only
   through $x^2$, so it is an *even* function: it takes the same value at
   $-x$ as at $x$. Multiplying an even function by the odd function $x$
   gives an odd integrand, and the integral of an odd function over a
   symmetric interval is zero, because the contribution from every $x>0$ is
   cancelled exactly by its mirror at $-x$.

   $$
   \langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 0
   $$

   The physical reading is that the density is balanced about the origin,
   so its centre of mass sits there, in the same sense that a symmetric
   object balances at its midpoint.

   Two guards on this argument, since parity shortcuts are easy to
   over-apply.

   The symmetry has to be of $|\psi|^2$, not of $\psi$. That is automatic
   when $\psi$ is even, and it is also automatic when $\psi$ is *odd*: a
   negative sign disappears under the squared magnitude. The first excited
   state of a symmetric well is odd and still has $\langle x\rangle=0$. So
   "odd wavefunction" does not defeat the argument, and it is worth
   checking which function you are claiming symmetry of.

   The shortcut settles $\langle x\rangle$ and nothing else. $\langle
   x^2\rangle$ has an even integrand, so no cancellation occurs and it is
   emphatically nonzero: for this Gaussian it is $\sigma^2/2$, giving
   $\Delta x = \sigma/\sqrt2$. Concluding that a centred state has no
   spread because it has no mean is the error the next question and the
   two densities plotted above are both aimed at.

   </details>

2. Using the general formula $\langle f(x)\rangle=\int f(x)|\psi(x)|^2dx$,
   write down (don't evaluate) the integral for $\langle V(x)\rangle$
   given a potential $V(x)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \langle V(x)\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} V(x)\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
   $$

   Substitute $f=V$ and stop. This is what the engine's
   `expectationPotential(V)` computes, as a Riemann sum of
   $V(x_i)|\psi(x_i)|^2\Delta x$ over the grid.

   The reason this substitution is legitimate is worth making explicit,
   because it is the one thing that does not generalize. The formula
   $\langle f(x)\rangle=\int f|\psi|^2dx$ holds because $\hat x$ is
   *diagonal* in the position basis: it acts by multiplication, so
   $f(\hat x)$ also acts by multiplication, and the general sandwich
   $\int\psi^*\,f(\hat x)\psi\,dx$ collapses to $\int f\,|\psi|^2dx$ with
   the two factors of $\psi$ combining into the density. Any function of
   position alone inherits this, and $V(x)$ is one.

   Now the boundary of the trick. The analogous move for momentum is
   wrong:

   $$
   \langle p\rangle \ne \int p\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx
   $$

   which is not even a sensible expression, since there is no $p$ in the
   integrand to integrate. $\hat p$ is not diagonal in the position basis;
   it differentiates. Its expectation value is
   $\int\psi^*\left(-i\hbar\,d\psi/dx\right)dx$, where the operator sits
   *between* the two factors and cannot be pulled out to meet the density.
   The same applies to $\langle H\rangle$, whose kinetic part carries a
   second derivative.

   So the correct summary is narrower than the formula looks: expectation
   values become plain density-weighted averages exactly for observables
   that are functions of position. Everything else has to keep the operator
   sandwiched, which is why the engine computes $\langle p\rangle$ by
   transforming to momentum space rather than by weighting $|\psi(x)|^2$.

   </details>

3. True or false, with justification: $\Delta x$ can be exactly zero for
   some valid, <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> wavefunction.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   False. No normalized wavefunction has $\Delta x = 0$.

   Suppose one did. Writing $\bar x = \langle x\rangle$, the variance is

   $$
   (\Delta x)^2 = \int (x-\bar x)^2\,|\psi(x)|^2\,dx = 0
   $$

   The integrand is a product of two non-negative factors, so the integral
   vanishes only if the integrand vanishes almost everywhere. At every
   $x\ne\bar x$ the factor $(x-\bar x)^2$ is strictly positive, so
   $|\psi(x)|^2$ must be zero there. That leaves $\psi$ supported on the
   single point $\bar x$, and a function that is zero except at one point
   has $\int|\psi|^2dx = 0$, not $1$. The state was not normalized after
   all, so the supposition fails.

   The physical content is that a position eigenstate does not exist as a
   state of the theory. What plays that role is the Dirac delta
   $\delta(x-\bar x)$, and it is not a function in $L^2(\mathbb{R})$ at
   all: it has no finite norm, and the earlier lesson introduced it only as
   something defined by what it does inside an integral. Squeezing a real
   wavefunction narrower and narrower drives $\Delta x$ toward zero without
   ever reaching it, exactly as the momentum-eigenstate discussion in
   Free-Particle Wave Packets does at the other end.

   Two things this does *not* say. It does not say $\Delta x$ has some
   positive lower bound in general: no such bound exists, and $\Delta x$
   can be made as small as you like. And it is a separate fact from the
   uncertainty relation, which forbids $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ from both
   being small at once. The result here forbids $\Delta x = 0$ on its own,
   for reasons of normalizability, before any commutator is mentioned.

   Notice this is also what rescues the callout above. The warning says
   $\langle x^2\rangle$ and $\langle x\rangle^2$ are equal "only for a
   state with zero variance," and the point of this question is that the
   exception is empty: for every genuine state the two really are
   different, and the gap between them is a strictly positive number.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\langle f(x)\rangle = \int f(x)|\psi(x)|^2\,dx$, derived directly from
  the abstract $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ postulate via the position-basis
  expansion.
- $\langle x\rangle$ and $(\Delta x)^2=\langle x^2\rangle-\langle
  x\rangle^2$ are the mean and variance of the position probability
  density, just as for any probability distribution.
- The engine's `expectationPosition()`, `variancePosition()`, and
  `expectationPotential()` are direct, discretized implementations of
  these integrals.
