## Opening up the method behind every number so far

Every numerical claim this course has made (norm staying at 1.0000,
energy matching analytical formulas, stationary states staying
stationary) rests on one specific numerical method. This lesson opens
that method up. The point is not to have you re-implement it, but to make
every number the simulator has shown you this course into something you
can account for.

## Kinetic and potential want different bases

The formal solution to the time-dependent <Term id="schrodinger-equation">Schrödinger equation</Term> is
$|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle$, but $\hat H
=\hat T+\hat V$ (kinetic plus potential) is a *sum* of two operators that
don't commute (they act naturally in different bases: momentum space for
$\hat T$, position space for $\hat V$), so $e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$ cannot be
split into $e^{-i\hat Tt/\hbar}$ times $e^{-i\hat Vt/\hbar}$. It
*can*, however, be split approximately, for a short enough time step, and
doing that approximation carefully, many times in a row, is the whole
method.

## Splitting an exponential that will not split

### Why the split isn't exact

For two operators $A,B$ that don't commute, compare
$e^{(A+B)\Delta t}$ to $e^{A\Delta t}e^{B\Delta t}$ by Taylor-expanding
both to second order in $\Delta t$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Taylor-expand the true, unsplit exponential to second order in Δt.">
$$
e^{(A+B)\Delta t} = I+(A+B)\Delta t+\frac{A^2+AB+BA+B^2}{2}\Delta t^2+\cdots
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Separately Taylor-expand each factor of the split product, then multiply the two series together and collect terms to second order.">
$$
e^{A\Delta t}e^{B\Delta t} = \left(I+A\Delta t+\frac{A^2}{2}\Delta t^2\right)\left(I+B\Delta t+\frac{B^2}{2}\Delta t^2\right) = I+(A+B)\Delta t+\frac{A^2+2AB+B^2}{2}\Delta t^2+\cdots
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Subtract the two expressions. They agree exactly through order Δt¹ and differ at order Δt² by the commutator, since AB and BA cancel only when they are equal.">
$$
\frac{AB-BA}{2}\Delta t^2 = \frac{[A,B]}{2}\Delta t^2
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

**The leading error is proportional to the <Term id="commutator">commutator</Term>.** If $\hat T$ and
$\hat V$ commuted, the split would be exact at every order. They don't
(that's the whole content of $[\hat x,\hat p]\ne0$), so splitting is only
approximate, with error shrinking as $\Delta t\to0$.

### The symmetric (Strang) split

Naively splitting $e^{-i(\hat T+\hat V)\Delta t/\hbar}\approx e^{-i\hat
V\Delta t/\hbar}e^{-i\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}$ has $O(\Delta t^2)$ error per
step, as derived above. The platform instead uses the **symmetric**
version,

$$
e^{-i\hat H\Delta t/\hbar} \approx e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/(2\hbar)}\;e^{-i\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}\;e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/(2\hbar)}
$$

<PipelineDiagram
  ariaLabel="Symmetric split-operator step: half potential step in position space, FFT to momentum space, kinetic step, inverse FFT back to position space, then a second half potential step in position space"
  steps={[
    { label: "e^(-iVΔt/2ħ)", detail: "half potential step (position space)" },
    { label: "FFT", detail: "position → momentum space" },
    { label: "e^(-iħk²Δt/2m)", detail: "kinetic step (momentum space)" },
    { label: "IFFT", detail: "momentum → position space" },
    { label: "e^(-iVΔt/2ħ)", detail: "half potential step (position space)" },
  ]}
/>

half a potential step, a full kinetic step, half a potential step. This
extra symmetry (not proven here, but a standard result) cancels the
leading error term entirely, leaving $O(\Delta t^3)$ error per step:
substantially more accurate for the same cost, and the reason
`SplitOperatorEvolver.step()` is implemented as potential-half, kinetic,
potential-half, in that order.

### The kinetic step and the FFT

$\hat V$ is diagonal (just multiplication) in position space, so
$e^{-i\hat V\Delta t/(2\hbar)}$ is a simple pointwise multiplication.
$\hat T=\hat p^2/2m$ is diagonal in *momentum* space instead, so the
method Fourier-transforms to momentum space (the Momentum Space lesson's
FFT), applies $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/(2m)}$ pointwise there, and
transforms back. Every one of these three factors is a **pure phase**
(magnitude exactly 1, pointwise or via a <Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term> transform), which is
why the method is unconditionally norm-preserving (proven in the
Probability Density lesson) regardless of how large $\Delta t$ is, even
though large $\Delta t$ still hurts *accuracy* through the commutator
error just derived.

### Norm-preserving is not the same as accurate

This distinction matters enough that this platform's own development
surfaced it directly: a numerical experiment with the infinite well's
tall-but-finite wall used a wall height and time step whose product was
too large. The *state* still evolved with norm locked at exactly
$1.0000$ and a visibly correct, stationary density, but the *energy*
expectation value, computed via the momentum-space kinetic term, drifted
by thousands of percent, because $\langle p^2\rangle$ is disproportionately
sensitive to poorly-resolved high-frequency content near the (very tall,
under-resolved) wall. The fix was straightforward once identified: keep
`wallHeight * dt` small, which is the commutator-error argument above
applied concretely, since a large $V$ makes $[\hat T,\hat V]$'s
contribution large for a fixed $\Delta t$. Every infinite-well and
harmonic-oscillator preset in this course is tuned with that constraint in
mind. It is a real engineering trade-off, and stating it is more useful
than hiding it.

## The knob that separates a simulation from an animation

The split-operator method is a physics simulation rather than a curve fit
or an animation trick: every frame is produced by applying the approximate
short-time propagator derived above, once more. What separates the two is
that this one has a knob. Halve $\Delta t$ and the answer gets closer to
the truth, at the cost of computation. A decorative animation has no such
knob, because it is not approximating anything.

## Why ten times smaller buys a thousand times better

**Why does making $\Delta t$ ten times smaller improve the split-operator
method's per-step error by roughly $1000\times$, not $10\times$?**

The (symmetric) method's per-step error scales as $O(\Delta t^3)$, so
shrinking $\Delta t$ by a factor of 10 shrinks the *per-step* error by
$10^3=1000$. (Note this doesn't mean the *total* simulated trajectory
gets 1000× more accurate for free: reaching the same total simulated time
now requires 10× more steps, each contributing error, so the *total*
accumulated error improves by roughly $1000/10=100\times$, still a
substantial win for a $10\times$ smaller time step.)

## The guarantee norm conservation does not give

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming norm conservation proves accuracy.** It doesn't. This
lesson's own case study above (the infinite-well energy blowup) is a
concrete, real case where norm held at $1.0000$ while energy
was off by a factor of 30. Different quantities can be sensitive to
different kinds of numerical error.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking a smaller $\Delta t$ is "free."** It's more accurate per
simulated time unit, but costs proportionally more computation to reach
the same total time. The platform's presets balance this deliberately
for smooth, responsive animation.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. If $[\hat T,\hat V]=0$ for some special potential, what would that
   imply about the split-operator method's accuracy for that potential
   specifically?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The split would be **exact**, at every order and for any time step. The
   method would stop being an approximation at all.

   The derivation above located the entire leading error in
   $\tfrac{[A,B]}{2}\Delta t^2$. Setting $[\hat T,\hat V]=0$ kills it, and
   the same is true at every higher order: for commuting operators
   $e^{A+B}=e^Ae^B$ is an identity, exactly as it is for ordinary numbers.
   With no error term, $\Delta t$ could be as large as the whole simulation
   and the answer would still be right.

   The obvious question is when this actually happens. $\hat T$ commutes
   with $\hat V$ precisely when $V$ is *constant*, since $[\hat T,V(x)]$ is
   built from $[\hat p^2,V]$, which vanishes only if $V' \equiv 0$. So the
   free particle qualifies, and so does a free particle with an arbitrary
   constant offset added to its energy, which changes nothing observable.
   That is the entire list on the line, and it is why this is a thought
   experiment rather than an optimization: any potential worth simulating
   has a nonzero gradient somewhere.

   It is still a useful thought experiment, for two reasons.

   It identifies what the numerical error is *made of*. The split-operator
   method is inexact for the same reason position and momentum have an
   uncertainty relation: $\hat T$ and $\hat V$ are built from operators
   that do not commute. The commutator is not an artifact of the algorithm;
   it is the physics the algorithm has to work around, which is why no
   amount of clever reordering removes it and why the symmetric split can
   only cancel the leading term rather than all of them.

   It predicts where the method performs best. The error scales with how
   much $\hat T$ and $\hat V$ fail to commute, which grows with the size of
   $V'$ and $V''$. A smooth, shallow potential is nearly free over a short
   step and the method is nearly exact; a very tall, very steep wall is the
   opposite, which is exactly the regime the lesson's `wallHeight * dt`
   constraint and the third question below are about.

   </details>

2. Explain in one sentence why the *kinetic* step is done in momentum
   space rather than position space.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\hat T=\hat p^2/2m$ is diagonal in momentum space, where it is
   just multiplication by $\hbar^2k^2/2m$, so $e^{-i\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}$
   becomes a pointwise multiplication by the number
   $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$ at each $k$; in position space it is a
   second derivative, and exponentiating a differential operator is not a
   pointwise operation at all.

   Unpacking that, since the one sentence hides the useful part.
   Exponentiating an operator is easy exactly when the operator is
   diagonal: you exponentiate each diagonal entry separately, which for a
   continuum of labels means multiplying by a number at each point. In
   position space $e^{-i\hat T\Delta t/\hbar}$ has no such form. Expanding
   its power series would require applying $d^2/dx^2$ arbitrarily many
   times, each application coupling every grid point to its neighbours, and
   truncating that series is what makes naive finite-difference schemes
   both inaccurate and non-unitary.

   This is why the method alternates bases at all. $\hat V$ is diagonal in
   position space and $\hat T$ is diagonal in momentum space, and neither
   is diagonal in the other's basis. Rather than fight that, the algorithm
   goes where each factor is easy: multiply pointwise in position space,
   FFT, multiply pointwise in momentum space, inverse FFT, multiply
   pointwise again. The FFT is affordable enough ($O(N\log N)$) that
   changing basis twice per step is cheaper than any scheme that stays put.

   And it is the same property that buys exact norm conservation. Each of
   the three factors multiplies by a number of magnitude exactly $1$, or
   applies a unitary transform, so no step can change $\||\psi\rangle\|$
   regardless of $\Delta t$. That guarantee comes from the *form* of the
   factors, not from the size of the time step, which is precisely why it
   is independent of accuracy.

   </details>

3. Using the commutator-error argument, explain qualitatively why a
   *taller* barrier (larger $V$) needs a *smaller* time step for the same
   accuracy, all else equal.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the per-step error is set by the commutator $[\hat T,\hat V]$,
   and that commutator grows with $V$. Making $V$ larger enlarges the error
   at fixed $\Delta t$, so $\Delta t$ has to shrink to bring it back.

   The scaling is visible in the structure of the error term. The leading
   error is proportional to $[\hat T,\hat V]\Delta t^2$ for the naive
   split, and $\hat V$ appears *linearly* inside the commutator, so
   doubling the barrier height roughly doubles the error at a fixed time
   step. Since the error also carries $\Delta t^2$, restoring the previous
   accuracy needs $\Delta t$ smaller by about $\sqrt2$. Note what this
   argument holds fixed: $V\Delta t^2$, not $V\Delta t$.

   A second, stricter condition is what the `wallHeight * dt` rule of thumb
   actually encodes. The split is
   accurate when the potential does not do much *during* one time step,
   because each factor is applied as though the other were frozen. What
   counts as "not much" is the phase $V\Delta t/\hbar$ that the potential
   factor imprints. If that phase is a large fraction of a full turn, the
   step is trying to represent a rapid rotation in one jump, and the
   alternation between the two bases stops tracking the true evolution. A
   taller wall winds that phase faster, so the step has to be shorter to
   keep it small. Keeping $V\Delta t$ itself bounded is a harder demand than
   keeping $V\Delta t^2$ bounded once $\Delta t$ is already small, which is
   why the practical rule is stated as a product and not as the commutator
   estimate alone.

   The reason this bites hardest at a very tall wall is that the wall is
   also where $\psi$ has its sharpest features. A steep potential produces
   rapidly varying, high-$k$ content, and $\langle p^2\rangle$ weights high
   $k$ quadratically, so the energy readout is far more sensitive to
   under-resolution there than the density is. That is the shape of the
   real bug the lesson describes: the norm stayed at $1.0000$ and the
   density looked stationary, both of which are insensitive to this error,
   while $\langle H\rangle$ was off by a factor of thirty.

   The practical conclusion is that the infinite well's idealization is
   numerically expensive. A literally infinite wall would demand
   $\Delta t\to0$, so the platform uses a tall-but-finite wall chosen so
   that the leakage is invisible on the plot *and* the product with
   $\Delta t$ stays small. Both constraints have to be met at once, and
   satisfying only the first is what produced the bug.

   </details>

## Summary

- $e^{-i(\hat T+\hat V)\Delta t/\hbar}$ isn't exactly splittable because
  $[\hat T,\hat V]\ne 0$; the leading error in a naive split is
  proportional to that commutator.
- The symmetric (Strang) split used by this platform cancels the leading
  error term, giving $O(\Delta t^3)$ per-step accuracy from three pure-phase
  factors (potential half-step, kinetic step via FFT, potential half-step).
- Every factor being a pure phase is why norm is preserved *exactly*,
  independent of $\Delta t$: a separate guarantee from *accuracy*, which
  does depend on $\Delta t$.
- A real numerical issue (wall height times time step too large,
  corrupting the energy calculation while leaving norm and shape intact)
  was found and fixed during this platform's own development, and is
  documented honestly rather than hidden.
