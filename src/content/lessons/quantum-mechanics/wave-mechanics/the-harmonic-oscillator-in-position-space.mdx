## The same spectrum by a completely different route

That promise was stated precisely: "the connection between this $H$ and
the physical potential $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ is made precise once
continuous position is developed." Keeping it means a second,
independent derivation of the *same* energy spectrum, this time by
direct substitution into the position-space Schrödinger equation. The
two routes agreeing is evidence that the physics is being described
consistently, and it also tells you something the algebra alone could
not: which potential the abstract $H$ was standing in for all along.

## Every stable minimum is a parabola up close

The classical harmonic oscillator's potential energy is
$V(x)=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$, a parabola, the potential every stable
equilibrium looks like to leading order near its minimum (any smooth
potential's Taylor expansion around a minimum starts with a quadratic
term). The ground state should be some localized "blob" centered at the
minimum; a Gaussian is the natural guess, and this lesson checks it works.

## Guess a Gaussian, then make it verify itself

### The candidate ground state

Guess $\psi_0(x) = \left(\dfrac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}
e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$, which is the formula
`harmonicOscillatorEigenstate(grid, 0, omega, mass)` evaluates on the
grid.

### Direct verification

Compute the derivatives needed for the Schrödinger equation:

$$
\psi_0'(x) = -\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\,\psi_0(x)
$$

$$
\psi_0''(x) = -\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\psi_0(x) + \left(\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\right)^{\!2} x^2\,\psi_0(x) = \frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\left[\left(\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\right)x^2 - 1\right]\psi_0(x)
$$

Substitute into $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_0'' + \dfrac12 m\omega^2x^2\psi_0$:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\left[\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^2-1\right]\psi_0 + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_0 = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left[\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x^2-1\right]\psi_0 + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_0
$$

$$
= -\frac{m\omega^2}{2}x^2\psi_0 + \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\psi_0 + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_0 = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,\psi_0
$$

The $x^2\psi_0$ terms cancel *exactly*, leaving

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_0'' + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_0 = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,\psi_0
$$

the time-independent Schrödinger equation with <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term>
$E_0=\hbar\omega/2$. This matches the ladder-operator result's ground
state energy, now confirmed by direct calculus, with no reference to
$a,a^\dagger$ anywhere in this derivation.

### One rung up: verifying $n=1$

The same check works one level higher. The first excited state is the
ground state's Gaussian multiplied by $x$ (odd, with a single node at
the origin): $\psi_1(x) = C\,x\,e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$. Writing
$a\equiv m\omega/\hbar$ to keep the algebra readable:

$$
\psi_1' = C\left(1 - ax^2\right)e^{-ax^2/2}, \qquad \psi_1'' = C\left(a^2x^3 - 3ax\right)e^{-ax^2/2} = \left(a^2x^2-3a\right)\psi_1
$$

Substituting into the Schrödinger equation, the $x^2$ terms cancel by
the same mechanism as before ($\tfrac{\hbar^2a^2}{2m} = \tfrac12 m\omega^2$),
and only the constant term survives:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_1'' + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi_1 = \frac{3a\hbar^2}{2m}\,\psi_1 = \frac{3\hbar\omega}{2}\,\psi_1
$$

$E_1 = 3\hbar\omega/2$ drops out, again matching the ladder: one
quantum $\hbar\omega$ above the ground state.

### The excited states

The higher eigenstates $\psi_n(x) \propto H_n(\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x)\,
e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$ (Hermite polynomials $H_n$ multiplying the same
Gaussian envelope, `harmonicOscillatorEigenstate` implements $n=0..3$
via this closed form) can be checked the same way, each giving
$E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$, the identical, equally-spaced ladder
derived purely algebraically last course. Two unrelated derivations
(raising and lowering operators on an abstract <Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term>; direct
substitution into a differential equation) landing on the same spectrum is
not a coincidence. It is one physical system described in two
mathematical languages.

Set next to the infinite well's ladder from the last lesson, the contrast
is immediate:

## Three levels at omega = 2 and the gap between them

**For $\omega=2$ (natural units, $\hbar=m=1$), find $E_0,E_1,E_2$ and
confirm the spacing is exactly $\omega$.**

$$
E_n = \omega\left(n+\tfrac12\right) \;\Longrightarrow\; E_0=1,\; E_1=3,\; E_2=5
$$

$$
E_1-E_0 = 2 = \omega, \qquad E_2-E_1 = 2 = \omega \;\checkmark
$$

matching `harmonicOscillatorEnergyLevel(n, omega)` and the last
course's algebraic result.

## Two derivations, one oscillator

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking this derivation and the ladder-operator one describe
different physical systems.** They're the same oscillator; the different
mathematical routes are a feature, not a coincidence to be suspicious of.
Cross-checking a result by an independent method is how physics
confirms understanding.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the derivative product rule** when computing $\psi_0''$.
$\psi_0'=-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\psi_0$ is itself a product of $x$ and
$\psi_0(x)$, so differentiating again needs the product rule, not just
differentiating the exponent.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Confirm $\psi_0(x)$ as given is properly <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> by checking the
   prefactor against the general Gaussian normalization worked out in the
   Probability Density lesson (with $\sigma^2=\hbar/(m\omega)$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The two expressions are the same function, once $\sigma^2$ is
   substituted.

   The Probability Density lesson normalized
   $\psi(x) = A\,e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and found $A=(\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$.
   Put $\sigma^2 = \hbar/(m\omega)$ into both halves of that.

   The exponent:

   $$
   -\frac{x^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{x^2}{2\hbar/(m\omega)} = -\frac{m\omega x^2}{2\hbar}
   $$

   which is exactly the exponent in the lesson's $\psi_0$.

   The prefactor:

   $$
   A = \left(\pi\sigma^2\right)^{-1/4} = \left(\frac{\pi\hbar}{m\omega}\right)^{-1/4} = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}
   $$

   which is exactly the lesson's prefactor. So $\psi_0$ is normalized, and
   no new integral was needed: the Gaussian integral was done once, three
   lessons ago, and this is that result with a physical width substituted
   in.

   Worth reading off what $\sigma^2 = \hbar/(m\omega)$ says on its own.
   The ground state's width shrinks as $\omega$ or $m$ grows, and it is the
   only length that can be built out of $\hbar$, $m$ and $\omega$ at all.
   That is why the oscillator has no free length scale to tune: unlike the
   infinite well, whose width $L$ is imposed from outside, this system
   manufactures its own.

   Note also the width of the *density* is not $\sigma$ but $\sigma/\sqrt2$,
   for the same reason the earlier lesson flagged: squaring
   $e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ gives $e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}$, a Gaussian of standard
   deviation $\sigma/\sqrt2$. So $\Delta x = \sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}$ for
   the ground state, and the corresponding $\Delta p =
   \sqrt{\hbar m\omega/2}$, whose product is exactly $\hbar/2$. The
   oscillator's ground state is a minimum-uncertainty state, which is the
   same fact the momentum-space lesson proved for a Gaussian packet, now
   arriving as a property of a physical system rather than of a chosen
   shape.

   </details>

2. Without redoing the full calculation, argue from the shape of the
   equation why doubling $\omega$ should roughly double the energy
   spacing.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\hbar\omega$ is the only energy that can be built from the
   three constants the problem contains, so every eigenvalue has to be a
   pure number times $\hbar\omega$, and every *gap* scales linearly with
   $\omega$ as a result.

   The equation is

   $$
   -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \frac12 m\omega^2 x^2\psi = E\psi
   $$

   and the only constants in it are $\hbar$, $m$ and $\omega$. Their
   dimensions are energy times time, mass, and inverse time, so the sole
   combination with units of energy is $\hbar\omega$; $m$ cannot appear in
   an energy on its own without dragging in a length, and the equation
   supplies no external length. Hence $E_n = c_n\hbar\omega$ for
   dimensionless numbers $c_n$ that depend on nothing but $n$. Doubling
   $\omega$ doubles every $E_n$ and therefore every difference
   $E_{n+1}-E_n$, whatever the $c_n$ turn out to be.

   That is stronger than "roughly": the scaling is exact, and it holds
   without ever solving for a single $c_n$. Filling them in afterwards
   ($c_n = n+\tfrac12$) confirms it but adds nothing to the argument.

   The mechanical reading is available too, and worth having beside the
   dimensional one. Doubling $\omega$ steepens the potential by a factor of
   four, which narrows the ground state's width $\sigma=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$
   by $\sqrt2$. A narrower state has more curvature, and the kinetic term
   charges for curvature, so the levels move up. The two effects, a
   stiffer potential and a tighter state, combine to give a linear rather
   than quadratic dependence on $\omega$.

   The contrast that makes the argument bite is the infinite well, where
   the same style of reasoning gives a different answer. There the
   available constants are $\hbar$, $m$ and the imposed width $L$, whose
   only energy combination is $\hbar^2/(mL^2)$, so
   $E_n \propto n^2\hbar^2/(mL^2)$ and the spacing grows with $n$. Same
   method, different ingredient list, different spectrum. Dimensional
   analysis does not guess which spectrum you have; it tells you how the
   one you have must scale.

   </details>

3. In natural units, if $\omega=0.5$, what is $E_3$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $E_3 = 1.75$.

   With $\hbar=1$, $E_n = \omega\left(n+\tfrac12\right)$, so

   $$
   E_3 = 0.5\left(3+\tfrac12\right) = 0.5\times3.5 = 1.75
   $$

   matching `harmonicOscillatorEnergyLevel(3, 0.5)`.

   Two errors are worth separating, because they differ by different
   amounts and only one of them is visible in the spacing.

   Dropping the $\tfrac12$ gives $E_3 = 1.5$. The zero-point energy is
   $\omega/2 = 0.25$ here, and it is added to every level equally, so the
   gaps come out right ($0.5$ either way) and only the absolute values are
   wrong. Checking the ladder's spacing will not catch this; checking
   $E_0$ will, since the correct ground state is $0.25$ and the incorrect
   one is $0$.

   Counting from $n=1$ gives $E_3 = 0.5\times2.5 = 1.25$, borrowing the
   infinite well's convention. The oscillator's ladder starts at $n=0$,
   because that is where $a|n\rangle$ returns the zero vector, and the well's
   starts at $n=1$, because $n=0$ there gives $\psi\equiv0$. The two
   conventions come from genuinely different terminating conditions and do
   not transfer.

   For the whole ladder at this $\omega$: $E_0=0.25$, $E_1=0.75$,
   $E_2=1.25$, $E_3=1.75$, spaced by $\omega=0.5$ throughout. Compare the
   infinite well at $L=5$, whose first four levels are $0.197$, $0.790$,
   $1.777$, $3.158$: gaps of $0.59$, $0.99$, $1.38$, widening steadily.
   Same course, same method, two potentials, two entirely different
   spectra.

   </details>

## Summary

- Direct substitution of the Gaussian ansatz $\psi_0(x)\propto
  e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$ into the position-space Schrödinger equation
  gives $E_0=\hbar\omega/2$, with the $x^2$ terms cancelling; the same
  substitution for $\psi_1\propto x\,e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$ gives
  $E_1=3\hbar\omega/2$.
- Excited states multiply the same Gaussian envelope by Hermite
  polynomials, reproducing $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$.
- This confirms, via an entirely independent method, the energy spectrum
  the last course derived algebraically from $[a,a^\dagger]=1$.
