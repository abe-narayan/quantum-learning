## The claim the packet lesson left unproved

The Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson asserted the packet's *average*
position obeys $d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle p\rangle/m$ without proof.
This lesson proves it (Ehrenfest's theorem), and goes further: explaining
*why* the packet doesn't just move. It spreads, growing wider over time,
a quantum effect with no classical counterpart for a free particle.

## Fast components pull ahead, slow ones fall behind

A wave packet is a superposition of plane waves with a spread of momenta
$\Delta p$ (<Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term>, unavoidable for any localized state).
Different momentum components move at different velocities ($v=p/m$): the
faster components pull ahead, the slower ones lag behind, and the
packet inevitably stretches out, like a group of runners with
slightly different speeds spreading apart over a long race, even though
each individual runner just moves in a straight line.

## Ehrenfest's theorem, then the width formula

### Ehrenfest's theorem

For any <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $A$ with no explicit time dependence, the rate of
change of $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ follows from
nothing but the product rule and the Schrödinger equation:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A carries no time dependence of its own, so the product rule puts all the time dependence in the two state factors.">
$$
\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt} = \left\langle\frac{d\psi}{dt}\Big|A\Big|\psi\right\rangle + \left\langle\psi\Big|A\Big|\frac{d\psi}{dt}\right\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute the Schrödinger equation d|ψ⟩/dt = (−i/ħ)H|ψ⟩ into the ket, and its adjoint ⟨dψ/dt| = (+i/ħ)⟨ψ|H into the bra. The sign flip uses H being Hermitian.">
$$
\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\psi|\hat HA|\psi\rangle - \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\psi|A\hat H|\psi\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The two terms combine into a single commutator. This is Ehrenfest's theorem in its general form: dynamics of averages is governed by commutators with H.">
$$
\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat H,A]\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Apply this to $A=\hat x$ with $\hat H=\hat p^2/2m+V(x)$. Since $V(x)$
commutes with $\hat x$ (both just multiply by functions of $x$), only the
kinetic term contributes.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="V(x) commutes with x̂, so only the kinetic term p̂²/2m survives in the commutator with x̂.">
$$
[\hat H,\hat x] = \frac{1}{2m}[\hat p^2,\hat x]
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Expand [p̂²,x̂] using the product rule for commutators, then substitute [p̂,x̂]=−iħ, the antisymmetric partner of the commutator derived in The Position and Momentum Operators.">
$$
[\hat p^2,\hat x] = \hat p[\hat p,\hat x]+[\hat p,\hat x]\hat p = \hat p(-i\hbar)+(-i\hbar)\hat p = -2i\hbar\hat p
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute back into [H,x] and simplify.">
$$
[\hat H,\hat x] = \frac{-2i\hbar\hat p}{2m} = \frac{-i\hbar\hat p}{m}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Plug into the general Ehrenfest identity from above. The i and −i cancel, leaving the classical-looking result.">
$$
\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\frac{-i\hbar}{m}\right)\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\langle p\rangle}{m}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

the group-velocity result from the Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson, now
derived from the general time-evolution machinery rather than assumed.

### Why a free packet spreads

Each momentum component $\phi(k)$ evolves with its *own* phase,
$e^{-i\hbar k^2t/(2m)}$ (from the free-particle energy $E(k)=\hbar^2
k^2/(2m)$), a $k$-*dependent* rate, unlike a simple overall phase. Since
different $k$-components drift apart at different rates $v(k)=\hbar
k/m$, the packet's spatial envelope stretches as time passes.
Working through the resulting integral (standard, not reproduced in full
here) gives the position variance's exact growth:

$$
\sigma(t)^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \left(\frac{\hbar t}{2m\sigma_0}\right)^{\!2}
$$

starting at the initial variance $\sigma_0^2$ and growing *quadratically*
in $t$, so the width $\sigma(t)$ itself settles into plain linear growth
once $t$ is large. This is what `timeEvolution.test.ts` checks
numerically: a zero-momentum Gaussian packet evolved forward under the
engine's split-operator method has its `variancePosition()` match this
formula directly. The simulator is reproducing dispersive physics, not
merely sliding a fixed shape across the screen.

## Spreading, decoherence, and the difference

Dispersion is not decoherence, noise, or numerical error. It's exact,
unitary quantum mechanics. A free particle's wavefunction spreading is as
real and as inevitable as its average position advancing; both follow
directly from the same Schrödinger equation. (A *bound* state, like the
infinite well or <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term> eigenstates from the last two
lessons, does *not* spread. Its probability density is
stationary, which is what makes those states special.)

Ultrafast electron microscopy runs into this spreading as a real
engineering problem: a pulse of free electrons, itself a matter-wave
packet obeying this same free-particle Schrödinger equation, broadens
in time as it travels down the microscope column, the same
$\sigma(t)$ growth derived above. Instrument designers correct for it
with RF compression cavities that re-bunch the pulse just before it
hits the sample, a direct, practical response to the same dispersive
spreading this lesson's formula predicts for any free wave packet,
electron or otherwise.

## The width after five time units

**A packet starts with $\sigma_0=1$ (natural units, $\hbar=m=1$). What is
$\sigma(t)$ at $t=5$?**

$$
\sigma(5)^2 = 1^2 + \left(\frac{5}{2\cdot1\cdot1}\right)^{\!2} = 1 + 6.25 = 7.25
$$

$$
\sigma(5) = \sqrt{7.25} \approx 2.69
$$

more than doubling the packet's width in 5 time units, and matching the
platform's own dispersion regression test in `timeEvolution.test.ts`.

## What spreading does to the norm

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking dispersion means the wavefunction is losing probability.**
Norm stays exactly 1 throughout. Dispersion redistributes probability
over a wider region; it never destroys it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting bound states to disperse too.** Energy eigenstates
(infinite well, harmonic oscillator) are exactly stationary. Dispersion
is specifically a free-particle (or more generally, non-eigenstate)
phenomenon, driven by different momentum components moving at different
rates.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using Ehrenfest's theorem's general form, what would
   $d\langle p\rangle/dt$ equal for a particle in a potential $V(x)$?
   (This is Ehrenfest's second theorem, a direct analogue of Newton's
   second law.) Compute the commutator explicitly, then say what has to
   be true of $V$ for the analogy with Newton's law to be *exact* rather
   than approximate.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = -\left\langle V'(x)\right\rangle
   $$

   the average of the force. The analogy with Newton's law is exact only
   when $V$ is a polynomial of degree at most two.

   **The commutator.** Start from the general identity
   $d\langle A\rangle/dt = (i/\hbar)\langle[\hat H,A]\rangle$ with
   $A=\hat p$. The kinetic term commutes with $\hat p$ (it is built from
   $\hat p$), so only $V$ contributes: $[\hat H,\hat p] = [V,\hat p]$.
   Evaluate that on an arbitrary $\psi$, using the product rule on the
   second term:

   $$
   [V,\hat p]\psi = V\left(-i\hbar\,\psi'\right) - \left(-i\hbar\right)\frac{d}{dx}\left(V\psi\right) = -i\hbar V\psi' + i\hbar\left(V'\psi + V\psi'\right) = i\hbar\,V'(x)\,\psi
   $$

   The $V\psi'$ terms cancel and what survives is the term the product rule
   generated, exactly as in the $[\hat x,\hat p]$ calculation. So
   $[V,\hat p] = i\hbar V'(x)$, and

   $$
   \frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle i\hbar V'\right\rangle = -\left\langle V'(x)\right\rangle
   $$

   Combined with $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p\rangle/m$ from the
   lesson, this gives $m\,d^2\langle x\rangle/dt^2 = -\langle V'\rangle$,
   which is why it is called Newton's second law for averages.

   **Where the analogy is only an analogy.** Newton's law says the force is
   evaluated *at* the particle's position: $-V'(\langle x\rangle)$.
   Ehrenfest gives the *average of the force over the packet*:
   $-\langle V'(x)\rangle$. Those are different numbers whenever the packet
   is wide enough to sample places where the force differs. Expanding
   $V'$ about $\langle x\rangle$ makes the gap explicit:

   $$
   \left\langle V'(x)\right\rangle = V'(\langle x\rangle) + \tfrac12 V'''(\langle x\rangle)\,(\Delta x)^2 + \cdots
   $$

   the first-order term vanishing because $\langle x - \langle
   x\rangle\rangle = 0$ by construction. So the leading discrepancy is
   $\tfrac12 V'''(\Delta x)^2$, and it vanishes for every state, of every
   width, exactly when $V'''\equiv0$: that is, when $V$ is at most
   quadratic. Free particle, uniform force, harmonic oscillator. For those
   three, a quantum packet's centre traces the classical trajectory
   exactly, forever, no matter how spread out it becomes.

   For anything else, and the Coulomb potential of the hydrogen atom is the
   case that matters most, the two part company as soon as $\Delta x$ grows
   comparable to the scale over which the force varies. That is the precise
   sense in which classical mechanics is recovered: not because quantum
   averages obey Newton's laws, which they generally do not, but because
   for a macroscopic object $\Delta x$ is small enough that the correction
   term is unmeasurable.

   </details>

2. For $\sigma_0=2$, at what time $t$ does $\sigma(t)$ first exceed
   $2\sigma_0$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At $t = 8\sqrt3 \approx 13.86$ (natural units, $\hbar=m=1$).

   Set the dispersion formula equal to the target width $2\sigma_0=4$:

   $$
   \sigma(t)^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \left(\frac{t}{2\sigma_0}\right)^{\!2} = 4 + \frac{t^2}{16} = 16
   $$

   $$
   \frac{t^2}{16} = 12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t^2 = 192 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{192} = 8\sqrt3 \approx 13.856
   $$

   Substituting back gives $\sigma = \sqrt{4+12} = 4$ exactly, which is the
   check worth doing.

   The mistake this question is shaped to catch is subtracting in the wrong
   place: writing $t/(2\sigma_0) = 2\sigma_0$ and solving for
   $t = 4\sigma_0^2 = 16$. That treats the *width* as doubling when the
   spreading term alone doubles, forgetting that the initial width adds in
   quadrature and so already accounts for part of the target. The two
   contributions combine as squares, not linearly, which is what makes the
   answer $8\sqrt3$ rather than $16$.

   Two structural features are visible in the algebra.

   The doubling time scales as $\sigma_0^2$. Repeating the calculation for
   general $\sigma_0$ gives $t = 2\sqrt3\,\sigma_0^2$ (in these units), so
   a packet twice as wide takes four times as long to double. That is the
   next question's point in reverse, and it is why a macroscopic object,
   whose $\sigma_0$ is enormous in atomic units, never visibly spreads.

   The early behaviour is not linear. For $t\ll2\sigma_0^2$ the quadratic
   term is a small correction and $\sigma(t)\approx\sigma_0$, nearly flat;
   only for $t\gg2\sigma_0^2$ does $\sigma(t)\approx t/(2\sigma_0)$ and the
   growth become a straight line. So "the packet spreads linearly" is the
   long-time limit, not a description of the whole motion, and reading it
   as the whole motion would predict the wrong answer here by a wide
   margin.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why a *narrower* initial packet ($\sigma_0$ small)
   disperses *faster*. Connect this to the uncertainty principle.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a narrow packet is forced to contain a wide spread of momenta,
   and it is the momentum spread that drives the spreading.

   Read it off the formula first. The growth term is
   $\left(\hbar t/(2m\sigma_0)\right)^2$, with $\sigma_0$ in the
   *denominator*, so halving the initial width quadruples the rate at
   which the variance grows. The packet that started better localized ends
   up worse localized, and quickly.

   The mechanism is the uncertainty principle supplying the momentum
   spread. A Gaussian saturates the bound, so
   $\Delta p = \hbar/(2\sigma_0)$: narrow in $x$ means broad in $p$. The
   components of the packet travel at speeds $v=p/m$ spread over
   $\Delta v = \Delta p/m = \hbar/(2m\sigma_0)$, so after time $t$ the
   fastest and slowest parts have separated by roughly $\Delta v\,t$. That
   is exactly the growth term:

   $$
   \sigma(t)^2 = \sigma_0^2 + \left(\Delta v\,t\right)^2
   $$

   an initial width and a velocity-spread contribution, added in
   quadrature the way independent spreads combine. The runners in the
   lesson's analogy start bunched, and the tighter you bunch them the wider
   the range of speeds you are forced to give them.

   Three clarifications keep this from being over-read.

   The momentum spread itself does *not* change with time. Each momentum
   component picks up only a phase $e^{-i\hbar k^2t/(2m)}$, which leaves
   $|\phi(k)|^2$ untouched, so $\Delta p$ is constant for a free particle
   forever. Only $\Delta x$ grows, which means the uncertainty *product*
   grows too: the packet starts at the minimum $\hbar/2$ and drifts above
   it. A minimum-uncertainty state does not stay one.

   None of this is decay or noise. The norm is exactly $1$ throughout;
   probability is being spread over a wider region, not lost.

   And it does not happen in a bound state. An energy eigenstate of the
   well or the oscillator has a stationary density and does not spread at
   all, however narrow it is, because the potential keeps returning the
   fast components rather than letting them run away. Dispersion is a
   free-particle statement, and the potential is what defeats it.

   </details>

## Summary

- Ehrenfest's theorem $d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle p\rangle/m$ follows
  directly from the general $d\langle A\rangle/dt=(i/\hbar)\langle[\hat
  H,A]\rangle$ identity and the canonical commutator.
- A free wave packet's different momentum components drift at different
  rates, causing the packet to spread: $\sigma(t)^2=\sigma_0^2+(\hbar
  t/(2m\sigma_0))^2$.
- This is exact, unitary (norm-preserving) quantum dynamics, numerically
  verified against the platform's split-operator time evolution.
