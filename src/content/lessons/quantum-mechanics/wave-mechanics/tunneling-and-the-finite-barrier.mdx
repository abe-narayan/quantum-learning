<LessonHook eyebrow="Through a hill it could never climb">
Picture a ball rolled at a hill it doesn't have enough energy to climb. Classically the outcome is never in doubt: it rises partway, stalls, and rolls back down, every time. Give a quantum particle the same hill and the same shortfall in energy, and every so often it turns up on the far side anyway, having never had the energy to get over the top.
</LessonHook>

## What 'cannot get over' means for a wavefunction

No energy bookkeeping is violated when a particle turns up beyond a
barrier it lacked the energy to climb; what changes is what "can't get over"
means for something described by a <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term> instead of a trajectory.
Solve the Schrödinger equation honestly inside the "forbidden" region,
and the wavefunction doesn't vanish there. It decays, smoothly and
continuously, which is all that's needed for some <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> to survive
to the far side of a barrier that isn't infinitely thick.

## Oscillation on one side, decay on the other

The infinite well's math showed that in a classically *allowed* region
($E>V$), $\psi$ oscillates (sines and cosines). This lesson asks: what
happens in a classically *forbidden* region ($E<V$)? The same equation,
solved honestly, gives a different but equally legitimate kind of
solution: not zero, not oscillatory, but exponential.

## Exponentials inside, and what survives to the far edge

### Inside the barrier

For a barrier of height $V_0$ where the particle's energy $E<V_0$, the
time-independent Schrödinger equation inside the barrier reads

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x) + V_0\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi''(x) = \kappa^2\,\psi(x), \qquad \kappa \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}
$$

Critically, $\kappa^2$ is **positive** here (since $V_0>E$), the
opposite sign from the well's $\psi''=-k^2\psi$. This flips the character
of the solution entirely: instead of $\sin,\cos$ (oscillatory), the
general solution is

$$
\psi(x) = C\,e^{\kappa x} + D\,e^{-\kappa x}
$$

real exponentials. **The wavefunction is nonzero inside the barrier.**
It decays (for a wave incident from the left, the physically relevant
solution is dominated by the decaying $e^{-\kappa x}$ term), but decaying
is not the same as vanishing.

### Why a finite barrier transmits

If the barrier extends from $x=0$ to $x=a$ (finite width $a$), the
decaying amplitude $\psi(0)e^{-\kappa a}$ at $x=a$ is small but nonzero
for finite $\kappa a$, and matching this to an oscillatory solution
resuming beyond the barrier (where $V=0$ again and $E>V$ once more)
produces a transmitted wave with nonzero amplitude. The transmission
probability falls off roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, exponentially
sensitive to both the barrier's height (through $\kappa$) and its width
$a$. That exponential is why <Term id="quantum-tunneling">tunneling</Term> matters at subatomic barriers and
disappears at everyday ones: doubling $a$ squares an already tiny number.
If the barrier were infinitely wide, $e^{-2\kappa a}\to0$ exactly,
recovering the classical "never crosses" result as a limiting case.

## Continuity of psi and its slope does all the work

Tunneling isn't the particle "borrowing energy" or briefly violating
energy conservation. Energy is exactly conserved throughout. It's a
direct, unavoidable consequence of the Schrödinger equation being a
second-order differential equation: continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$
across the barrier's edges (required for $\psi''$ to stay well-defined)
forces some amplitude to always be present in and past the barrier,
however small.

Before running the simulator, it helps to see the shape being described:
a flat-bottomed rectangular bump, height $V_0$, sitting in an otherwise
empty landscape.

Everywhere outside the shaded bump, $V=0$ and the earlier lessons'
oscillatory solutions apply; inside it, $V=V_0$ and this section's
exponential solution takes over. The wavefunction has to be continuous
across both edges, and that matching condition is what lets a sliver of
amplitude survive to the far side.

Two real devices depend directly on this exponential sensitivity to
barrier width. A scanning tunneling microscope images individual atoms
by measuring the tunneling current across the vacuum gap between a sharp
metal tip and a surface, a current so sensitive to that gap's width that
it can resolve height differences far smaller than a single atom. The
same effect sets a practical limit on flash memory: the thin insulating
layer that traps charge on a memory cell can't be made arbitrarily thin,
because past a certain point the stored charge tunnels back out on its
own, one of the physical limits on how far that layer can be scaled down.

## Doubling the barrier height

**Qualitatively, if the barrier height is doubled (with width fixed),
does the transmission probability increase or decrease, and roughly by
how much?**

$\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$ increases (since $V_0$ increased), so
$\kappa a$ increases, so $e^{-2\kappa a}$, the rough transmission scale,
*decreases*, and it decreases exponentially fast in $\kappa$, so even a
modest increase in barrier height suppresses transmission sharply. This
prediction is directly checkable: raise the Explorer's barrier height
parameter and watch $P(\text{transmitted})$ fall.

**Now with real numbers.** Take an electron facing a vacuum gap with
$V_0-E = 4$ eV, roughly a metal's work function, which is the situation
inside a scanning tunneling microscope:

$$
\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m_e(V_0-E)}}{\hbar} = \frac{\sqrt{2(9.11\times10^{-31})(6.41\times10^{-19})}}{1.055\times10^{-34}} \approx 1.02\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} = 10.2\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}
$$

At a gap of $a=0.5$ nm, $2\kappa a \approx 10.2$, giving
$e^{-2\kappa a}\approx 3.7\times10^{-5}$: about one electron in $27{,}000$
gets across. Now move the tip $1$ ångström closer, $a = 0.4$ nm. The
exponent drops by $2\kappa(0.1\ \mathrm{nm}) \approx 2.05$, and the
transmission rises by $e^{2.05}\approx 7.8$.

Nearly an order of magnitude of current for a tenth of a nanometre of
motion is what makes atomic-resolution imaging possible. An STM does not
need a probe sharpened to atomic dimensions so much as it needs this
exponential: whichever atom on the tip is closest to the surface carries
almost all the current, so the instrument images with a single atom
whether or not it was built to.

## Exponentially small is still not zero

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking $\psi=0$ inside a classically forbidden region.** It's
exponentially *small* for a thick or tall barrier, not exactly zero. The
distinction is the entire mechanism behind tunneling.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting all-or-nothing transmission.** A real packet is a
superposition (Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson) of many momenta, some
above and some below the classical threshold. This course's tunneling
preset splits into transmitted and reflected *parts*, not one outcome or
the other, matching the correct probabilistic interpretation.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does $\kappa^2$ flip sign between the classically allowed
   ($E>V$) and forbidden ($E<V$) cases? Trace it through the
   Schrödinger equation directly.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the equation is $\psi'' = \dfrac{2m(V-E)}{\hbar^2}\psi$, and the
   bracket $V-E$ is what changes sign. Everything else in the coefficient
   is positive.

   Rearrange the time-independent Schrödinger equation to isolate the
   second derivative:

   $$
   -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi = E\psi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi'' = \frac{2m\left(V-E\right)}{\hbar^2}\,\psi
   $$

   In an allowed region $E>V$, so the coefficient is negative and the
   equation reads $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ with $k=\sqrt{2m(E-V)}/\hbar$. In a
   forbidden region $E<V$, so the coefficient is positive and it reads
   $\psi'' = +\kappa^2\psi$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V-E)}/\hbar$. The two
   definitions differ only in which way the subtraction runs, chosen so
   that the square root stays real in each case.

   The sign is not cosmetic; it changes what kind of function solves the
   equation. $\psi''=-k^2\psi$ says the curvature always points *back
   toward the axis*, which is a restoring behaviour and produces the
   oscillating $\sin$ and $\cos$. $\psi''=+\kappa^2\psi$ says the curvature
   points *away* from the axis, which is runaway behaviour and produces
   the real exponentials $e^{\pm\kappa x}$. A function cannot oscillate
   without curving back, so no combination of exponentials will ever cross
   zero more than once, and no combination of sines will ever decay
   monotonically.

   The reading that matters physically is what happens to kinetic energy.
   In the allowed region $E-V>0$ is the kinetic energy, and it sets a
   wavelength. In the forbidden region $E-V<0$ would be a negative kinetic
   energy, which is why classical mechanics stops here: no real momentum
   corresponds to it. Quantum mechanics does not stop, because the equation
   still has a solution. It just stops being a wave, and the "imaginary
   momentum" $i\hbar\kappa$ that a formal substitution would give is
   another way of naming the decay rate rather than a physical momentum
   anyone could measure.

   The wrong turn to name is concluding from $E<V$ that $\psi=0$ inside,
   by analogy with the infinite well. The infinite well gets $\psi=0$
   because $V\to\infty$ sends $\kappa\to\infty$ and the decay becomes
   instantaneous. For finite $V_0$, $\kappa$ is finite, the decay is
   gradual, and the difference between "small" and "zero" is the entire
   tunnelling effect.

   </details>

2. If a barrier's width is doubled (height fixed), what happens to the
   rough transmission estimate $e^{-2\kappa a}$? Express the new estimate
   in terms of the old one, then apply it to the scanning-tunneling
   numbers above: what does widening that $0.5$ nm gap to $1.0$ nm do to
   the current?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The estimate is **squared**. Since $\kappa$ is unchanged (it depends on
   $V_0-E$, not on $a$),

   $$
   e^{-2\kappa(2a)} = e^{-4\kappa a} = \left(e^{-2\kappa a}\right)^{2}
   $$

   so $T_{\text{new}} = T_{\text{old}}^{2}$. Squaring a number already far
   below $1$ is catastrophic for it, which is the point.

   With the lesson's STM numbers, $\kappa\approx10.2\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$
   and $T\approx3.7\times10^{-5}$ at $a=0.5$ nm, or roughly one electron in
   $27{,}000$. Doubling the gap gives

   $$
   T \approx \left(3.7\times10^{-5}\right)^2 \approx 1.4\times10^{-9}
   $$

   about one electron in $700$ million. The current falls by the same
   factor of $27{,}000$ that the original transmission was, from an extra
   half-nanometre of vacuum.

   Two things follow, and both are worth stating.

   The dependence on width is exponential, not proportional. Doubling $a$
   does not halve the current; it squares an already tiny fraction. A
   student who reasons "twice as thick, half as much gets through" is
   applying an intuition that fits a leaky pipe and does not fit an
   exponential. The same arithmetic run backwards is what makes the STM
   work: the lesson's $0.1$ nm approach multiplied the current by about
   $7.8$, and it is that steepness, not any sharpening of the tip, that
   gives atomic resolution.

   Height and width do not enter symmetrically. Width sits linearly in the
   exponent, so doubling it squares $T$. Height enters through
   $\kappa\propto\sqrt{V_0-E}$, so doubling $V_0-E$ multiplies $\kappa$ by
   $\sqrt2\approx1.41$ and raises $T$ to the power $1.41$: a large effect,
   but a smaller one than doubling the width. Width is the more powerful
   knob, which is why flash-memory scaling runs into tunnelling by thinning
   an oxide rather than by weakening it.

   One honest caveat: $e^{-2\kappa a}$ is the exponential *scale* of the
   transmission, not the exact coefficient. The full expression carries a
   prefactor that depends on $E$, $V_0$ and $a$ algebraically. That
   prefactor is of order one and changes slowly, so it never competes with
   an exponential, which is why the estimate is good enough to predict
   ratios like these.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why $P(\text{transmitted})+P(\text{reflected})\approx1$
   is a *check* on the simulator's correctness, not merely a nice-to-have.
   What would it mean physically if this sum were significantly less
   than 1?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because it is the norm-conservation theorem restated for this geometry.
   Unitary evolution preserves $\int|\psi|^2dx = 1$ exactly, and once the
   packet has finished interacting, all of that probability is either to
   the left of the barrier or to the right of it. So the two fractions must
   add to one, as an identity rather than an approximation, and a
   simulator that reports otherwise has a defect.

   That makes the sum a genuine test with a pass condition known in
   advance, which is what separates a check from a decoration. It does not
   require knowing the right answer for $T$; it holds for every barrier
   height, width and incident energy, so it can be run on any preset
   without a reference calculation to compare against.

   If the sum came out significantly below $1$, the candidate explanations
   are all numerical, since no physical process here destroys probability.

   Probability is still inside the barrier, or still in transit. The
   accounting is only meaningful after the interaction is complete; read
   too early, some amplitude is in neither region yet. This is the first
   thing to rule out, and the fix is to run longer.

   Probability has left the grid. A finite simulation box means a packet
   that reaches an edge is either wrapped around by periodic boundaries or
   clipped, and the left-and-right accounting no longer covers all of
   space.

   The evolution operator is not unitary. A method not built from
   pure-phase factors leaks norm every step, which is the failure mode the
   split-operator method exists to avoid.

   What the sum would *not* mean is that the barrier absorbed the particle.
   A real, static potential barrier has no mechanism to do that: it is a
   Hermitian term in the Hamiltonian, and a Hermitian Hamiltonian conserves
   probability. Absorption in a simulation is something you have to add
   deliberately, as a complex potential, and if it is not there the missing
   probability is missing from the arithmetic rather than from the physics.

   Note also the direction of the check. The sum being $1$ confirms nothing
   about whether $T$ itself is *correct*: a method could conserve
   probability perfectly while splitting it between the two sides in the
   wrong proportion. That is the same distinction the next lesson draws
   between norm conservation and accuracy, and it is why the lesson also
   asks you to compare $T$ against $e^{-2\kappa a}$ rather than stopping at
   the sum.

   </details>

## Summary

- In a classically forbidden region, the Schrödinger equation gives
  exponential (not oscillatory) solutions. The wavefunction decays but
  does not vanish.
- A barrier of finite width lets a nonzero (though possibly tiny)
  amplitude survive to the far side, producing real, calculable
  transmission.
- Transmission falls off roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, explaining why
  tunneling is significant at atomic scales and negligible at macroscopic
  ones.
- The Wavefunction Explorer's tunneling preset numerically confirms
  probability conservation across the whole process.
