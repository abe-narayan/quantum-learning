## Motivation

Classically, a ball with less energy than a hill's height can never cross
it. It rolls partway up and back down, full stop. Quantum mechanically,
this is false: solve the Schrödinger equation honestly inside the
"forbidden" region, and the wavefunction doesn't vanish there. It decays,
smoothly and continuously, which is all that's needed for some amplitude
to survive to the far side of a barrier that isn't infinitely thick.

## Intuition

The infinite well's math showed that in a classically *allowed* region
($E>V$), $\psi$ oscillates (sines and cosines). This lesson asks: what
happens in a classically *forbidden* region ($E<V$)? The same equation,
solved honestly, gives a different but equally legitimate kind of
solution: not zero, not oscillatory, but exponential.

## Mathematical Development

### Inside the barrier

For a barrier of height $V_0$ where the particle's energy $E<V_0$, the
time-independent Schrödinger equation inside the barrier reads

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x) + V_0\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi''(x) = \kappa^2\,\psi(x), \qquad \kappa \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}}{\hbar}
$$

Critically, $\kappa^2$ is **positive** here (since $V_0>E$), the
opposite sign from the well's $\psi''=-k^2\psi$. This flips the character
of the solution entirely: instead of $\sin,\cos$ (oscillatory), the
general solution is

$$
\psi(x) = C\,e^{\kappa x} + D\,e^{-\kappa x}
$$

real exponentials. **The wavefunction is nonzero inside the barrier.**
It decays (for a wave incident from the left, the physically relevant
solution is dominated by the decaying $e^{-\kappa x}$ term), but decaying
is not the same as vanishing.

### Why a finite barrier transmits

If the barrier extends from $x=0$ to $x=a$ (finite width $a$), the
decaying amplitude $\psi(0)e^{-\kappa a}$ at $x=a$ is small but nonzero
for finite $\kappa a$, and matching this to an oscillatory solution
resuming beyond the barrier (where $V=0$ again and $E>V$ once more)
produces a transmitted wave with nonzero amplitude. The transmission
probability falls off roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, exponentially
sensitive to both the barrier's height (through $\kappa$) and its width
$a$, which is exactly why tunneling is dramatic for subatomic barriers
and utterly negligible for everyday macroscopic ones (a wide barrier
makes $e^{-2\kappa a}$ astronomically small). If the barrier were
infinitely wide, $e^{-2\kappa a}\to0$ exactly, recovering the classical
"never crosses" result as a limiting case.

## Physical Interpretation

Tunneling isn't the particle "borrowing energy" or briefly violating
energy conservation. Energy is exactly conserved throughout. It's a
direct, unavoidable consequence of the Schrödinger equation being a
second-order differential equation: continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$
across the barrier's edges (required for $\psi''$ to stay well-defined)
forces some amplitude to always be present in and past the barrier,
however small.

Before running the simulator, it helps to see the shape being described:
a flat-bottomed rectangular bump, height $V_0$, sitting in an otherwise
empty landscape.

Everywhere outside the shaded bump, $V=0$ and the earlier lessons'
oscillatory solutions apply; inside it, $V=V_0$ and this section's
exponential solution takes over. The wavefunction has to be continuous
across both edges, which is exactly the matching condition that lets a
sliver of amplitude survive to the far side.

Two real devices depend directly on this exponential sensitivity to
barrier width. A scanning tunneling microscope images individual atoms
by measuring the tunneling current across the vacuum gap between a sharp
metal tip and a surface, a current so sensitive to that gap's width that
it can resolve height differences far smaller than a single atom. The
same effect sets a practical limit on flash memory: the thin insulating
layer that traps charge on a memory cell can't be made arbitrarily thin,
because past a certain point the stored charge tunnels back out on its
own, one of the physical limits on how far that layer can be scaled down.

## Worked Example

**Qualitatively, if the barrier height is doubled (with width fixed),
does the transmission probability increase or decrease, and roughly by
how much?**

$\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$ increases (since $V_0$ increased), so
$\kappa a$ increases, so $e^{-2\kappa a}$, the rough transmission scale,
*decreases*, and it decreases exponentially fast in $\kappa$, meaning even
a modest barrier-height increase can suppress transmission dramatically.
This qualitative prediction is directly checkable: raise the Explorer's
barrier height parameter and watch $P(\text{transmitted})$ fall sharply.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking $\psi=0$ inside a classically forbidden region.** It's
exponentially *small* for a thick or tall barrier, not exactly zero. The
distinction is the entire mechanism behind tunneling.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting all-or-nothing transmission.** A real packet is a
superposition (Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson) of many momenta, some
above and some below the classical threshold. This course's tunneling
preset genuinely splits into transmitted and reflected *parts*, not one
outcome or the other, matching the correct probabilistic interpretation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a classically forbidden region, the Schrödinger equation gives
  exponential (not oscillatory) solutions. The wavefunction decays but
  does not vanish.
- A barrier of finite width lets a nonzero (though possibly tiny)
  amplitude survive to the far side, producing genuine, calculable
  transmission.
- Transmission falls off roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, explaining why
  tunneling is significant at atomic scales and negligible at macroscopic
  ones.
- The Wavefunction Explorer's tunneling preset numerically confirms
  probability conservation across the whole process.
