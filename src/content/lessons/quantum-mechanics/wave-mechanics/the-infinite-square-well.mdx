## The first potential solved in closed form

The system is a particle strictly confined to $0 < x < L$ by walls of
infinite potential energy. Everything about it can be worked out with
nothing beyond the time-independent Schrödinger equation and two
boundary conditions.

## A guitar string has the same problem

Classically, a ball bouncing between two rigid walls can have *any*
energy: bounce it harder, it just goes faster. Quantum mechanically, the
wavefunction must fit the box the way a guitar string's vibration must
fit its fixed length. Only certain wavelengths, and hence only certain
energies, are geometrically possible.

## How two zeros at the walls force a discrete spectrum

### Setting up the equation

The potential is $V(x)=0$ for $0<x<L$ and $V(x)=\infty$ outside. Inside
the well, the time-independent Schrödinger equation (from two lessons
ago) reduces to

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''(x) = E\,\psi(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi''(x) = -k^2\psi(x), \qquad k \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}
$$

with general solution $\psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx)$.

### Boundary conditions force quantization

Outside the well $\psi=0$ (infinite potential energy makes the region
inaccessible), and $\psi$ must be continuous, so $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$.

$\psi(0)=0$: $A\sin(0)+B\cos(0) = B = 0$, eliminating the cosine term
entirely.

$\psi(L)=0$: with $B=0$, this requires $A\sin(kL)=0$. Since $A=0$ would
give the trivial (zero, unnormalizable) solution, we need
$\sin(kL)=0$, which holds only for

$$
kL = n\pi, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
$$

($n=0$ is excluded: it gives $\psi\equiv0$ everywhere, not a valid
state). This is the entire mechanism. **The boundary conditions permit
only a discrete set of $k$ values**, and since $E=\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, only a
discrete set of energies.

### The energy levels

$$
k_n = \frac{n\pi}{L} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2}}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots
$$

This is the formula `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(n, L, mass)`
implements (natural units drop the $\hbar^2$). Before drawing the ladder,
commit to what it should look like.

The gaps between rungs widen sharply as $n$ grows ($E_2-E_1\ne E_3-E_2$),
the visual signature of the $n^2$ scaling this section just derived.

### Normalized eigenstates

$$
\psi_n(x) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right), \qquad 0 < x < L
$$

Normalizing: $\int_0^L A^2\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,dx = A^2\cdot\dfrac L2 = 1$
(using $\int_0^L\sin^2(\cdot)\,dx=L/2$, a standard half-period average),
giving $A=\sqrt{2/L}$:

$$
\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)
$$

which is `infiniteSquareWellEigenstate`'s formula (shifted to a
well centered at $x=0$ rather than starting at $x=0$, a pure relabeling of
the origin that changes nothing physical, as the What Is a Wavefunction
lesson's continuum-limit discussion already established).

### Orthogonality

Distinct eigenstates satisfy $\int_0^L\psi_n(x)\psi_m(x)\,dx=0$ for
$n\ne m$, a direct consequence of $\hat H$'s Hermiticity (proven in
general in Mathematical Foundations: distinct eigenvalues of a <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term>
operator have orthogonal eigenvectors), confirmed here numerically by
`overlapProbability` between different-$n$ eigenstates in the engine's
test suite.

## Counting humps, and paying for curvature

$n$ counts the number of "humps" in $\psi_n$ (equivalently, $n-1$
internal nodes, points where $\psi_n=0$ inside the well). More nodes means
more curvature, and the kinetic term $-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''$ charges
directly for curvature, which is why $E_n$ climbs with $n$. Comparing the
Explorer's ground and excited state presets shows both changes at once.

Real semiconductor devices build this confinement on purpose. A
**quantum well** is a thin layer of one semiconductor sandwiched between
layers of another with a higher bandgap, trapping electrons in a
potential well only a few nanometers wide. The discrete $E_n$ ladder
this lesson derives is the literal operating principle behind quantum
well laser diodes, used in fiber-optic telecom transmitters and
Blu-ray players. Shrinking the well's width raises $E_n$ by this lesson's
own $E_n\propto1/L^2$ scaling, and that is the knob engineers turn to set
the emitted wavelength: a laser's colour is chosen at the growth stage, by
depositing a layer of the right thickness.

## Two levels in a well of width ten

**For $L=10$ (natural units, $\hbar=m=1$), find $E_1$ and $E_3$, and
confirm $E_3/E_1=9$.**

$$
E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2}{2L^2} = \frac{n^2\pi^2}{200}
$$

$$
E_1 = \frac{\pi^2}{200} \approx 0.0493, \qquad E_3 = \frac{9\pi^2}{200} \approx 0.444
$$

$$
\frac{E_3}{E_1} = \frac{9\pi^2/200}{\pi^2/200} = 9 = 3^2 \;\checkmark
$$

confirming the $E_n\propto n^2$ scaling, and matching the Wavefunction
Explorer's live comparison panel at these preset defaults.

## The cosine term and the missing n = 0

<Callout type="warning">
**Including the cosine term.** $\psi(0)=0$ eliminates $B\cos(kx)$
entirely. A common error is keeping both terms and applying boundary
conditions incorrectly, or forgetting $n=0$ must be excluded (it isn't a
valid state, not merely the "ground state with zero energy").
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming a literal infinite wall in a numerical simulation.** A
literal infinity can't be represented on a computer or evolved with the
periodic-boundary numerical method this course uses. The platform uses a
finite but very tall wall instead, an honest, standard, well-controlled
approximation (see the Numerically Evolving Quantum States lesson).
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $L=5$, find $E_1, E_2, E_4$ and confirm the ratios match
   $1:4:16$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In natural units ($\hbar=m=1$), $E_n = n^2\pi^2/(2L^2) =
   n^2\pi^2/50$:

   $$
   E_1 = \frac{\pi^2}{50} \approx 0.1974, \qquad E_2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{50} \approx 0.7896, \qquad E_4 = \frac{16\pi^2}{50} \approx 3.1583
   $$

   matching `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(n, 5)` to eight decimal places.
   The ratios are

   $$
   \frac{E_1}{E_1} : \frac{E_2}{E_1} : \frac{E_4}{E_1} = 1 : 4 : 16
   $$

   since every constant, $\pi^2$, the $2$, and $L^2$ alike, cancels in the
   ratio, leaving only $n^2$: $1^2$, $2^2$, $4^2$.

   That cancellation is worth noticing on its own. It means the *shape* of
   the spectrum is universal for this potential: any infinite well, of any
   width, holding any mass, has levels in the proportions
   $1:4:9:16:\ldots$. The width and the mass set the overall energy scale
   and nothing else. This is why the graded problem asking for $E_4/E_2$
   can be answered without knowing $L$ at all.

   The scaling in the other direction is the one with engineering
   consequences. $E_n \propto 1/L^2$, so halving the well's width
   quadruples every level. Narrower confinement is more expensive,
   steeply, which is the same $1/L^2$ that sets the colour of a quantum
   well laser and makes the smallest quantum dots glow bluest.

   The common numerical slip is dropping the factor of $2$ in the
   denominator, which comes from the classical $p^2/2m$ and survives
   natural units untouched. It doubles every energy, and it is invisible in
   the ratios, so checking $1:4:16$ will not catch it. Only comparing an
   absolute value against the formula or the simulator will.

   </details>

2. How many internal nodes does $\psi_4(x)$ have inside the well?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Three.

   $\psi_4(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(4\pi x/L)$ vanishes wherever
   $4\pi x/L$ is a multiple of $\pi$, that is at

   $$
   x = 0,\ \frac{L}{4},\ \frac{L}{2},\ \frac{3L}{4},\ L
   $$

   five zeros in total. Two of them, $x=0$ and $x=L$, are the walls, and
   the question asks for zeros *inside*. That leaves three.

   The general rule is $n-1$ internal nodes for $\psi_n$, which is easy to
   state and easy to misapply in exactly one way: counting the walls. The
   walls are forced zeros for every state, including the ground state, so
   they carry no information about which level you are looking at. Only the
   interior crossings do. A state with $6$ interior zeros is $n=7$, not
   $n=6$, and since energy goes as $n^2$ the difference between those two
   readings is $49E_1$ against $36E_1$: a large error from an off-by-one.

   Why the count rises with $n$ at all is the physics underneath. Fitting
   more half-wavelengths into a fixed width means the wavefunction has to
   turn around more often, so $|\psi''|$ grows, and the kinetic term
   $-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''$ charges directly for that curvature. Nodes
   are therefore a visual readout of energy: count the interior crossings
   in the Explorer's density plot, add one, and square, and you have the
   level's energy in units of $E_1$ without touching the formula.

   One caution when reading the *density* rather than the wavefunction.
   $|\psi_n|^2$ touches zero at the same places but does not change sign
   there, so the nodes appear as points where the curve dips to the axis
   between humps. The hump count is $n$ and the node count is $n-1$;
   whichever you count, be clear which one you are reporting.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why $\psi_n(0)=\psi_n(L)=0$ is required physically
   (not just mathematically). What would $\psi_n(0)\ne 0$ imply about
   the particle's presence outside the well?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $|\psi|^2$ is a probability density, and a nonzero value at the
   wall would mean a nonzero probability of finding the particle where the
   potential energy is infinite: an outcome with unbounded energy, which no
   finite-energy state can have.

   The argument runs in two steps.

   Outside the well the potential is infinite, so any nonzero amplitude
   there contributes an infinite amount to $\langle V\rangle =
   \int V(x)|\psi(x)|^2dx$. A state with infinite energy is not a solution
   of the eigenvalue equation for any finite $E$, so $\psi$ must be exactly
   zero outside, not merely small.

   Then continuity forces the boundary values. $\psi$ has to be continuous
   for $\psi''$ to exist in the Schrödinger equation at all, so the inside
   solution must match the outside solution at the walls, and the outside
   solution is $0$. Hence $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$.

   If instead $\psi_n(0)\ne0$, continuity would carry a nonzero amplitude
   across into the forbidden region, and the particle would have a genuine,
   nonzero probability of being found somewhere it can never be. The
   wavefunction would also have a jump discontinuity if you insisted on
   zero outside anyway, and a jump has no finite second derivative, so the
   equation the state is supposed to solve would be undefined at the wall.

   Two refinements keep this honest.

   The condition on $\psi'$ is *not* imposed here, and that is deliberate.
   For a finite step, both $\psi$ and $\psi'$ must be continuous, which is
   what makes tunnelling work later in this course. At an infinite wall
   $\psi'$ is allowed to jump, which is why the sine can arrive at the wall
   with a nonzero slope and stop dead. Demanding continuity of $\psi'$ as
   well would over-constrain the problem and leave only the zero solution.

   And the infinite well is an idealization, which the simulator makes
   plain by using a tall-but-finite wall instead. For a finite $V_0$ the
   wavefunction does leak into the wall, decaying as $e^{-\kappa x}$ with
   $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$, and the boundary value is small rather
   than zero. Letting $V_0\to\infty$ sends $\kappa\to\infty$ and collapses
   that tail to nothing, recovering the hard boundary condition as a limit.
   So "zero at the wall" is the $V_0\to\infty$ endpoint of the same physics
   the tunnelling lesson works out, not a separate rule.

   </details>

## Summary

- Boundary conditions $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$, applied to the general
  solution $A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$, force $k_n=n\pi/L$ and hence quantized
  $E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$.
- <Term id="normalization">Normalized</Term> eigenstates are $\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$,
  mutually orthogonal by Hermiticity.
- Both formulas are implemented in `potentials.ts` and visualized live in
  the Wavefunction Explorer's infinite-well presets.
