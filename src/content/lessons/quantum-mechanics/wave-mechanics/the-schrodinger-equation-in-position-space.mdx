## Kinetic plus potential, promoted to operators

Energy in classical mechanics splits into kinetic ($p^2/2m$) plus
potential ($V(x)$). The Hamiltonian operator does the same split, with
$p$ replaced by the operator $\hat p$ derived two lessons ago. Substituting
that operator's explicit differential form into the abstract time-evolution
equation is pure bookkeeping: no new physical input required, just
algebra applied to something already proven.

## Substituting the explicit Hamiltonian in

### The position-space Hamiltonian

By direct analogy with the classical energy $E = \dfrac{p^2}{2m}+V(x)$,
promote each piece to its operator:

$$
\hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V(\hat x) = \frac{1}{2m}\left(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{\!2} + V(x) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V(x)
$$

using $\hat p^2 = (-i\hbar\,d/dx)(-i\hbar\,d/dx) = -\hbar^2\,d^2/dx^2$
directly (apply the derivative twice; the two minus signs multiply to a
plus, and the two factors of $i$ supply $i^2=-1$, which is where the
surviving minus sign in $-\hbar^2$ comes from).

### Substituting into the abstract equation

Take $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt = \hat H|\psi\rangle$ from the last course
and apply $\langle x|\cdot\rangle$ to both sides, working in the position
representation as every quantity in this course has been.
The left side becomes $i\hbar\,\partial\psi(x,t)/\partial t$;
the right side, using the explicit $\hat H$ above, becomes
$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2+V(x)\psi(x,t)$. Together:

$$
\boxed{\;i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi(x,t)}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi(x,t)}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\,\psi(x,t)\;}
$$

This *is* the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, derived here as a
direct substitution into an equation already proven from Hermiticity, not
introduced as a fresh axiom.

### Stationary states, revisited

The last course's Stationary States lesson showed that energy
eigenstates evolve by a pure phase, $|\psi(t)\rangle =
e^{-iEt/\hbar}|\psi(0)\rangle$, with $|\psi(t)\rangle$'s *probabilities*
exactly constant in time. In position space, this means: if $\psi_n(x)$
solves the **time-independent** Schrödinger equation

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_n''(x) + V(x)\psi_n(x) = E_n\,\psi_n(x)
$$

(an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> equation for the differential operator $\hat H$, the
same shape as $H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle$ for a matrix), then
$\psi(x,t)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\psi_n(x)$ solves the full time-dependent
equation, and $|\psi(x,t)|^2=|\psi_n(x)|^2$ for all $t$: the numerically
verified stationarity the Infinite Square Well and Harmonic Oscillator
lessons build on directly.

## Curvature is what the kinetic term charges for

The kinetic term $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''$ measures curvature: a
sharply-curving <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term> (large $|\psi''|$) carries large kinetic
energy, which is the earlier lesson's observation that a tightly localized
wavefunction has broad, high-momentum Fourier content, restated as a
property of the equation. The potential term is the plainest thing on the
page: it weights each position by the potential energy a classical
particle would have there.

## Checking that a phase factor solves it

**Verify that $\psi(x,t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi_0(x)$ satisfies the
time-dependent equation, given that $\psi_0$ solves the time-independent
one with energy $E$.**

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = i\hbar\left(\frac{-iE}{\hbar}\right)e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi_0(x) = E\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi_0(x)
$$

$$
\hat H\psi = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,\hat H\psi_0(x) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,E\,\psi_0(x)
$$

(using the time-independent equation on $\psi_0$ in the last step). Both
sides come out to $E\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi_0(x)$, so the ansatz holds.

## Time-dependent against time-independent

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing the time-dependent and time-independent equations.** The
time-independent one is an eigenvalue equation for $\hat H$ with no time
dependence at all. It determines the *stationary states* $\psi_n(x)$ and
their energies, which then get dressed with a phase factor to build actual
time-dependent solutions.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Dropping the $\hbar^2$ or a sign in the kinetic term.** The double
derivative and the two factors of $-i\hbar$ combine to a **positive**
$\hbar^2/2m$ coefficient with an overall **minus** sign in front, a
common transcription slip.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Starting from $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$, show explicitly that $\hat
   p^2\psi = -\hbar^2\psi''$ (apply the operator twice, step by step).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Apply the operator once, then again to the result:

   $$
   \hat p\,\psi = -i\hbar\,\psi'
   $$

   $$
   \hat p^2\psi = \hat p\left(\hat p\,\psi\right) = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\left(-i\hbar\,\psi'\right) = (-i\hbar)(-i\hbar)\,\psi'' = (-i)^2\hbar^2\,\psi'' = -\hbar^2\,\psi''
   $$

   The constant $-i\hbar$ pulls out of the second derivative because
   differentiation is linear, and $(-i)^2 = i^2 = -1$, leaving the single
   minus sign in front of $\hbar^2$.

   Feeding that into the kinetic term gives the sign the lesson boxes:

   $$
   \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} = \frac{-\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}
   $$

   Two sign errors are worth pointing at directly, because they are
   different mistakes that produce the same wrong-looking answer.

   Writing $(-i\hbar)^2 = +\hbar^2$ mistakes where the surviving minus comes
   from. Squaring destroys the explicit minus, since $(-1)^2 = +1$; what
   supplies the overall minus is $i^2 = -1$ instead. So the sign in front of
   the $i$ never mattered here, and $(+i\hbar)^2$ would have given
   $-\hbar^2$ too, leaving the kinetic term exactly as it stands.

   Writing $\hat p^2\psi = \left(-i\hbar\psi'\right)^2 = -\hbar^2(\psi')^2$
   squares the *result* rather than applying the operator twice. Operator
   powers mean repeated application, not pointwise squaring of the output,
   and the difference shows immediately: the correct answer is linear in
   $\psi$, as any operator acting on a state must be, while $(\psi')^2$ is
   not.

   The physical reading of the surviving minus is that the kinetic energy
   charges for *curvature with the right sign*. Where $\psi$ curves back
   toward the axis, $\psi''$ has the opposite sign to $\psi$, so
   $-\hbar^2\psi''/2m$ is a positive multiple of $\psi$: positive kinetic
   energy. A wavefunction that wiggles faster has larger $|\psi''|$ and
   costs more, which is the same statement as narrow packets carrying
   broad, high-$k$ Fourier content.

   </details>

2. Why does $|\psi(x,t)|^2=|\psi_n(x)|^2$ for all $t$, given
   $\psi(x,t)=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\psi_n(x)$? (One line, using
   $|e^{i\theta}|=1$.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   |\psi(x,t)|^2 = \left|e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\right|^2\left|\psi_n(x)\right|^2 = 1\cdot|\psi_n(x)|^2 = |\psi_n(x)|^2
   $$

   using $|zw| = |z||w|$ and $|e^{i\theta}|=1$ for real $\theta$, which
   requires $E_n$ to be real. It is, because $\hat H$ is Hermitian, so
   nothing here is accidental.

   The consequences run further than the one line suggests, and both are
   worth stating.

   Every expectation value built from the density is frozen too, not just
   the density itself: $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle x^2\rangle$, $\Delta x$,
   $\langle V\rangle$, all of them, since each is an integral of some
   function of $x$ against $|\psi|^2$. And the same argument in momentum
   space, where the phase factors out identically, freezes the momentum
   statistics as well. This is what "stationary" means, and it is why a
   textbook orbital can be drawn as a still image.

   The phase is not absent, though, and this is where the concept usually
   goes wrong. $\psi(x,t)$ genuinely changes at every instant; it rotates
   in the complex plane at angular frequency $E_n/\hbar$. It is
   unobservable only because it is *global*, the same factor at every $x$,
   so it cancels out of every squared magnitude. The moment two different
   energies are present, their two phases turn at different rates, the
   ratio between them is no longer constant, and the phase stops being
   global. Then the cross terms in $|\psi|^2$ carry
   $\cos\big((E_2-E_1)t/\hbar\big)$ and the density visibly beats, which is
   the superposition case the capstone lesson works out.

   So the correct summary is not "the state does not change." It is "the
   state changes by a factor that no measurement can see, and only because
   a single energy is present."

   </details>

3. In natural units ($\hbar=m=1$, this platform's convention), write the
   time-independent Schrödinger equation with the $\hbar$'s and $m$'s
   dropped.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   -\frac12\,\psi''(x) + V(x)\,\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x)
   $$

   Setting $\hbar=1$ and $m=1$ in $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi''+V\psi=E\psi$
   turns the kinetic coefficient into $-\tfrac12$. The factor of $2$ is
   *not* one of the constants being set to one: it came from the classical
   $p^2/2m$, and it stays.

   That is the mistake this question exists to catch. Writing
   $-\psi''+V\psi=E\psi$ silently doubles every kinetic energy and would
   put every computed level off by a factor that depends on the potential.
   Check it against a known answer: with the infinite well's
   $\psi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)$, the correct equation gives
   $E_n = \tfrac12(n\pi/L)^2 = n^2\pi^2/(2L^2)$, which at $L=10$ is
   $E_1 = \pi^2/200 \approx 0.0493$, matching the well lesson's worked
   example and the engine's `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel`. Dropping the
   $\tfrac12$ would give $0.0987$, and the simulator's comparison panel
   would disagree with the formula immediately.

   It is worth being explicit about what natural units are and are not
   doing here. They are a choice of measuring stick, not a change to the
   physics: energies are now expressed in whatever unit makes $\hbar$ and
   the particle's mass equal to one, so the *numbers* change while every
   ratio, every level spacing relative to another, and every dimensionless
   result stays exactly as it was. Restoring the constants is always
   possible by dimensional analysis, and the platform works in natural
   units for the same reason numerical code usually does: it keeps
   quantities near order one and away from the floating-point extremes
   that $10^{-34}$ invites.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\hat H = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x)$, from directly
  substituting $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ into the classical energy split.
- Substituting into $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt=\hat H|\psi\rangle$ gives the
  time-dependent Schrödinger equation, a derivation, not a new axiom.
- Stationary states solve the time-independent (eigenvalue) version and
  evolve by a pure phase, with probability density exactly constant.
- The Wavefunction Explorer numerically integrates the time-dependent
  equation directly; the accuracy of that integration is checkable via the
  live $\langle H\rangle$ readout against known analytical energies.
