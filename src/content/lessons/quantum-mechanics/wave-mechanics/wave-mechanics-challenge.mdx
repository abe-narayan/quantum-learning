## Everything that crossed over, and what carried it

The crossing went lesson by lesson: the wavefunction as a
continuum-limit <Term id="basis">basis</Term> expansion, momentum space as a Fourier-transform
basis change, the Schrödinger equation as calculus substituted into
already-proven dynamics, and three fully solved systems (the infinite
well, the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>, and tunneling). Only new mathematical
machinery was needed to express the same four postulates in an
infinite-dimensional setting.

This is not a new observation. Dirac's transformation theory established in
1926 that Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics
were the same physics written in two representations. The table below is
that equivalence, laid out row by row for the two courses you have just
worked through.

## Recap: Finite Dimensions to Continuous Position

| Finite-dimensional (last course) | Continuous position (this course) |
|---|---|
| State = unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^n$ | State = $\psi(x)$ with $\int|\psi|^2dx=1$ |
| Basis $\{|i\rangle\}$, $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$ | Position basis $\{|x\rangle\}$, $\int|\psi(x)|^2dx=1$ (What Is a Wavefunction) |
| $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$, a finite sum | $\langle f(x)\rangle=\int f(x)|\psi(x)|^2dx$ (Expectation Values) |
| $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ (stated) | $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ (derived from $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$) |
| Change of basis via inner products | Momentum space $\phi(k)$ via the Fourier transform |
| $i\hbar\,d\lvert\psi\rangle/dt=H\lvert\psi\rangle$, $H$ a matrix | Same equation, $H=-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\tfrac{d^2}{dx^2}+V(x)$ |
| Harmonic oscillator via ladder operators | Same spectrum, via direct substitution of a Gaussian ansatz |
| $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$, exact for 2-level systems | Split-operator numerical approximation, exact only as $\Delta t\to0$ |

The last row is the one substantively new feature: finite-dimensional
evolution (a $2\times2$ or truncated matrix exponential) could often be
computed exactly. Continuous-position evolution generally cannot, and that
gap is why this course had to build a real numerical engine (the FFT, the
split-operator method) rather than only proving formulas on paper.

**1. A particle is in the infinite well's ground state. Using both this
course's position-space energy formula and Ehrenfest's theorem, explain
why $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$ are both exactly constant in
time for this state, without recomputing any integral.**

The ground state is an energy <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term>, so (Schrödinger Equation in
Position Space lesson) it evolves by a pure phase,
$\psi(x,t)=e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\psi_1(x)$, and $|\psi(x,t)|^2=|\psi_1(x)|^2$
for all $t$. Every expectation value computed from $|\psi|^2$, including
$\langle x\rangle$, is therefore time-independent. Ehrenfest's theorem
then gives $d\langle x\rangle/dt=\langle p\rangle/m=0$ automatically
(a constant $\langle x\rangle$ has zero derivative), consistent with the
well's symmetric ground state having $\langle p\rangle=0$ by parity.

**2. Compare the harmonic oscillator's ground state to the infinite
well's ground state: which has a nonzero wavefunction outside its
"classical" region, and why?**

The harmonic oscillator's Gaussian ground state (Harmonic Oscillator in
Position Space) has smooth exponential tails extending past the classical
turning points, a direct consequence of the *smooth*, finite potential
allowing exponentially decaying (not zero) solutions there. This is the
Tunneling lesson's forbidden-region mechanism, applied to a bound state
instead of a barrier. The infinite well's walls are (numerically,
approximately) infinite, so its "forbidden region" decay constant
$\kappa\to\infty$, collapsing the tail to exactly zero. The well is the
$V_0\to\infty$ limiting case of the same underlying physics.

**3. A superposition of two energy eigenstates is prepared. Using the
Schrödinger equation in position space, explain why its probability
density oscillates in time, while a single eigenstate's does not.**

$\psi(x,t) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} +
\psi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\right)$. Computing $|\psi(x,t)|^2$ produces
cross terms proportional to $\cos\!\big((E_2-E_1)t/\hbar\big)$ (from
expanding $|a+b|^2=|a|^2+|b|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}(a^*b)$ with the two
time-dependent phases): a beat frequency set by the energy gap alone, and
what the Wavefunction Explorer's superposition preset animates. A single
eigenstate has no second term to beat against, so no oscillation survives.

## The two misreadings worth carrying forward

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "continuous" quantum mechanics as a different theory from the
finite-dimensional qubit picture.** Every result in this course specialized
the same four postulates to an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. No new
axiom was introduced anywhere.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing numerical approximation with physical approximation.** The
split-operator method's $\Delta t$-dependent error is a *numerical*
artifact of simulating on a computer; it has nothing to do with the
physics itself being approximate. The underlying Schrödinger equation this
course derived is exact.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the recap table, identify which single derivation in this course
   most directly reproved something the last course had only stated
   (rather than deriving something new from scratch).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The derivation of $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ in The Position and Momentum
   Operators.

   The table names it outright: the row reads "$[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$
   (stated)" on the left and "(derived from $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$)" on the
   right. It is the only row where the left-hand entry is flagged as
   something asserted rather than established, and the Position and
   Momentum lesson of the last course said so explicitly at the time,
   promising the justification once continuous position existed. The debt
   was paid with two lines of the product rule.

   Notice how little the derivation needed. Only one physical input went
   in, that a plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ carries momentum $p$, and that
   fixed $\hat p$ uniquely; the commutator then followed from calculus with
   no further physics. Everything the last course built on top of that
   commutator, the Heisenberg bound above all, was left untouched, because
   its proof used only the commutator's *value* and never $\hat p$'s form.

   The near-miss worth ruling out is the harmonic oscillator. Its spectrum
   is derived twice in these two courses, algebraically from
   $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ and then again by substituting a Gaussian into the
   differential equation, and that is a genuine independent cross-check.
   But the first derivation was a derivation, not an assertion, so the
   second one confirms a result rather than discharging a promise. What the
   position-space treatment *did* add there is different in kind: it
   identified which physical potential, $\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$, the
   abstract $H$ had been standing in for, which the algebra alone could
   never have told you.

   The other rows are translations rather than proofs of anything
   outstanding: the Born rule reinterpreted as a density, the basis change
   carried out by Fourier transform, the same evolution equation with a
   differential $H$. None of them settles a claim the last course had left
   unsupported.

   </details>

2. A particle tunnels through a barrier. Using Ehrenfest's theorem,
   explain whether $\langle p\rangle$ is conserved throughout the process
   (hint: consider whether $V(x)$ is uniform).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. $\langle p\rangle$ is not conserved, and Ehrenfest's second theorem
   says exactly why.

   The theorem gives $d\langle p\rangle/dt = -\langle V'(x)\rangle$. For a
   rectangular barrier, $V$ is flat everywhere except at its two edges,
   where it steps up and then down, so $V'$ is nonzero there. As long as
   the packet has amplitude sitting on an edge, $\langle V'(x)\rangle\ne0$
   and $\langle p\rangle$ changes. Momentum is conserved only where the
   potential is uniform, which is true before the packet arrives and after
   it has left, and false during the interaction.

   The sign and size are worth working out, because they make the effect
   concrete. A packet arriving from the left with $\langle p\rangle = +p_0$
   leaves as two pieces: a transmitted part still moving right with roughly
   $+p_0$, and a reflected part moving left with roughly $-p_0$. Afterwards

   $$
   \langle p\rangle \approx \left(P_T - P_R\right)p_0
   $$

   which for a typical barrier, where $P_R$ is close to $1$, is *negative*.
   The packet's average momentum has reversed. The barrier pushed back on
   it, and $-\langle V'\rangle$ integrated over the interaction is the
   impulse that did so.

   Two clarifications.

   Energy *is* conserved throughout, exactly, because $H$ commutes with
   itself and $V$ has no explicit time dependence. So this is a case where
   one average is rigorously constant and another is not, and the
   difference between them is entirely whether the corresponding operator
   commutes with $H$. Ehrenfest's theorem in its general form,
   $d\langle A\rangle/dt=(i/\hbar)\langle[\hat H,A]\rangle$, makes that the
   whole criterion.

   Nothing here is peculiar to tunnelling. The same statement holds for a
   classical ball bouncing off a hill: momentum reverses, energy does not
   change, and the force that reversed it acted only on the slope. What
   tunnelling adds is that the outgoing state is a *superposition* of the
   two outcomes rather than one or the other, so $\langle p\rangle$ lands
   at an intermediate value that no single measurement would ever return.

   </details>

3. If a future course extended this platform to three spatial dimensions,
   which single piece of this course's machinery (the grid, the FFT, the
   split-operator method) would need the most fundamental rework, and
   which would generalize essentially unchanged?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The **grid** needs the most fundamental rework. The **split-operator
   method** generalizes essentially unchanged, and the FFT sits between
   them, extended mechanically but not rethought.

   **The split-operator method survives untouched**, because the argument
   behind it never mentioned dimension. $\hat V$ is still diagonal in
   position space, $\hat T = (\hat p_x^2+\hat p_y^2+\hat p_z^2)/2m$ is
   still diagonal in momentum space, the same symmetric
   potential-half/kinetic/potential-half sequence still applies, each
   factor is still a pure phase so norm is still preserved exactly, and the
   error is still $O(\Delta t^3)$ from the same commutator. The only change
   is that the kinetic phase reads $e^{-i\hbar(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2)\Delta
   t/2m}$ instead of $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$, which is a change to one
   expression rather than to the method.

   **The FFT extends rather than changes.** A multidimensional Fourier
   transform is separable: transform along $x$ for every $(y,z)$, then
   along $y$, then along $z$, reusing the existing one-dimensional
   radix-2 routine each time. No new algorithm and no new mathematics, but
   real work in indexing and in memory access patterns, since striding
   through a three-dimensional array along its slowest axis is nothing like
   walking a contiguous line.

   **The grid is where the rework is fundamental**, and for two distinct
   reasons. Structurally, `Grid1D` and the `amplitudes` array indexed by a
   single integer become a three-index object, and every routine that walks
   them, every expectation value, every probability-in-a-region sum,
   changes shape with them; the Riemann sums pick up $\Delta x\,\Delta
   y\,\Delta z$ in place of $\Delta x$. Quantitatively, the cost explodes:
   $N$ points becomes $N^3$, so the $256$-point grid this course uses
   becomes about $16.8$ million complex amplitudes, and refining the
   resolution by a factor of two now costs eight times the memory rather
   than two.

   That second point is the one with consequences beyond engineering. The
   $N^3$ scaling is why real three-dimensional simulations lean on symmetry
   rather than brute force: a central potential separates into radial and
   angular equations, which reduces the problem back to something
   one-dimensional in $r$ with the angular part handled analytically by
   spherical harmonics. That is the route the hydrogen atom takes later in
   this track, and it is chosen because the grid does not scale, not
   because the split-operator method fails.

   The wrong answer worth naming is the split-operator method, on the
   intuition that a more complicated geometry must need a more complicated
   integrator. It does not. The integrator is indifferent to how many
   dimensions the vectors it phases have; what suffers is the cost of
   storing and traversing them.

   </details>

## Summary

- Every idea in this course (the wavefunction, momentum space, the
  Schrödinger equation, bound states, tunneling) is the last course's
  finite-dimensional postulates, carried across to continuous position by
  a continuum limit, a Fourier transform, and direct calculus.
- The one new ingredient is numerical: continuous-position time
  evolution generally has no closed form, so this course built (and
  honestly documented the limitations of) a real split-operator engine.
- Bound states (well, oscillator) are stationary; superpositions of
  distinct energies beat at a frequency set exactly by the energy gap;
  barriers transmit a calculable, generally nonzero fraction of any
  incident wave packet.
