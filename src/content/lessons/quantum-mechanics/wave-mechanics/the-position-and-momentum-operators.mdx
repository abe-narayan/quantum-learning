## The preview that owed you a derivation

The "From Classical to Quantum" course's Position and Momentum lesson was
explicit that it was only a *preview*. It wrote down
$[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$, drew the uncertainty principle out of it, and
said the justification would have to wait. The reason for the wait was
concrete: $\hat p$ is a derivative, and a derivative has nothing to act on
until states are functions of a continuous variable. Three lessons ago
they became exactly that.

So the debt can now be paid. Two operators are needed, and the lesson
treats them very differently. Position is almost free: in a basis labelled
by position, $\hat x$ can only multiply. Momentum is the interesting one,
because nothing in the position basis obviously represents it, and this
lesson does not postulate an answer. It states one physical fact about
momentum, that a plane wave of wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ carries momentum
$p$, and lets that fact determine the operator uniquely. The commutator
then falls out of the product rule, and the uncertainty principle follows
from the commutator with no further physics.

## Start from the plane wave and work backwards

A free particle with definite momentum $p$ is described (from ordinary
wave physics, de Broglie's relation $p=\hbar k$) by a plane wave $e^{ikx}
= e^{ipx/\hbar}$. Whatever operator represents momentum, it had better
have this plane wave as an eigenfunction with <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $p$. That single
requirement pins down the operator's form completely.

## One requirement, and p-hat has no choice

### The position operator

In the position representation, the position operator acts by simple
multiplication:

$$
\hat x\,\psi(x) = x\,\psi(x)
$$

This is what makes $\langle x\rangle = \int\psi^* (x\psi)\,dx = \int
x|\psi|^2 dx$ from the last lesson consistent with the general
$\langle\psi|\hat x|\psi\rangle$ definition: $\hat x$ acting as "multiply
by $x$" is what turns the general sandwich into that integral.

### Deriving the momentum operator

Require an operator $\hat p$ such that the plane wave $\phi_p(x) =
e^{ipx/\hbar}$ is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $p$:

$$
\hat p\,\phi_p(x) = p\,\phi_p(x)
$$

Differentiate $\phi_p$ once: $\dfrac{d\phi_p}{dx} = \dfrac{ip}{\hbar}\phi_p(x)$.
Rearranging,

$$
p\,\phi_p(x) = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\phi_p}{dx} = -i\hbar\,\frac{d\phi_p}{dx}
$$

Comparing to the eigenvalue equation above identifies the operator
directly:

$$
\boxed{\hat p = -i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}}
$$

Nothing was postulated to get here. The *only* input was "plane waves
carry definite momentum $p=\hbar k$," a statement about what momentum
physically means, translated into an eigenvalue requirement and then
solved.

### Hermiticity check

For $\hat p$ to be a valid observable it must be <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term> (the last
course's requirement for real eigenvalues). Using integration by parts
(boundary terms vanish for normalizable $\psi,\phi$, which go to zero at
$\pm\infty$):

$$
\int \phi^*\left(-i\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dx}\right)dx = -i\hbar\Big[\phi^*\psi\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + i\hbar\int\frac{d\phi^*}{dx}\psi\,dx = \int\left(-i\hbar\frac{d\phi}{dx}\right)^{\!*}\psi\,dx
$$

confirming $\langle\phi|\hat p\psi\rangle = \langle\hat p\phi|\psi\rangle$,
which is the Hermiticity condition. It also shows why the factor of $i$ in
$\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ is not optional. Integration by parts flips the
sign of a derivative moved from one side of the inner product to the
other, so $d/dx$ on its own is *anti*-Hermitian; multiplying by $i$
supplies the compensating sign flip through the conjugation of the bra.

### Reproving the commutator

Apply $[\hat x,\hat p] = \hat x\hat p - \hat p\hat x$ to an arbitrary
$\psi(x)$, using the product rule on the second term:

$$
\hat x\hat p\,\psi = x\left(-i\hbar\frac{d\psi}{dx}\right) = -i\hbar x\psi'
$$

$$
\hat p\hat x\,\psi = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}(x\psi) = -i\hbar\left(\psi + x\psi'\right) = -i\hbar\psi - i\hbar x\psi'
$$

Subtracting,

$$
[\hat x,\hat p]\psi = \hat x\hat p\,\psi - \hat p\hat x\,\psi = -i\hbar x\psi' - \left(-i\hbar\psi - i\hbar x\psi'\right) = i\hbar\,\psi
$$

Since this holds for *every* $\psi$, the operators satisfy

$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar\,\hat I
$$

the relation the last course stated, now derived from two lines of
calculus rather than asserted. The <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term>
$\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \tfrac12|\langle[\hat x,\hat p]\rangle| =
\hbar/2$, proven in full generality in that course's Expectation Values
and Uncertainty lesson, carries over with no changes at all: that proof
never needed $\hat p$'s explicit form, only the value of the commutator.

## Why multiplying and differentiating refuse to commute

$\hat x$ and $\hat p$ don't commute because "multiply by $x$" and "take a
derivative" interfere with each other: differentiating a product picks up
a term the other order does not, and that leftover term *is* the
commutator.

The physical content is visible in the shapes involved. Squeeze a Gaussian
packet to a width of $\sigma = 0.1$ nm, roughly an atomic radius, and its
slopes steepen in proportion, so the momentum content it needs in order to
be that narrow grows as $1/\sigma$. Putting numbers to it,
$\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\sigma) \approx 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{kg\,m/s}$,
which for an electron is a speed spread of around $580$ km/s. Confining an
electron to an atom is what makes it fast. There is no way to have a
narrow spike and a slowly varying derivative at once, and the uncertainty
principle is that statement about calculus, read as physics.

## Checking the eigenvalue equation in one line

**Verify $\hat p\phi_p(x)=p\,\phi_p(x)$ directly for $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$.**

$$
\hat p\,\phi_p = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}e^{ipx/\hbar} = -i\hbar\cdot\frac{ip}{\hbar}\,e^{ipx/\hbar} = p\,e^{ipx/\hbar} = p\,\phi_p
$$

confirming the eigenvalue equation this whole derivation was built to
satisfy, by direct substitution.

built with a definite central momentum approximates one closely, and

## The factor of i is load-bearing

<Callout type="warning">
**Dropping the factor of $i$.** $\hat p = -\hbar\,d/dx$ (missing the $i$)
is not Hermitian and gives an imaginary $[\hat x,\hat p]$. The $i$ is
load-bearing, not decorative.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying $\hat x\hat p$ and $\hat p\hat x$ in the wrong order.**
$\hat p\hat x\,\psi$ requires differentiating the *product* $x\psi(x)$
(product rule, picking up the extra $\psi$ term). It is not the same as
$x$ times $\hat p\psi$.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show that $\hat x$ is trivially Hermitian (i.e. that "multiply by the
   real number $x$" satisfies the Hermiticity condition without needing
   integration by parts).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Hermiticity asks that $\langle\phi|\hat x\psi\rangle =
   \langle\hat x\phi|\psi\rangle$. Write both sides out:

   $$
   \langle\phi|\hat x\psi\rangle = \int \phi(x)^*\,x\,\psi(x)\,dx, \qquad \langle\hat x\phi|\psi\rangle = \int \left(x\,\phi(x)\right)^*\psi(x)\,dx = \int x^*\,\phi(x)^*\,\psi(x)\,dx
   $$

   Since $x$ is a real number, $x^* = x$, and the two integrands are
   identical term for term. The two sides are equal, and nothing was moved
   from one factor to the other, so no integration by parts and no boundary
   terms were needed.

   The contrast with $\hat p$ is the point of the exercise. Multiplication
   is a *pointwise* operation: at each $x$ it scales the value of $\psi$
   there by a number, and the only way that can fail Hermiticity is if the
   number is not real. Differentiation is not pointwise. It compares
   neighbouring values, so moving it across the inner product means
   integrating by parts, which flips a sign and produces boundary terms.
   That flip is why $d/dx$ alone is anti-Hermitian and why the $i$ in
   $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$ is required.

   The same one-line argument covers more than $\hat x$. Any operator that
   acts as multiplication by a real-valued function $V(x)$ is Hermitian by
   this identical calculation, which is why the potential term of the
   Hamiltonian never needs a separate check. The rule to carry forward is
   that a multiplication operator is Hermitian exactly when the function it
   multiplies by is real; a complex potential, the tool for modelling
   absorption, is non-Hermitian for precisely this reason and loses norm on
   purpose.

   </details>

2. Compute $[\hat p,\hat x]$ directly (not by just negating the result
   above, redo the calculation with the operators in the other order) and
   confirm it equals $-i\hbar$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $[\hat p,\hat x] = -i\hbar$.

   Apply $[\hat p,\hat x] = \hat p\hat x - \hat x\hat p$ to an arbitrary
   $\psi(x)$, keeping careful track of which term needs the product rule.
   The first term differentiates the product $x\psi$:

   $$
   \hat p\hat x\,\psi = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\,\psi\right) = -i\hbar\left(\psi + x\psi'\right)
   $$

   The second differentiates $\psi$ alone and then multiplies by $x$, so
   no product rule appears:

   $$
   \hat x\hat p\,\psi = x\left(-i\hbar\,\psi'\right) = -i\hbar\,x\psi'
   $$

   Subtracting, the $x\psi'$ terms cancel and the leftover is the term the
   product rule generated:

   $$
   [\hat p,\hat x]\psi = -i\hbar\psi - i\hbar x\psi' - \left(-i\hbar x\psi'\right) = -i\hbar\,\psi
   $$

   Since this holds for every $\psi$, the operator identity is
   $[\hat p,\hat x] = -i\hbar\,\hat I$, which is $-[\hat x,\hat p]$ as
   antisymmetry requires. Redoing the calculation rather than negating is
   the check: an independent route landing on the same answer means the
   product rule was applied to the right factor both times.

   The mistake this question is designed to expose is applying the product
   rule in the wrong place, or in both places. It is easy to write
   $\hat x\hat p\,\psi = -i\hbar\,(x\psi)'$ by reflex, which would make the
   two terms identical and give a commutator of zero, that is, position and
   momentum commuting and no uncertainty principle at all. The operators
   are read right to left: in $\hat x\hat p$, $\hat p$ acts first on $\psi$
   and $\hat x$ multiplies the result afterwards, so the derivative never
   sees the $x$.

   The whole of the canonical commutator therefore comes from one term in
   one product rule. That is a small piece of calculus carrying the entire
   position-momentum uncertainty relation, since the bound
   $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\tfrac12|\langle[\hat x,\hat p]\rangle|$ collapses
   to zero the moment that term is dropped.

   </details>

3. Using $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$, compute $\hat p^2\psi$ for
   $\psi(x)=\sin(kx)$, and identify what physical quantity
   $\langle\psi|\hat p^2|\psi\rangle/(2m)$ represents.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\hat p^2\psi = \hbar^2k^2\sin(kx) = \hbar^2k^2\,\psi$, and
   $\langle\hat p^2\rangle/(2m)$ is the expectation value of the
   **kinetic energy**.

   Apply the operator twice, one derivative at a time:

   $$
   \hat p\,\psi = -i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}\sin(kx) = -i\hbar k\cos(kx)
   $$

   $$
   \hat p^2\psi = -i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\left(-i\hbar k\cos(kx)\right) = (-i\hbar)^2 k\,\frac{d}{dx}\cos(kx) = \left(-\hbar^2\right)\left(-k^2\sin(kx)\right) = \hbar^2k^2\,\psi
   $$

   using $(-i\hbar)^2 = -\hbar^2$ and $\tfrac{d}{dx}\cos(kx) =
   -k\sin(kx)$. So $\sin(kx)$ is an eigenfunction of $\hat p^2$ with
   eigenvalue $\hbar^2k^2$.

   The identification of $\langle\hat p^2\rangle/(2m)$ follows from
   promoting the classical kinetic energy $p^2/2m$ to an operator, exactly
   as the Hamiltonian's kinetic term is built in the next lesson: for this
   state it equals $\hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, a definite number.

   The subtlety worth the effort here is that $\sin(kx)$ is an
   eigenfunction of $\hat p^2$ while emphatically *not* being an
   eigenfunction of $\hat p$. Check it: $\hat p\psi = -i\hbar k\cos(kx)$,
   and $\cos$ is not a multiple of $\sin$. Written as a superposition,

   $$
   \sin(kx) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ikx} - e^{-ikx}\right)
   $$

   the state is an equal mix of momentum $+\hbar k$ and momentum
   $-\hbar k$. So $\langle p\rangle = 0$ while $\langle p^2\rangle =
   \hbar^2k^2$, giving $\Delta p = \hbar k$. The particle has a perfectly
   definite kinetic energy and a completely undetermined direction of
   travel, which is exactly what a standing wave is.

   The wrong turn is to reason that a definite $\hat p^2$ means a definite
   $\hat p$, and to report $\langle p\rangle = \hbar k$. Squaring destroys
   the sign, so $\hat p^2$ cannot distinguish the two directions and has a
   larger set of eigenfunctions than $\hat p$ does. This is why an infinite
   well's eigenstates, which are exactly these sines, have sharp energies
   and zero average momentum: the particle is not sitting still, it is
   going both ways at once.

   One caveat on rigour. $\sin(kx)$ is not normalizable on the whole line,
   so $\langle\psi|\hat p^2|\psi\rangle$ is not literally defined there;
   the calculation above is an operator identity, and it becomes a genuine
   expectation value on a finite interval, which is precisely the infinite
   square well two lessons ahead, with $k = n\pi/L$.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\hat x$ acts by multiplication; $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$ is *derived*
  from requiring plane waves $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ to be its eigenfunctions with
  eigenvalue $p$.
- The factor of $i$ is what makes $\hat p$ Hermitian, as integration by
  parts shows; without it the operator is anti-Hermitian.
- $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ follows from two lines of calculus (product
  rule), reproving what the last course previewed.
- The uncertainty principle's proof from the last course applies
  unchanged, since it only used the commutator's value.
