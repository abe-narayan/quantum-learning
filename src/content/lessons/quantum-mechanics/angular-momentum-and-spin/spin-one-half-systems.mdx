## A claim that is easy to under-read

The <Term id="pauli-matrices">Pauli matrices</Term> $X,Y,Z$ from Course 1 are, up to a factor of
$\hbar/2$, the operators $J_x,J_y,J_z$ at $j=\tfrac12$. That claim is
easy to state and easy to under-read, so this lesson checks it against
the actual matrices before drawing any conclusions from it.

The conclusion is worth the check. If the identification holds, then the
qubit machinery built in Course 1 was never an abstraction that happens
to resemble spin. It was spin, and every picture in it acquires a
physical reading.

## Entry for entry, against the engine

### The identification

$j=\tfrac12$'s general angular momentum operators (Lesson 2's ladder
construction) are $2\times2$ matrices. Comparing
`angularMomentumX(0.5)`, `angularMomentumY(0.5)`, `angularMomentumZ(0.5)`
directly against `PAULI_X`, `PAULI_Y`, `PAULI_Z`:

$$
S_x=\frac\hbar2X,\qquad S_y=\frac\hbar2Y,\qquad S_z=\frac\hbar2Z
$$

and the engine's test suite confirms the match to floating-point
precision. This is not an analogy holding up unexpectedly well. The
general construction of Lesson 2, evaluated at $j=\tfrac12$, produces the
Pauli matrices as its output.

$S_z$'s eigenvalues are $\pm\hbar/2$ ($m=\pm\tfrac12$, the
"$-j$ to $j$ in integer steps" rule with $j=\tfrac12$ giving the two
values $-\tfrac12,+\tfrac12$), and $\vec S^{\,2}$'s <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> is
$\tfrac12\left(\tfrac12+1\right)\hbar^2=\tfrac34\hbar^2$, Lesson 2's
$j(j+1)\hbar^2$ formula, at $j=\tfrac12$.

### No single-valuedness restriction

Lesson 3's integer-$l$ argument rested entirely on how a position-space
wavefunction behaves under $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$. Spin has no position
dependence at all: $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$, which
are the qubit basis $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, are not functions of
angle, so there is nothing for single-valuedness to constrain.

The value $j=\tfrac12$, unavailable to orbital motion, is therefore open,
and it is what an electron's intrinsic spin turns out to use. Which
invites the obvious question about the very rotation that ruled it out
for orbital motion.

So a $2\pi$ rotation applied to a spin-$\tfrac12$
state returns it multiplied by $-1$, the very factor the orbital argument
rejected. For spin this is not a contradiction, because the sign is a
global phase on a state rather than a disagreement between two values of
one function at one point.

### The Bloch sphere, reinterpreted

Every rotation you have ever applied to a qubit
(`rotationAboutAxis`, used since Qubits & Quantum States) is, physically,
a rotation of the spin-$\tfrac12$ angular momentum direction. The Bloch
sphere point *is* the <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> expectation-value direction
$(\langle S_x\rangle,\langle S_y\rangle,\langle S_z\rangle)$. That was
always true of it; what changes here is that the arrow now points
somewhere, in a laboratory, along the axis a magnetic moment would
actually align with.

## Every single-qubit result, reread as spin

Every single-qubit result already proven (the Pauli algebra, the Bloch
sphere, $R_x,R_y,R_z$ rotations, measurement probabilities) is a
statement about a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle in a magnetic field. This
course rebuilds none of it. It supplies the physical referent.

The most familiar instance is a hydrogen nucleus: one proton, spin
$\tfrac12$. Put it in a field $B$ and the two $S_z$ eigenstates split by
$\Delta E = \gamma\hbar B$, which for a clinical scanner at a few tesla
puts the resonance in the radio band rather than anywhere near optical.

That splitting is tiny compared with $k_BT$ at body temperature, so the
two levels are populated almost equally, with an excess in the lower
state of only a few parts per million. An MRI image is reconstructed
entirely from that excess. The reason the technique works at all is
numbers: a voxel of tissue holds enough protons that a few parts per
million is still an enormous count.

## Summing the squares of three Pauli matrices

**Confirm $\vec S^{\,2}=S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2=\tfrac34\hbar^2 I$ using the
Pauli matrices directly (with $\hbar=1$, the engine's convention).**

$X^2=Y^2=Z^2=I$ (each Pauli matrix squares to the identity, a fact from
Qubits & Quantum States). So
$S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2=\tfrac14(X^2+Y^2+Z^2)=\tfrac14(I+I+I)=\tfrac34I$,
which is $j(j+1)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32=\tfrac34$, matching Lesson 2's
general formula at $j=\tfrac12$.

## Spin needed no derivation of its own

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking spin-$\tfrac12$ needed a separate derivation of its own.** It is
the $j=\tfrac12$ case of Lesson 2's construction. The Pauli matrices built
for the qubit engine satisfy the identical commutation algebra, as the
comparison above shows directly.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Picturing a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle as physically spinning in space,
like a classical top.** The "spin" name is historical; there is no
position-space wavefunction associated with it (unlike orbital angular
momentum), so there is no rotational motion to picture, only
the abstract angular-momentum algebra, realized at $j=\tfrac12$.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Confirm $S_z|{\uparrow}\rangle=\tfrac\hbar2|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and
   $S_z|{\downarrow}\rangle=-\tfrac\hbar2|{\downarrow}\rangle$ directly
   from $Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$, $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Substitute $S_z=\tfrac\hbar2Z$ and the identifications
   $|{\uparrow}\rangle=|0\rangle$, $|{\downarrow}\rangle=|1\rangle$, and
   the two eigenvalue equations you already have carry straight over:

   $$
   S_z|{\uparrow}\rangle = \frac\hbar2 Z|0\rangle = \frac\hbar2|0\rangle = \frac\hbar2|{\uparrow}\rangle, \qquad S_z|{\downarrow}\rangle = \frac\hbar2 Z|1\rangle = -\frac\hbar2|1\rangle = -\frac\hbar2|{\downarrow}\rangle
   $$

   Multiplying an operator by a constant multiplies every eigenvalue by
   that constant and changes no eigenvector, so nothing had to be
   recomputed.

   The factor of $\hbar/2$ is doing more than rescaling. $Z$ is
   dimensionless with eigenvalues $\pm1$, which is all a qubit ever needed;
   $S_z$ is a physical angular momentum, and its eigenvalues must come out
   in units of angular momentum. Matching against Lesson 2's general rule
   that $J_z$'s eigenvalues are $m\hbar$ for $m$ running from $-j$ to $j$,
   these two values are $m=\pm\tfrac12$: the entire ladder at $j=\tfrac12$,
   which has exactly $2j+1=2$ rungs and no $m=0$ in between.

   </details>

2. Using $S_\pm=S_x\pm iS_y$ and Lesson 2's general ladder formula,
   confirm $S_+|{\downarrow}\rangle\propto|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and
   $S_+|{\uparrow}\rangle=0$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Build $S_+$ from the Pauli matrices, then act with it. With
   $\hbar$ kept explicit:

   $$
   S_+ = S_x + iS_y = \frac\hbar2(X+iY) = \frac\hbar2\left[\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\right] = \frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Acting on the two basis states, with $|{\uparrow}\rangle=(1,0)^T$ and
   $|{\downarrow}\rangle=(0,1)^T$:

   $$
   S_+|{\downarrow}\rangle = \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \hbar\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \hbar|{\uparrow}\rangle, \qquad S_+|{\uparrow}\rangle = \hbar\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Now the cross-check against Lesson 2's general coefficient,
   $J_+|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m+1)}\,|j,m+1\rangle$, at
   $j=\tfrac12$:

   $$
   m=-\tfrac12:\ \sqrt{\tfrac34-\left(-\tfrac12\right)\left(\tfrac12\right)} = \sqrt{\tfrac34+\tfrac14} = 1, \qquad m=+\tfrac12:\ \sqrt{\tfrac34-\left(\tfrac12\right)\left(\tfrac32\right)} = \sqrt{0} = 0
   $$

   Coefficients $\hbar$ and $0$, exactly what the matrices gave. The
   general formula's zero at the top rung is not imposed by hand; it falls
   out of $j(j+1)-m(m+1)$ vanishing at $m=j$.

   The trap here comes from familiarity with gates. $S_+$ looks like it
   should be a spin flip, and it is nothing of the sort. $X$ is unitary
   and flips both ways, $X|{\uparrow}\rangle=|{\downarrow}\rangle$ and
   $X|{\downarrow}\rangle=|{\uparrow}\rangle$. $S_+$ is not unitary, has
   no inverse, and is not a gate any circuit can run: it maps
   $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ to the zero vector, which is not a state at all.
   Ladder operators are bookkeeping devices for relating basis vectors and
   for computing matrix elements. They are not operations you can perform
   on a qubit.

   </details>

3. Explain why $\vec S^{\,2}$ commutes with $S_z$ (a general angular
   momentum fact from Lesson 2) but $S_x$ and $S_z$ do not. Connect this
   to which quantities can be simultaneously measured for a spin-1/2
   particle.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\vec S^{\,2}$ commutes with every component because the pieces of
   $[\vec S^{\,2},S_z]$ coming from $S_x^2$ and $S_y^2$ cancel each other.
   $S_x$ and $S_z$ do not commute because that is the defining algebra:
   $[S_x,S_z]=-i\hbar S_y$, nonzero.

   Check the second one on the actual matrices. Using the Course 1 result
   $[X,Z]=-2iY$:

   $$
   [S_x,S_z] = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}[X,Z] = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}(-2iY) = -i\hbar\left(\frac\hbar2 Y\right) = -i\hbar S_y
   $$

   which is the cyclic identity $[S_z,S_x]=i\hbar S_y$ with the bracket
   reversed.

   For the first, there is a caution specific to $j=\tfrac12$ that is
   worth being honest about. At $j=\tfrac12$,
   $\vec S^{\,2}=\tfrac34\hbar^2I$ is a multiple of the identity, so it
   commutes with everything for a reason that has nothing to do with
   angular momentum. This case therefore cannot be used as evidence for
   the general theorem; it is the one value of $j$ where the theorem is
   true for free. The real argument is Lesson 2's, which holds for all $j$:
   in $[S_x^2,S_z]=S_x[S_x,S_z]+[S_x,S_z]S_x=-i\hbar(S_xS_y+S_yS_x)$ and
   the matching $[S_y^2,S_z]=+i\hbar(S_xS_y+S_yS_x)$, the two anticommutator
   terms are equal and opposite, and $[S_z^2,S_z]=0$ trivially.

   What this means for measurement, and the part that is easy to
   overstate. The compatible pair is $(\vec S^{\,2},S_z)$, which is why
   states are labelled $|j,m\rangle$: length and one projection, known
   together. But for a fixed spin-$\tfrac12$ particle, $\vec S^{\,2}$
   returns $\tfrac34\hbar^2$ on every state without exception, so
   measuring it tells you nothing you did not know. All the information a
   spin-$\tfrac12$ state carries is in a single component, and choosing
   which component to measure is a real choice, because $S_x$, $S_y$ and
   $S_z$ are mutually incompatible. Measure one and the other two are
   maximally uncertain. That is the whole content of the next lesson's
   sequential Stern-Gerlach experiment.

   </details>

4. If spin-1/2 has no position-space wavefunction, what does
   $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|{\uparrow}\rangle+\beta|{\downarrow}\rangle$
   actually describe physically, if not "where" the particle is?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It describes a *direction in ordinary three-dimensional space*: the
   axis along which this particle's spin is definitely up.

   That is a real theorem, not a picture. For any normalized $(\alpha,
   \beta)$ there is a unit vector $\hat n$ such that $|\psi\rangle$ is the
   $+\hbar/2$ eigenstate of $\hat n\cdot\vec S$. Writing
   $\alpha=\cos(\theta/2)$ and $\beta=e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)$, which
   costs nothing but a choice of overall phase, that vector is

   $$
   \hat n = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta), \qquad \langle\vec S\rangle = \frac\hbar2\hat n
   $$

   So the Bloch sphere point is not a bookkeeping device. It is where a
   Stern-Gerlach magnet has to be pointed for this particle to come out
   the "up" port with certainty, and it is the direction the particle's
   magnetic moment $\vec\mu=\gamma\vec S$ contributes along in a field. A
   spin state is an orientation.

   The misconception worth killing here: reading $|\alpha|^2$ and
   $|\beta|^2$ as the whole story, so that a superposition means "it is up
   with probability $|\alpha|^2$, we just do not know which." Take
   $\alpha=\beta=1/\sqrt2$ and $\alpha=1/\sqrt2,\ \beta=i/\sqrt2$. Both
   give $50/50$ along $z$. They are entirely different states, pointing
   along $+x$ and $+y$ respectively, and a magnet along $x$ separates them
   with certainty. The relative phase of $\beta$ is a physical direction,
   not a hidden ignorance, and a genuine statistical mixture of up and
   down points nowhere at all and gives $50/50$ on every axis.

   One thing this state is not: a description of the *whole* electron. A
   real electron's state is a wavefunction of position tensored with a
   spin state, and the spin factor is an extra two-dimensional degree of
   freedom alongside the position one, never a replacement for it. The
   hydrogen atom in the next course carries both at once, which is where
   the $2n^2$ rather than $n^2$ degeneracy comes from.

   </details>

## Summary

- $S_x,S_y,S_z=\tfrac\hbar2X,\tfrac\hbar2Y,\tfrac\hbar2Z$: the
  $j=\tfrac12$ case of this course's general angular momentum operators,
  checked against the actual matrices rather than argued by analogy.
- Spin has no position-space wavefunction, so Lesson 3's integer-only
  restriction never applies, which is why $j=\tfrac12$ is physically
  realized for spin but excluded for orbital motion.
- The Bloch sphere, used since Course 1, is physically the spin
  direction $(\langle S_x\rangle,\langle S_y\rangle,\langle S_z\rangle)$.
