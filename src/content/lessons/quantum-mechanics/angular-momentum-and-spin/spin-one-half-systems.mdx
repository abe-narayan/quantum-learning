## Motivation

The <Term id="pauli-matrices">Pauli matrices</Term> $X,Y,Z$ from Course 1 are, up to a factor of
$\hbar/2$, the operators $J_x,J_y,J_z$ at $j=\tfrac12$. That claim is
easy to state and easy to under-read, so this lesson checks it against
the actual matrices before drawing any conclusions from it.

The conclusion is worth the check. If the identification holds, then the
qubit machinery built in Course 1 was never an abstraction that happens
to resemble spin. It was spin, and every picture in it acquires a
physical reading.

## Mathematical Development

### The identification

$j=\tfrac12$'s general angular momentum operators (Lesson 2's ladder
construction) are $2\times2$ matrices. Comparing
`angularMomentumX(0.5)`, `angularMomentumY(0.5)`, `angularMomentumZ(0.5)`
directly against `PAULI_X`, `PAULI_Y`, `PAULI_Z`:

$$
S_x=\frac\hbar2X,\qquad S_y=\frac\hbar2Y,\qquad S_z=\frac\hbar2Z
$$

and the engine's test suite confirms the match to floating-point
precision. This is not an analogy holding up unexpectedly well. The
general construction of Lesson 2, evaluated at $j=\tfrac12$, produces the
Pauli matrices as its output.

$S_z$'s eigenvalues are $\pm\hbar/2$ ($m=\pm\tfrac12$, the
"$-j$ to $j$ in integer steps" rule with $j=\tfrac12$ giving the two
values $-\tfrac12,+\tfrac12$), and $\vec S^{\,2}$'s <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> is
$\tfrac12\left(\tfrac12+1\right)\hbar^2=\tfrac34\hbar^2$, Lesson 2's
$j(j+1)\hbar^2$ formula, at $j=\tfrac12$.

### No single-valuedness restriction

Lesson 3's integer-$l$ argument rested entirely on how a position-space
wavefunction behaves under $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$. Spin has no position
dependence at all: $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$, which
are the qubit basis $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, are not functions of
angle, so there is nothing for single-valuedness to constrain.

The value $j=\tfrac12$, unavailable to orbital motion, is therefore open,
and it is what an electron's intrinsic spin turns out to use. One
consequence is worth flagging: a $2\pi$ rotation applied to a spin-$\tfrac12$
state returns it multiplied by $-1$, the very factor the orbital argument
rejected. For spin this is not a contradiction, because the sign is a
global phase on a state rather than a disagreement between two values of
one function at one point.

### The Bloch sphere, reinterpreted

Every rotation you have ever applied to a qubit
(`rotationAboutAxis`, used since Qubits & Quantum States) is, physically,
a rotation of the spin-$\tfrac12$ angular momentum direction. The Bloch
sphere point *is* the <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> expectation-value direction
$(\langle S_x\rangle,\langle S_y\rangle,\langle S_z\rangle)$. That was
always true of it; what changes here is that the arrow now points
somewhere, in a laboratory, along the axis a magnetic moment would
actually align with.

## Physical Interpretation

Every single-qubit result already proven (the Pauli algebra, the Bloch
sphere, $R_x,R_y,R_z$ rotations, measurement probabilities) is a
statement about a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle in a magnetic field. This
course rebuilds none of it. It supplies the physical referent.

The most familiar instance is a hydrogen nucleus: one proton, spin
$\tfrac12$. Put it in a field $B$ and the two $S_z$ eigenstates split by
$\Delta E = \gamma\hbar B$, which for a clinical scanner at a few tesla
puts the resonance in the radio band rather than anywhere near optical.

That splitting is tiny compared with $k_BT$ at body temperature, so the
two levels are populated almost equally, with an excess in the lower
state of only a few parts per million. An MRI image is reconstructed
entirely from that excess. The reason the technique works at all is
numbers: a voxel of tissue holds enough protons that a few parts per
million is still an enormous count.

## Worked Example

**Confirm $\vec S^{\,2}=S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2=\tfrac34\hbar^2 I$ using the
Pauli matrices directly (with $\hbar=1$, the engine's convention).**

$X^2=Y^2=Z^2=I$ (each Pauli matrix squares to the identity, a fact from
Qubits & Quantum States). So
$S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2=\tfrac14(X^2+Y^2+Z^2)=\tfrac14(I+I+I)=\tfrac34I$,
which is $j(j+1)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32=\tfrac34$, matching Lesson 2's
general formula at $j=\tfrac12$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking spin-$\tfrac12$ needed a separate derivation of its own.** It is
the $j=\tfrac12$ case of Lesson 2's construction. The Pauli matrices built
for the qubit engine satisfy the identical commutation algebra, as the
comparison above shows directly.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Picturing a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle as physically spinning in space,
like a classical top.** The "spin" name is historical; there is no
position-space wavefunction associated with it (unlike orbital angular
momentum), so there is no rotational motion to picture, only
the abstract angular-momentum algebra, realized at $j=\tfrac12$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $S_x,S_y,S_z=\tfrac\hbar2X,\tfrac\hbar2Y,\tfrac\hbar2Z$: the
  $j=\tfrac12$ case of this course's general angular momentum operators,
  checked against the actual matrices rather than argued by analogy.
- Spin has no position-space wavefunction, so Lesson 3's integer-only
  restriction never applies, which is why $j=\tfrac12$ is physically
  realized for spin but excluded for orbital motion.
- The Bloch sphere, used since Course 1, is physically the spin
  direction $(\langle S_x\rangle,\langle S_y\rangle,\langle S_z\rangle)$.
