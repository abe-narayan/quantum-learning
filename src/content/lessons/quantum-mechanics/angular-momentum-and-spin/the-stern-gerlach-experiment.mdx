<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field and let it land on a glass screen. Classical physics predicted a smear. They got two spots, with nothing at all in between them.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

The classical prediction is worth stating carefully, because it is not a
weak one. A silver atom's magnetic moment gets deflected by an amount
proportional to its component along the field gradient. If those moments
point in random directions, as any classical account requires, that
component takes every value between $-|\mu|$ and $+|\mu|$, and the beam
should arrive as one continuous vertical band, densest in the middle.
Instead the middle was empty. The deflection took two values and no
others.

Stern and Gerlach had no framework for explaining what they had
recorded. Electron spin wasn't proposed for another three years, so they
read their own result through orbital angular momentum, the only
quantized angular momentum anyone knew about in 1922. The now-standard
reading came only in hindsight, once spin entered the theory: silver's
outer electron carries zero orbital angular momentum, so the splitting
they photographed could only be spin. In today's terms, those two spots
are the first direct experimental evidence of $S_z$'s quantized,
two-valued spectrum, $\pm\hbar/2$, derived abstractly over the last three
lessons.

## Mathematical Development

### What the apparatus measures

The magnetic field gradient deflects atoms by an amount proportional to
their spin's component along the field's axis. A <Term id="stern-gerlach-experiment">Stern-Gerlach</Term> (SG)
apparatus oriented along $z$ physically measures $S_z$, sorting atoms
into the $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ (deflected one way) and
$|{\downarrow}\rangle$ (deflected the other) outcomes, with the ordinary
Born-rule probabilities. An apparatus can be oriented along any axis, and
rotating it is the same operation as `rotationAboutAxis` from Qubits &
Quantum States applied before measuring in the computational basis.

Turning the apparatus turns which two states count as "up" and "down" in
the first place. Rotate the analyzer axis $\hat n$ and its own
eigenstates $|{\pm}\hat n\rangle$ rotate with it, so a fixed input state's
overlap with each, and therefore its measured probability, varies
continuously with the angle. The two *outcomes* stay two; only the odds
move.

### The sequential experiment

Prepare an atom in $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ (post-select on the
$S_z$-apparatus's "up" outcome). Send it through a **second** SG
apparatus oriented along $x$. Since
$|{\uparrow}\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|{+}\rangle+|{-}\rangle)$ (standard
basis change), the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> gives $50\%/50\%$ for the two $x$-outcomes.
Post-select on "$+x$" and send that atom through a **third** apparatus,
oriented back along $z$.

The atom was measured "$\uparrow$" a moment ago and nothing has touched
its $z$-component since, so "$\uparrow$" again with certainty looks like
the safe answer. It is wrong. The state after the second measurement is
$|{+}\rangle$, an equal superposition of $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and
$|{\downarrow}\rangle$, so the third apparatus splits it $50/50$ like any
other unpolarized input.

### Why the third measurement erases the first

The $x$-measurement actively **disturbs** the earlier $z$-information
rather than adding to it, because $S_x$ and $S_z$ do not commute (Lesson
1). Measuring $S_x$ leaves the atom in an $S_x$ <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term>, and every $S_x$
eigenstate has equal overlap with both $S_z$ eigenstates. There is
nowhere left for the earlier $z$-result to be stored.

That last sentence is the useful way to hold this. Collapse is not the
apparatus jostling the atom; a two-dimensional state space has room for
one sharp answer at a time, and writing an $x$-answer into it necessarily
overwrites the $z$-answer. The uncertainty relation derived in this
pillar's Expectation Values lesson is the quantitative version of the
same cramped bookkeeping.

## Physical Interpretation

The two-spot result on its own established quantization. The *sequential*
experiment does something the single measurement cannot: it rules out the
obvious rescue, which is that each atom carries a definite
three-dimensional moment that the apparatus merely reports coarsely.

If that were true, the atom's $z$-component would still be sitting there
after the $x$-measurement, and the third apparatus would recover it. The
third apparatus recovers nothing. Whatever the atom carries, it is not a
list of answers to every question you might ask.

## Worked Example

**Compute the joint probability of the sequence
"$\uparrow$" (first, $z$), "$+$" (second, $x$), "$\uparrow$" (third,
$z$ again).**

$P(\uparrow\text{ first})=\tfrac12$ (starting from an unpolarized beam,
by symmetry). Given "$\uparrow$," $P(+\text{ second})=\tfrac12$ (computed
above). Given "$+$," $P(\uparrow\text{ third})=\tfrac12$ (computed above,
since the second measurement erased the correlation with the first). Net
joint probability: $\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac18$.

The multiplication is the point. These three factors multiply as though
the stages were independent coin flips, which is only legitimate because
each incompatible measurement destroys the correlation the previous one
established. Replace the middle apparatus with a second $z$-apparatus and
the factorization collapses: the sequence becomes $\tfrac12\times1\times1$,
because repeating a compatible measurement adds no randomness at all.

The full branching structure makes this concrete: every path through the
tree below carries probability $\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac18$,
including the "↑,+,↑" path just computed and its seven siblings.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting the third measurement to reproduce the first with
certainty.** It does not, because the intervening $x$-measurement
overwrites the $z$-information. The Born rule applied to the
post-$x$-measurement state predicts the $50/50$ split directly; nothing
about the apparatus's quality enters the calculation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Picturing the original two-spot result as consistent with a classical
"hidden" continuous magnetic moment that just happens to always measure
as one of two values.** The sequential experiment specifically rules out
this picture; see the Physical Interpretation above.
</Callout>

   considered at all.

## Summary

- A Stern-Gerlach apparatus measures spin along its own orientation axis:
  two discrete outcomes, not a classical continuum, the first direct
  evidence of spin quantization.
- A sequential $z$-then-$x$-then-$z$ experiment gives $50/50$ odds at
  every stage, including the third. The intervening $x$-measurement
  erases the earlier $z$-information entirely.
- This is measurement disturbance from incompatible observables
  ($[S_x,S_z]\neq0$), demonstrated on a real historical experiment, not
  just asserted abstractly.
