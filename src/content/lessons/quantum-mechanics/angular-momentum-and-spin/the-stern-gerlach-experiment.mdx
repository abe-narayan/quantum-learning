## Motivation

In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent silver atoms through a
non-uniform magnetic field and looked at where they landed on a screen.
Classically, if the atoms' magnetic moments pointed in random directions,
the beam should smear into a continuous vertical band. It didn't. It
split into **exactly two** distinct spots. In modern terms, this is the
first direct experimental evidence of $S_z$'s quantized, two-valued
spectrum, $\pm\hbar/2$, derived abstractly over the last three lessons.
(Historically, Stern and Gerlach interpreted their own result via
orbital angular momentum, the only quantized angular momentum anyone
knew about in 1922; electron spin wasn't proposed until 1925, and the
now-standard reading, that silver's outer electron has zero orbital
angular momentum, so the splitting can only be spin, came only in
hindsight.)

## Mathematical Development

### What the apparatus measures

The magnetic field gradient deflects atoms by an amount proportional to
their spin's component along the field's axis. A Stern-Gerlach (SG)
apparatus oriented along $z$ physically measures $S_z$, sorting atoms
into the $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ (deflected one way) and
$|{\downarrow}\rangle$ (deflected the other) outcomes with the ordinary
Born-rule probabilities this platform has computed since Course 1. An SG
apparatus can be oriented along any axis; rotating it corresponds
exactly to `rotationAboutAxis` from Qubits & Quantum States, applied
before measuring in the computational ($S_z$) basis.

### The sequential experiment

Prepare an atom in $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ (post-select on the
$S_z$-apparatus's "up" outcome). Send it through a **second** SG
apparatus oriented along $x$. Since
$|{\uparrow}\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|{+}\rangle+|{-}\rangle)$ (standard
basis change, already used throughout this platform), the Born rule gives
exactly $50\%/50\%$ for the two $x$-outcomes. Post-select on "$+x$."
Send that atom through a **third** apparatus, oriented back along $z$.
Naively, since the atom was originally measured "$\uparrow$," it might be
expected to give "$\uparrow$" again with certainty. It doesn't: the state
after the second measurement is $|{+}\rangle$, which is an *equal*
superposition of $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$, giving
another $50\%/50\%$ split, computed directly via `qubitMeasurementProbabilities`
applied to $|+\rangle=\text{rotationY}(\pi/2)|0\rangle$'s known form
(exactly matching the platform's `the-bloch-sphere`/`measurement-and-probability`
lessons' machinery).

### Why the third measurement erases the first

The $x$-measurement is not merely "additional information" layered on
top of the $z$-information. It actively **disturbs** it, because
$S_x,S_z$ don't commute (Lesson 1). Measuring $S_x$ collapses the state
into an $S_x$ eigenstate, which (having equal overlap with both $S_z$
eigenstates) carries no memory of the original $S_z$ outcome at all. This
is the same measurement-disturbance principle from Operators, Observables
& Measurement, now demonstrated on a physically historical experiment
rather than an abstract one.

## Physical Interpretation

The two-spot result alone already proved quantization; the *sequential*
experiment goes further, demonstrating that $S_x$ and $S_z$ are
genuinely incompatible observables in a way no amount of classical
imprecision could produce. A classical magnetic moment with some
definite (if unknown) 3D direction would give a $z$-measurement result
correlated with its history, not a fresh, uncorrelated $50/50$ coin flip
every time a different axis is measured in between.

## Worked Example

**Compute the joint probability of the sequence
"$\uparrow$" (first, $z$), "$+$" (second, $x$), "$\uparrow$" (third,
$z$ again).**

$P(\uparrow\text{ first})=\tfrac12$ (starting from an unpolarized beam,
by symmetry). Given "$\uparrow$," $P(+\text{ second})=\tfrac12$ (computed
above). Given "$+$," $P(\uparrow\text{ third})=\tfrac12$ (computed above,
since the second measurement erased the correlation with the first). Net
joint probability: $\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac18$. Each
stage is genuinely independent of the one before it, once an intervening
incompatible measurement has occurred.

The full branching structure makes this concrete: every path through the
tree below carries probability $\tfrac12\times\tfrac12\times\tfrac12=\tfrac18$,
including the "↑,+,↑" path just computed and its seven siblings.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting the third measurement to reproduce the first with
certainty.** It doesn't, because the intervening $x$-measurement actively
randomizes the $z$-information. This isn't experimental imperfection,
it's the Born rule applied correctly to the post-$x$-measurement state.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Picturing the original two-spot result as consistent with a classical
"hidden" continuous magnetic moment that just happens to always measure
as one of two values.** The sequential experiment specifically rules out
this picture; see the Physical Interpretation above.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $P(\downarrow\text{ third}\mid+\text{ second})$, and confirm
   it sums with $P(\uparrow\text{ third}\mid+\text{ second})$ to $1$.
2. Repeat the joint-probability calculation for the sequence
   "$\uparrow$," "$-$" (second apparatus's other $x$-outcome),
   "$\downarrow$" (third).
3. If the *second* apparatus were oriented along $z$ again instead of
   $x$ (i.e., measuring the same axis twice in a row with nothing in
   between), what would $P(\uparrow\text{ second}\mid\uparrow\text{
   first})$ be, and why is this case different from the $x$-then-$z$
   sequence?
4. Explain, in your own words, why the original (single-apparatus)
   Stern-Gerlach result, exactly two spots, not a smear, was already
   enough to rule out a classical continuous magnetic moment, before even
   considering the sequential experiment.

## Summary

- A Stern-Gerlach apparatus measures spin along its own orientation axis:
  two discrete outcomes, not a classical continuum, the first direct
  evidence of spin quantization.
- A sequential $z$-then-$x$-then-$z$ experiment gives $50/50$ odds at
  every stage, including the third. The intervening $x$-measurement
  erases the earlier $z$-information entirely.
- This is measurement disturbance from incompatible observables
  ($[S_x,S_z]\neq0$), demonstrated on a real historical experiment, not
  just asserted abstractly.
