## Motivation

Six lessons ago the only thing on the table was $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$,
an identity about operators with no obvious physical payload. It forced
$j$ to be an integer or a half-integer, which produced the explicit
$Y_l^m$ functions on one branch and the $2\times2$ spin-$\tfrac12$ case on
the other.

This capstone puts the chain in one place and then does something more
useful than summarizing: it shows how much of the hydrogen atom is
already finished before the next course writes down a single differential
equation.

## Mathematical Development

### The logical chain, in one line each

1. $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ and the canonical commutators give
   $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ (Lesson 1).
2. Ladder operators $J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y$, built from that commutator alone,
   force $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$ and $m=-j,\dots,j$ (Lesson 2).
3. Single-valuedness under $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$ restricts *orbital*
   $l$ to integers only, giving the explicit $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$
   (Lesson 3).
4. $j=\tfrac12$, exempt from that restriction, *is* this platform's
   Pauli-matrix qubit engine (Lesson 4), and it is what Stern and Gerlach
   photographed (Lesson 5).
5. Combining two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles reproduces the Bell states,
   built years earlier in this curriculum for unrelated reasons
   (Lesson 6).

Read that chain backwards and it is one long consequence of a single
commutator. Nothing physical was added after step 1 except the
single-valuedness requirement in step 3, and that requirement is
geometric rather than dynamical.

### What the Hydrogen Atom course reuses directly

A hydrogen atom's Hamiltonian, $H=\frac{p^2}{2m}-\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}$,
depends only on the *distance* $r$ from the nucleus, not the direction:
a **central potential**. For any central potential, $\vec L^{\,2}$ and
$L_z$ both commute with $H$ (a fact the next course proves, building on
this course's operators), so energy eigenstates can be chosen as
simultaneous eigenstates of $H$, $\vec L^{\,2}$, and $L_z$. The angular
part of every hydrogen wavefunction is therefore one of this course's
$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ functions, unchanged, with only a
still-to-be-found radial function $R(r)$ in front of it:

$$
\psi_{n,l,m}(r,\theta,\varphi) = R_{n,l}(r)\,Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)
$$

That is why this course had to come first. The angular half of the
hydrogen problem is finished, by Lesson 3, before the next course writes
down a radial equation.

The payoff is not only qualitative. Every $m$ at a given $l$ shares the
same $\vec L^{\,2}$ <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $l(l+1)\hbar^2$, and $H$ for a central
potential sees $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $r$ but never $L_z$, so those $2l+1$
states must be degenerate. Counting them gives the size of each hydrogen
shell without solving anything:

The degeneracy counts are $1, 4, 9, 16$: the sum $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)$
evaluates to $n^2$, and each $2l+1$ in that sum is the dimension of an
angular momentum multiplet from Lesson 2. This course supplies every
number in the labels. The next course supplies only the *positions* of
the lines, through $E_n$, which is where the radial equation earns its
keep.

One honest caveat: the $n^2$ counting above ignores electron spin, which
Lesson 4 showed doubles every count to $2n^2$. That factor of two is what
makes the periodic table's rows come out $2, 8, 8, 18$ rather than
$1, 4, 4, 9$.

## Physical Interpretation

Splitting a three-dimensional problem into radial and angular pieces is
not a trick that happens to work for hydrogen. It follows from the
potential depending on $r$ alone, which makes $\vec L^{\,2}$, $L_z$ and
$H$ commute and therefore share an eigenbasis. Any $V(r)$ gets the same
treatment: the three-dimensional oscillator, a nucleus modelled as a
uniform sphere, a quantum dot with rotational symmetry.

What hydrogen adds is an accident. Its energies depend only on $n$ and
not on $l$, so the $2l+1$ states of different $l$ within one shell are
degenerate too, which is a stronger statement than anything this course
proved. That extra degeneracy is specific to the $1/r$ potential and
breaks the moment the potential deviates from it, which is exactly what
happens in every atom heavier than hydrogen.

## Worked Example

**Given that hydrogen's ground state has $l=0$, predict which spherical
harmonic appears in its wavefunction, without solving any radial
equation.**

$l=0$ allows exactly one $m$ value, $m=0$, from Lesson 2's $2l+1$ count.
The ground state's angular part is therefore $Y_0^0=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}$,
a constant. So the hydrogen ground state has **no angular dependence at
all**: it is spherically symmetric, and that was settled without touching
a radial equation.

The prediction is testable in the ordinary way. A spherically symmetric
ground state has no electric dipole moment and no preferred axis, so
hydrogen in its ground state cannot be oriented by a weak field. The next
course reaches the same conclusion from the Schrödinger equation.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming this course's results only apply to hydrogen specifically.**
The angular momentum algebra applies to *any* central potential (a
uniform sphere, a 3D <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>, any $V(r)$); hydrogen is the
next course's specific example, not the scope of what this course
proved.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the radial equation still needs to determine the angular
shape.** It doesn't: Lesson 3 already fully determined
$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$; the radial equation's only job is finding
$R_{n,l}(r)$ and the resulting energy levels.
</Callout>

## Summary

- This course's six lessons form one continuous derivation: commutation
  relations → quantized spectrum → explicit spherical harmonics → the
  spin-$\tfrac12$ special case → its experimental confirmation → two-spin
  combination.
- Central potentials (hydrogen included) separate into radial ×
  angular parts, with the angular part being exactly this course's
  $Y_l^m$ functions, no new derivation needed for that piece.
- The $n^2$ degeneracy of each hydrogen shell is $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)$,
  built entirely from multiplet dimensions this course derived, and it
  doubles to $2n^2$ once spin is included.
- The singlet/Bell-state identity from Lesson 6 connects this course to
  Quantum Gates & Circuits by an equality between vectors, not by
  resemblance.
