<LessonHook eyebrow="Why half-integers are allowed at all">
The <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>'s entire energy spectrum came from ladder operators and one commutation relation, with no calculus and no wavefunctions. Angular momentum yields to the same strategy, and answers a question the oscillator never raised: why half-integers are allowed at all.
</LessonHook>

## The oscillator's trick, aimed somewhere new

The oscillator's spectrum fell out, back in From Classical to Quantum,
from the ladder operators $a,a^\dagger$ and the single relation
$[a,a^\dagger]=1$. Here the ladder operators are built from $J_x$ and
$J_y$ instead.

The two cases differ in one respect that turns out to matter. The
oscillator's ladder had a floor and no ceiling, so its spectrum ran
$0,1,2,\dots$ upward forever. Angular momentum's ladder is bounded at
*both* ends, and being trapped between two walls while stepping in fixed
units is a much stronger constraint. It is what produces the
half-integers.

## Building the rungs, then finding both ends

### Building the ladder operators

Define $J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y$. From last lesson's <Term id="commutator">commutators</Term>:

$$
[J_z,J_\pm] = [J_z,J_x]\pm i[J_z,J_y] = i\hbar J_y \pm i(-i\hbar J_x) = \pm\hbar(J_x\pm iJ_y) = \pm\hbar J_\pm
$$

### J± raises/lowers Jz's eigenvalue by ħ

Suppose $J_z|j,m\rangle=m\hbar|j,m\rangle$ for some eigenstate. Then:

$$
J_z(J_\pm|j,m\rangle) = (J_\pm J_z + [J_z,J_\pm])|j,m\rangle = J_\pm(m\hbar)|j,m\rangle \pm\hbar J_\pm|j,m\rangle = (m\pm1)\hbar\,(J_\pm|j,m\rangle)
$$

So $J_\pm|j,m\rangle$ is itself a $J_z$ eigenstate, with eigenvalue raised
or lowered by exactly $\hbar$. This is the harmonic oscillator's
$a^\dagger|n\rangle\propto|n+1\rangle$ argument with $J_z$ in the role
$\hat N$ played there.

One more operator earns its keep before the main argument. $\vec J^{\,2}
= J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2$ commutes with all three components (check it on
$J_z$: the $J_x^2$ and $J_y^2$ terms produce cancelling contributions),
so it commutes with $J_\pm$ too. Climbing the ladder therefore never
changes $\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue: the whole ladder sits at one fixed
total, which is why a single label $j$ can name all of it.

### Boundedness forces a top and bottom rung

$\vec J^{\,2}-J_z^2=J_x^2+J_y^2$ is a sum of squares of <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term>
operators, so its expectation value in *any* state is $\geq0$. Writing
$\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue as $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ (a labeling convention,
not yet a constraint) on a state with $J_z$ eigenvalue $m\hbar$:

$$
j(j+1)\hbar^2 - m^2\hbar^2 \geq 0 \implies m^2 \leq j(j+1)
$$

$m$ is bounded above and below. Since $J_+$ raises $m$ by $1$ every time
it is applied, the only way to respect that bound is for the climb to
stop: some **top rung** $m_{\max}$ must satisfy $J_+|m_{\max}\rangle=0$,
and by the mirror argument some **bottom rung** $m_{\min}$ must satisfy
$J_-|m_{\min}\rangle=0$.

### Why the top rung is at exactly $m=j$

So far $j$ was only a way of writing $\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue. The
following identity is what ties it to the ladder's actual endpoints.
Expand $J_-J_+$ using $J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y$ and $[J_x,J_y]=i\hbar J_z$:

$$
J_-J_+ = J_x^2+J_y^2 + i[J_x,J_y] = \vec J^{\,2} - J_z^2 - \hbar J_z
$$

Apply both sides to the top rung, where the left side gives zero:

$$
0 = \left(\vec J^{\,2} - m_{\max}^2\hbar^2 - m_{\max}\hbar^2\right)|m_{\max}\rangle
$$

so $\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue is $m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)\hbar^2$. Comparing
with the label $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ identifies $j=m_{\max}$: the convention was
chosen precisely so that $j$ names the highest rung. Running the same
argument with $J_+J_- = \vec J^{\,2}-J_z^2+\hbar J_z$ on the bottom rung
gives $m_{\min}(m_{\min}-1)=j(j+1)$. That quadratic has two roots,
$m_{\min}=-j$ and $m_{\min}=j+1$; the second is discarded because the
bottom rung cannot sit above the top rung $m_{\max}=j$, leaving
$m_{\min}=-j$.

Everything needed is now on the table: a bottom rung at $-j$, a top rung
at $+j$, and steps of exactly $1$ between them. That alone pins down which
values of $j$ are possible, and it is worth committing to an answer before
reading the next line.

The ladder therefore runs from $-j$ to $+j$ in steps of $1$, so the total
climb $2j$ is a **non-negative integer**.

$$
\boxed{j = 0,\ \tfrac12,\ 1,\ \tfrac32,\ 2,\ \dots}
$$

This is the integer-or-half-integer spectrum angular momentum is known
for, and it arrived without a single wavefunction. Note what did the
work: the ladder steps by $1$ and is pinned at both ends, so $2j$ counts
the rungs and has to be a whole number. Nothing forbids $2j$ from being
odd, which is the entire origin of half-integer spin.

top state ($m=j$, index 0 in this basis convention) it returns the zero

## No rotation entered this argument anywhere

Nothing about space or rotation entered this derivation. It followed from
the commutation algebra $[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$ and nothing
else, which is why it applies unchanged to spin, a quantity with no
classical rotational picture behind it at all. Spin-$\tfrac12$ is not an
extra postulate bolted on: $j=\tfrac12$ is the smallest nontrivial
solution the algebra already had.

The next lesson supplies the other half of the story. Orbital angular
momentum, which does come from motion through space, turns out to admit
only the *integer* solutions, and the half-integers are left over for
something that is not motion.

## Counting the m values at j = 1

**For $j=1$, list every allowed value of $m$ and confirm there are
exactly $2j+1=3$ of them.**

Starting at the top rung $m=j=1$ and stepping down by $1$ until reaching
the bottom rung $m=-j=-1$ gives $m=1,0,-1$: three values, matching
$2j+1=3$. The platform's `angularMomentumZ(1)` is a $3\times3$ matrix
with exactly those three numbers on its diagonal.

Total angular momentum for this state is $\sqrt{j(j+1)}\,\hbar =
\sqrt2\,\hbar \approx 1.41\hbar$, while the largest projection available
is $1\hbar$. The vector can never lie along $z$, however you prepare it.

Drawn as a ladder, each rung is one $m$ value, spaced by $\hbar$, with
$J_+$ climbing and $J_-$ descending until each runs out of rungs:

## Which j a system has is a separate question

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming $j$ can be any half-integer independently of the specific
system.** The *set* of allowed $j$ values is $0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$
in general, but which $j$ a specific physical system actually has (spin-
$\tfrac12$ for an electron's intrinsic spin, integer $l$ for orbital
angular momentum specifically, shown next lesson) is additional physical
input, not derivable from the commutation algebra alone.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing $\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue $j(j+1)\hbar^2$ with $(j\hbar)^2$.**
They're different numbers for any $j>0$: $j(j+1)>j^2$ always, reflecting
that $\vec J^{\,2}$ includes contributions from $J_x^2+J_y^2$, not just
$J_z^2$'s own eigenvalue squared.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $j=3/2$, list all four allowed values of $m$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $m=\tfrac32,\ \tfrac12,\ -\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac32$.

   Start at the top rung $m=j=\tfrac32$ and step down by $1$ until the
   bottom rung $m=-j=-\tfrac32$. The count is $2j+1=4$, matching the
   $4\times4$ matrices `angularMomentumZ(1.5)` builds.

   Worth noticing: there is no $m=0$ on this ladder, and there never is
   for half-integer $j$. Every $m$ differs from $j$ by a whole number, so
   a half-integer $j$ gives half-integer $m$ throughout. A spin-$\tfrac32$
   nucleus therefore cannot have zero angular momentum projection along
   any axis you choose to measure, while an $l=1$ orbital state can. That
   difference has experimental consequences, and it is the reason
   half-integer and integer systems behave differently in a magnetic
   field rather than just carrying different labels.

   </details>

2. Confirm $j(j+1)>j^2$ for $j=1$ specifically, and explain what physical
   quantity the difference, $j(j+1)-j^2=j$, represents (Hint: relate it
   to $J_x^2+J_y^2$'s expectation value on the top state $|j,j\rangle$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At $j=1$, $j(j+1)=2$ and $j^2=1$, so the gap is $1$, and in general
   $j(j+1)-j^2=j$. That gap is $\langle J_x^2+J_y^2\rangle/\hbar^2$ on the
   top rung: the transverse angular momentum that survives even when the
   state is as aligned with $z$ as the algebra permits.

   On $|j,j\rangle$, both $\vec J^{\,2}$ and $J_z$ act as numbers, so

   $$
   \langle J_x^2+J_y^2\rangle = \langle \vec J^{\,2}\rangle - \langle J_z^2\rangle = j(j+1)\hbar^2 - j^2\hbar^2 = j\hbar^2
   $$

   For $j=1$ that is $\hbar^2$, nonzero on the very state that is supposed
   to be "pointing along $z$." Geometrically it is the same statement the
   lesson made with lengths: the vector's length is $\sqrt{2}\,\hbar$ and
   its longest available projection is $\hbar$, so
   $\sqrt{2-1}\,\hbar=\hbar$ of it is always left over sideways.

   The cross-check that makes this more than an inequality. Symmetry
   between $x$ and $y$ on $|j,j\rangle$ gives
   $\langle J_x^2\rangle=\langle J_y^2\rangle=\tfrac{j\hbar^2}{2}$, and
   $\langle J_x\rangle=\langle J_y\rangle=0$ there (both $J_x$ and $J_y$
   are built from $J_\pm$, which move the state to a different rung,
   orthogonal to the one you started on). So
   $\Delta J_x=\Delta J_y=\hbar\sqrt{j/2}$ and

   $$
   \Delta J_x\,\Delta J_y = \frac{j\hbar^2}{2} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\langle J_z\rangle\right|
   $$

   The top rung saturates the uncertainty relation from Lesson 1 exactly.
   The leftover transverse piece is not slack in the bound. It is the
   minimum the bound allows, which is why no state can do better.

   The misconception to name: reading $j\hbar^2$ as the vector being
   *tilted* off the $z$ axis by some definite amount, so that a classical
   cone picture with a definite tilt angle would work. It is not a tilt.
   The first moments $\langle J_x\rangle$ and $\langle J_y\rangle$ both
   vanish, so there is no direction the transverse part points. What is
   nonzero is the second moment, the spread. The cone picture is a
   drawing of a variance, and treating it as a real orientation is what
   leads people to expect $\vec J^{\,2}=(j\hbar)^2$ in the first place.

   </details>

3. Using $[J_z,J_+]=\hbar J_+$ directly (not the general argument),
   verify that if $J_z|j,m\rangle=m\hbar|j,m\rangle$ then
   $J_z(J_+|j,m\rangle)=(m+1)\hbar(J_+|j,m\rangle)$, showing every step
   of the algebra explicitly.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The whole move is to trade $J_zJ_+$ for $J_+J_z$ plus a correction, so
   that $J_z$ ends up next to the state whose $J_z$ eigenvalue you know.

   Rearranging the definition of the commutator, $[J_z,J_+]=J_zJ_+-J_+J_z$,
   gives $J_zJ_+=J_+J_z+[J_z,J_+]=J_+J_z+\hbar J_+$. Now act on
   $|j,m\rangle$:

   $$
   J_z\left(J_+|j,m\rangle\right) = \left(J_+J_z + \hbar J_+\right)|j,m\rangle = J_+\left(J_z|j,m\rangle\right) + \hbar J_+|j,m\rangle
   $$

   The inner $J_z|j,m\rangle$ is $m\hbar|j,m\rangle$ by hypothesis, and
   $m\hbar$ is a number, so it slides out through $J_+$ by linearity:

   $$
   = J_+\left(m\hbar|j,m\rangle\right) + \hbar J_+|j,m\rangle = m\hbar\left(J_+|j,m\rangle\right) + \hbar\left(J_+|j,m\rangle\right) = (m+1)\hbar\left(J_+|j,m\rangle\right)
   $$

   Notice why the commutator was needed at all. $J_+|j,m\rangle$ is an
   unknown vector, so $J_z$ acting on it cannot be evaluated. The only
   thing in the problem with a known action is $J_z$ acting on
   $|j,m\rangle$, and the commutator is the licence to move $J_z$ past
   $J_+$ to reach it. The price of moving it is the $\hbar J_+$ term, and
   that price is precisely the step of the ladder.

   One caveat that matters more than it looks, and that the next question
   turns on. This shows $J_+|j,m\rangle$ *satisfies* the eigenvalue
   equation for $(m+1)\hbar$. It does not show $J_+|j,m\rangle$ is an
   eigenvector, because the zero vector satisfies every eigenvalue
   equation and is excluded from being an eigenvector by definition. So
   the correct conclusion is: either a rung at $m+1$ exists, or
   $J_+|j,m\rangle=0$. That escape hatch is what lets the ladder end.

   </details>

4. Why does the boundedness argument need *both* a top rung and a bottom
   rung to conclude $2j$ is an integer? What would go wrong with only one
   of the two?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $2j$ is the *distance between the two ends*, measured in unit
   steps. One end alone gives you nothing to measure against.

   Spell out what each end contributes. The top rung sets
   $\vec J^{\,2}$'s eigenvalue to $m_{\max}(m_{\max}+1)\hbar^2$, which is
   only the statement that $j$ is defined as $m_{\max}$. Nothing there
   restricts $j$. Take $j=0.317$ and a ladder running downward forever:
   $0.317,\ -0.683,\ -1.683,\dots$. Every one of those satisfies the
   raising-operator algebra of Question 3, which is the only thing the top
   rung constrains. What is wrong is the sequence as a whole: run it far
   enough and $m^2$ passes $j(j+1)$, violating the boundedness bound. The
   top rung alone never tells you that; it only tells you where the ladder
   starts.

   The bottom rung is where it becomes visible. Descending from $j$ in
   unit steps produces $j-n$ for $n=0,1,2,\dots$, and the ladder must
   terminate at $-j$, so

   $$
   -j = j - n \implies n = 2j
   $$

   with $n$ a non-negative whole number by construction, since it counts
   how many times $J_-$ was applied. That is the entire quantization. It
   is a closure condition: the descent starting at $+j$ has to land on
   $-j$ exactly, never overshoot it. In the $j=0.317$ example the descent
   passes $-0.317$ without stopping (the nearest rungs are $0.317$ and
   $-0.683$), so the ladder never closes and no such $j$ exists.

   With only a bottom rung the picture mirrors: the ladder climbs forever
   and $m^2\le j(j+1)$ is violated above instead of below.

   The contrast with the harmonic oscillator is the point of the whole
   argument. That ladder has a floor and no ceiling, so the closure
   condition never applies, and its quantum number is fixed instead by
   pinning the floor itself to a specific value through
   $a|0\rangle=0$. Angular momentum pins neither end to a specific
   number. Both ends are set by the same unknown $j$, and quantization
   comes from the two ends having to be consistent with each other.

   The subtle wrong turn worth naming: imagining the ladder "stops"
   because the next rung would violate $m^2\le j(j+1)$, as though the
   bound acts as a wall. It does not work that way. If $J_+|j,j\rangle$
   were any nonzero vector, it would be a genuine eigenstate with
   $m=j+1$, and the bound would simply be false. The only consistent exit
   is $J_+|j,j\rangle=0$ exactly, which the interactive above confirms
   against the real matrices: the top bar reads zero, not small.

   </details>

## Summary

- $J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y$ satisfy $[J_z,J_\pm]=\pm\hbar J_\pm$, making them
  raise/lower $J_z$'s eigenvalue by exactly $\hbar$, the same strategy as
  the harmonic oscillator's ladder operators.
- Boundedness ($J_x^2+J_y^2\geq0$) forces a top and bottom rung, and the
  integer-step spacing between them forces $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$,
  derived, not assumed.
- Verified directly: `angularMomentumRaising`/`Lowering` annihilate the
  predicted top/bottom states exactly, for every $j$ tested.
