<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Solve the angular momentum algebra abstractly and nothing forbids half-integer values. Ask the same algebra to describe a particle actually orbiting in space, and half of the previously allowed values quietly vanish: only integer l survives.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

$j=\tfrac12$ is a perfectly good
representation of the commutation relations, and electron spin uses
exactly that one — but a particle actually
orbiting in space, position, angle, a real <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term>
$\psi(\theta,\varphi)$, is different. The reason has nothing to do with new
physics being layered on top. It's a single, almost embarrassingly simple
geometric fact about what a wavefunction is allowed to do when you walk
all the way around a circle and arrive back where you started. This
lesson derives exactly why, then builds the explicit position-space
wavefunctions, $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$, that the integer restriction makes
possible.

## Intuition

Walk one full lap around the $z$-axis and you end up at exactly the same
physical point you started from, so any actual function
of position has to return to the same value it started with. The
candidate function $e^{im\varphi}$ only manages that if $m$ is a whole
number: with a half-integer $m$, one full lap leaves the function
pointing the opposite way in the complex plane, disagreeing with itself
about what value it's supposed to take at a point it never actually left.
That flat contradiction, not any additional physical law, is the entire
content of the derivation below.

## Mathematical Development

### Why orbital l must be an integer

In position space, $L_z=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\varphi$ (the angular
momentum operator generating rotations about the $z$-axis, acting on the
azimuthal angle $\varphi$ specifically). Its eigenfunctions are
$\Phi_m(\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}$. Direct substitution gives
$-i\hbar\,\partial_\varphi e^{im\varphi}=-i\hbar(im)e^{im\varphi}=m\hbar\,e^{im\varphi}$,
confirming <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $m\hbar$.

But $\varphi$ and $\varphi+2\pi$ describe the *same physical point* in
space, and a wavefunction must be **single-valued**:
$\Phi_m(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi_m(\varphi)$. This requires
$e^{im(\varphi+2\pi)}=e^{im\varphi}$, i.e. $e^{im2\pi}=1$, which holds
**only for integer $m$**. A half-integer $m$ (like $m=\tfrac12$) would
give $e^{i\pi}=-1\neq1$, violating single-valuedness outright. Since $m$
ranges over $-l,\dots,l$ in integer steps (last lesson), $m$ integer for
every value forces $l$ itself to be an integer.

Watch this happen directly: the marker below sits at angle $m\varphi$ on
the unit circle and traces $e^{im\varphi}$ as $\varphi$ is scrubbed from
$0$ to $2\pi$, the exact lap around the $z$-axis the argument above
describes. Toggle $m$ between the integer and half-integer presets, drag
the slider all the way to $\varphi=2\pi$, and read off where the marker
actually lands.

<PhaseWindingCircle ariaLabel="Marker at angle m times phi on the unit circle, tracing e^(i m phi) as phi sweeps from 0 to 2 pi" />

For integer $m$ the marker lands back exactly on the start point: the
same physical point in space, the same wavefunction value, no
contradiction. For half-integer $m$ it lands on the exact opposite point
of the circle, $e^{i\pi}=-1$, which is a different value at a point the
wavefunction never actually left. That's the whole contradiction,
visible directly rather than only algebraically.

### Why this argument doesn't apply to spin

Spin has no position-space wavefunction to begin with. There's no
$\varphi$-dependence to demand single-valuedness of, since spin isn't a
function of position at all. This is exactly why half-integer $j$ (like
electron spin, $j=\tfrac12$) is mathematically allowed by the commutation
algebra and physically realized for spin, while being *excluded* for
orbital motion specifically by this single-valuedness argument.

### The spherical harmonics

$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec
L^{\,2}$ (eigenvalue $l(l+1)\hbar^2$) and $L_z$ (eigenvalue $m\hbar$),
in position space. For $l=0,1$:

$$
Y_0^0=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi},\qquad Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta,\qquad Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\varphi}
$$

with the analogous, more involved $l=2$ set implemented in
`sphericalHarmonics.ts`. Both **normalization**
($\int|Y_l^m|^2\,d\Omega=1$) and **orthogonality** (different $(l,m)$
pairs integrate to exactly $0$) are checked directly by numerical
integration over the sphere ($d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$), not
assumed from the closed-form coefficients alone. Every one of the nine
$Y_l^m$ for $l=0,1,2$ normalizes to $1.000$ (to the numerical grid's
precision), and every distinct pair tested is orthogonal to
$10^{-6}$ or better.

## Physical Interpretation

$Y_0^0$ is spherically symmetric (an $s$-orbital's angular shape,
central to the next course's hydrogen atom); $Y_1^0$ is largest along the
$z$-axis and vanishes at the equator (a $p_z$-orbital's characteristic
two-lobed shape); $Y_1^{\pm1}$ peak at the equator. These aren't
arbitrary functions. They're the *only* functions consistent with being
simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$/$L_z$ eigenstates, which is exactly why atomic
orbitals have the specific shapes they do.

## Worked Example

**Confirm $Y_1^0$'s normalization by hand, using
$\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac23$ and
$\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=2\pi$.**

$$
\int|Y_1^0|^2\,d\Omega = \frac{3}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3}{4\pi}(2\pi)\left(\frac23\right) = \frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3} = 1
$$

exactly $1$, confirming the coefficient $\sqrt{3/4\pi}$ was chosen
correctly, matching `sphericalHarmonicNormSquared({l:1,m:0})`'s direct
numerical output of $\approx1.0000308$ (the small residual is the grid
integration's own discretization error, not a flaw in the formula).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming half-integer l is simply "not yet observed" rather than
mathematically excluded.** It's excluded outright by single-valuedness.
A half-integer-$l$ position-space wavefunction would be multi-valued —
not merely unusual, but not a valid wavefunction at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating spherical harmonics as arbitrary basis functions chosen for
convenience.** They're the *unique* (up to normalization phase
convention) simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$. Any
other choice would fail to diagonalize one or both operators.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Single-valuedness of a position-space wavefunction under
  $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$ forces orbital angular momentum's $m$ (and
  hence $l$) to be an integer. Spin has no such restriction, since it
  has no position-space wavefunction to be single-valued.
- $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$/$L_z$
  eigenfunctions; explicit formulas for $l=0,1,2$ are implemented and
  checked directly for normalization and orthogonality by numerical
  integration, not assumed.
- The specific shapes of $s$, $p$, $d$ orbitals in the next course follow
  directly from these functions.
