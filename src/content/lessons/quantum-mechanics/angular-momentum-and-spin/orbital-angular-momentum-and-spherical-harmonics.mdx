<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Solve the angular momentum algebra abstractly and nothing forbids half-integer values. Ask the same algebra to describe a particle actually orbiting in space, and half of the previously allowed values quietly vanish: only integer l survives.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

The previous lesson's algebra permitted $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$,
and it meant it: $j=\tfrac12$ is a perfectly good representation of the
commutation relations, and electron spin is built on that one.

Orbital angular momentum is different, and not because extra physics gets
layered on. The difference is that orbital angular momentum comes with a
position-space <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term> $\psi(\theta,\varphi)$ attached, and a
function of position has an obligation the abstract algebra never
imposed: it has to agree with itself. Walk a full circle around the
$z$-axis and you are standing where you started, so the function has to
return the value it returned before.

That one requirement cuts the allowed list in half. This lesson shows
how, then builds the surviving position-space wavefunctions,
$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$.

## Intuition

The candidate function is $e^{im\varphi}$, and a full lap multiplies it by
$e^{2\pi i m}$. For whole-number $m$ that factor is $1$ and the function
is back where it started. For half-integer $m$ the factor is $-1$: the
function has come back pointing the opposite way in the complex plane,
disagreeing with itself about the value it takes at a point it never
left.

No additional physical law is needed to rule that out. It is a
contradiction on its own terms, and the derivation below is that
observation written carefully.

## Mathematical Development

### Why orbital l must be an integer

In position space, $L_z=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\varphi$ (the angular
momentum operator generating rotations about the $z$-axis, acting on the
azimuthal angle $\varphi$ specifically). Its eigenfunctions are
$\Phi_m(\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}$. Direct substitution gives
$-i\hbar\,\partial_\varphi e^{im\varphi}=-i\hbar(im)e^{im\varphi}=m\hbar\,e^{im\varphi}$,
confirming <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $m\hbar$.

But $\varphi$ and $\varphi+2\pi$ describe the *same physical point* in
space, and a wavefunction must be **single-valued**:
$\Phi_m(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi_m(\varphi)$. This requires
$e^{im(\varphi+2\pi)}=e^{im\varphi}$, i.e. $e^{im2\pi}=1$, which holds
**only for integer $m$**. A half-integer $m$ (like $m=\tfrac12$) would
give $e^{i\pi}=-1\neq1$, violating single-valuedness outright. Since $m$
ranges over $-l,\dots,l$ in integer steps (last lesson), $m$ integer for
every value forces $l$ itself to be an integer.

Watch this happen directly: the marker below sits at angle $m\varphi$ on
the unit circle and traces $e^{im\varphi}$ as $\varphi$ is scrubbed from
$0$ to $2\pi$, the exact lap around the $z$-axis the argument above
describes. Toggle $m$ between the integer and half-integer presets, drag
the slider all the way to $\varphi=2\pi$, and read off where the marker
actually lands.

<PhaseWindingCircle ariaLabel="Marker at angle m times phi on the unit circle, tracing e^(i m phi) as phi sweeps from 0 to 2 pi" />

For integer $m$ the marker returns to the start point: same physical
location, same wavefunction value. For half-integer $m$ it lands
diametrically opposite, at $e^{i\pi}=-1$, assigning a different value to a
point the wavefunction never left. The algebra and the picture say the
same thing, and the picture is harder to argue with.

### Why this argument doesn't apply to spin

Spin has no position-space wavefunction to begin with. There is no
$\varphi$-dependence to demand single-valuedness of, because spin is not
a function of position at all. So half-integer $j$ stays available, and
nature uses it: the electron's intrinsic spin is $j=\tfrac12$, a value
the orbital argument has just ruled out for motion through space.

<Callout type="note">
The single-valuedness argument given here is the standard one and gets
the right answer, but it is a heuristic rather than the last word: the
careful statement is that $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, acting on
square-integrable functions on three-dimensional space, admits only
integer $l$. Nothing below depends on which version you prefer, and the
intuition the phase-winding picture gives is the one worth carrying.
</Callout>

### The spherical harmonics

$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec
L^{\,2}$ (eigenvalue $l(l+1)\hbar^2$) and $L_z$ (eigenvalue $m\hbar$),
in position space. For $l=0,1$:

$$
Y_0^0=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi},\qquad Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta,\qquad Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\varphi}
$$

with the analogous, more involved $l=2$ set implemented in
`sphericalHarmonics.ts`. Both **normalization**
($\int|Y_l^m|^2\,d\Omega=1$) and **orthogonality** (different $(l,m)$
pairs integrate to $0$) are checked by numerical integration over the
sphere ($d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$) rather than taken from
the closed-form coefficients. All nine $Y_l^m$ for $l=0,1,2$ normalize to
$1.000$ within the grid's precision, and every distinct pair tested is
orthogonal to $10^{-6}$ or better.

The orthogonality is not a lucky property of these particular functions.
$\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$ are Hermitian, and eigenfunctions of a Hermitian
operator with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, as Operators,
Observables & Measurement proved. The numerical check confirms the
implementation, not the theorem.

## Physical Interpretation

$Y_0^0$ is spherically symmetric (an $s$-orbital's angular shape,
central to the next course's hydrogen atom); $Y_1^0$ is largest along the
$z$-axis and vanishes at the equator (a $p_z$ orbital's two-lobed shape);
$Y_1^{\pm1}$ peak at the equator instead. These are not a convenient
choice of basis functions. They are the only functions that are
simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$ eigenstates, which is why atomic
orbitals come in these shapes and not others, in every atom, without
anyone choosing.

angular momentum.

## Worked Example

**Confirm $Y_1^0$'s normalization by hand, using
$\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac23$ and
$\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=2\pi$.**

$$
\int|Y_1^0|^2\,d\Omega = \frac{3}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3}{4\pi}(2\pi)\left(\frac23\right) = \frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3} = 1
$$

The answer is $1$, so the coefficient $\sqrt{3/4\pi}$ was the right
choice. The platform's `sphericalHarmonicNormSquared({l:1,m:0})` returns
$\approx1.0000308$; the residual is the integration grid's discretization
error, and it shrinks with a finer grid rather than converging to
something other than $1$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming half-integer $l$ is merely "not yet observed" rather than
excluded.** A half-integer-$l$ position-space wavefunction would be
multi-valued, which does not make it unusual; it makes it not a function.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating spherical harmonics as arbitrary basis functions chosen for
convenience.** They're the *unique* (up to normalization phase
convention) simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$. Any
other choice would fail to diagonalize one or both operators.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Single-valuedness of a position-space wavefunction under
  $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$ forces orbital angular momentum's $m$ (and
  hence $l$) to be an integer. Spin has no such restriction, since it
  has no position-space wavefunction to be single-valued.
- $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$/$L_z$
  eigenfunctions; explicit formulas for $l=0,1,2$ are implemented and
  checked directly for normalization and orthogonality by numerical
  integration, not assumed.
- The specific shapes of $s$, $p$, $d$ orbitals in the next course follow
  directly from these functions.
