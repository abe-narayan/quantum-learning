Solve the angular momentum algebra abstractly and nothing forbids half-integer values. Ask the same algebra to describe a particle actually orbiting in space, and half of the previously allowed values quietly vanish: only integer l survives.

## The algebra meant what it said

The previous lesson's algebra permitted $j=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\dots$,
and it meant it: $j=\tfrac12$ is a perfectly good representation of the
commutation relations, and electron spin is built on that one.

Orbital angular momentum is different, and not because extra physics gets
layered on. The difference is that orbital angular momentum comes with a
position-space <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term> $\psi(\theta,\varphi)$ attached, and a
function of position has an obligation the abstract algebra never
imposed: it has to agree with itself. Walk a full circle around the
$z$-axis and you are standing where you started, so the function has to
return the value it returned before.

That one requirement cuts the allowed list in half. This lesson shows
how, then builds the surviving position-space wavefunctions,
$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$.

## One full lap, and where the function lands

The candidate function is $e^{im\varphi}$, and a full lap around the
$z$-axis multiplies it by $e^{2\pi im}$.

For whole-number $m$ that factor is $1$ and the function
is back where it started. For half-integer $m$ the factor is $-1$: the
function has come back pointing the opposite way in the complex plane,
disagreeing with itself about the value it takes at a point it never
left.

No additional physical law is needed to rule that out. It is a
contradiction on its own terms, and the derivation below is that
observation written carefully.

## Single-valuedness, and what survives it

### Why orbital l must be an integer

In position space, $L_z=-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\varphi$ (the angular
momentum operator generating rotations about the $z$-axis, acting on the
azimuthal angle $\varphi$ specifically). Its eigenfunctions are
$\Phi_m(\varphi)=e^{im\varphi}$. Direct substitution gives
$-i\hbar\,\partial_\varphi e^{im\varphi}=-i\hbar(im)e^{im\varphi}=m\hbar\,e^{im\varphi}$,
confirming <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $m\hbar$.

But $\varphi$ and $\varphi+2\pi$ describe the *same physical point* in
space, and a wavefunction must be **single-valued**:
$\Phi_m(\varphi+2\pi)=\Phi_m(\varphi)$. This requires
$e^{im(\varphi+2\pi)}=e^{im\varphi}$, i.e. $e^{im2\pi}=1$, which holds
**only for integer $m$**. A half-integer $m$ (like $m=\tfrac12$) would
give $e^{i\pi}=-1\neq1$, violating single-valuedness outright. Since $m$
ranges over $-l,\dots,l$ in integer steps (last lesson), $m$ integer for
every value forces $l$ itself to be an integer.

Watch this happen directly: the marker below sits at angle $m\varphi$ on
the unit circle and traces $e^{im\varphi}$ as $\varphi$ is scrubbed from
$0$ to $2\pi$, the exact lap around the $z$-axis the argument above
describes. Toggle $m$ between the integer and half-integer presets, drag
the slider all the way to $\varphi=2\pi$, and read off where the marker
actually lands.

For integer $m$ the marker returns to the start point: same physical
location, same wavefunction value. For half-integer $m$ it lands
diametrically opposite, at $e^{i\pi}=-1$, assigning a different value to a
point the wavefunction never left. The algebra and the picture say the
same thing, and the picture is harder to argue with.

### Why this argument doesn't apply to spin

Spin has no position-space wavefunction to begin with. There is no
$\varphi$-dependence to demand single-valuedness of, because spin is not
a function of position at all. So half-integer $j$ stays available, and
nature uses it: the electron's intrinsic spin is $j=\tfrac12$, a value
the orbital argument has just ruled out for motion through space.

<Callout type="note">
The single-valuedness argument given here is the standard one and gets
the right answer, but it is a heuristic rather than the last word: the
careful statement is that $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, acting on
square-integrable functions on three-dimensional space, admits only
integer $l$. Nothing below depends on which version you prefer, and the
intuition the phase-winding picture gives is the one worth carrying.
</Callout>

### The spherical harmonics

$Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec
L^{\,2}$ (eigenvalue $l(l+1)\hbar^2$) and $L_z$ (eigenvalue $m\hbar$),
in position space. For $l=0,1$:

$$
Y_0^0=\frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi},\qquad Y_1^0=\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta,\qquad Y_1^{\pm1}=\mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\varphi}
$$

with the analogous, more involved $l=2$ set implemented in
`sphericalHarmonics.ts`. Both **normalization**
($\int|Y_l^m|^2\,d\Omega=1$) and **orthogonality** (different $(l,m)$
pairs integrate to $0$) are checked by numerical integration over the
sphere ($d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$) rather than taken from
the closed-form coefficients. All nine $Y_l^m$ for $l=0,1,2$ normalize to
$1.000$ within the grid's precision, and every distinct pair tested is
orthogonal to $10^{-6}$ or better.

The orthogonality is not a lucky property of these particular functions.
$\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$ are Hermitian, and eigenfunctions of a Hermitian
operator with distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal, as Operators,
Observables & Measurement proved. The numerical check confirms the
implementation, not the theorem.

## The shapes chemistry calls s, p and d

$Y_0^0$ is spherically symmetric (an $s$-orbital's angular shape,
central to the next course's hydrogen atom); $Y_1^0$ is largest along the
$z$-axis and vanishes at the equator (a $p_z$ orbital's two-lobed shape);
$Y_1^{\pm1}$ peak at the equator instead. These are not a convenient
choice of basis functions. They are the only functions that are
simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$ eigenstates, which is why atomic
orbitals come in these shapes and not others, in every atom, without
anyone choosing.

$\varphi$-dependence lives entirely in the phase $e^{im\varphi}$, which
$|Y_l^m|^2$ discards, and which is exactly the part that carries the
angular momentum.

## Normalizing one spherical harmonic by hand

**Confirm $Y_1^0$'s normalization by hand, using
$\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac23$ and
$\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi=2\pi$.**

$$
\int|Y_1^0|^2\,d\Omega = \frac{3}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\varphi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3}{4\pi}(2\pi)\left(\frac23\right) = \frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3} = 1
$$

The answer is $1$, so the coefficient $\sqrt{3/4\pi}$ was the right
choice. The platform's `sphericalHarmonicNormSquared({l:1,m:0})` returns
$\approx1.0000308$; the residual is the integration grid's discretization
error, and it shrinks with a finer grid rather than converging to
something other than $1$.

## Multi-valued is not a function at all

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming half-integer $l$ is merely "not yet observed" rather than
excluded.** A half-integer-$l$ position-space wavefunction would be
multi-valued, which does not make it unusual; it makes it not a function.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating spherical harmonics as arbitrary basis functions chosen for
convenience.** They're the *unique* (up to normalization phase
convention) simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\vec L^{\,2}$ and $L_z$. Any
other choice would fail to diagonalize one or both operators.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Confirm $e^{im2\pi}=1$ fails for $m=3/2$ specifically, by direct
   substitution.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   e^{i(3/2)(2\pi)} = e^{3\pi i} = e^{2\pi i}e^{\pi i} = (1)(-1) = -1 \neq 1
   $$

   The lap factor is $-1$, so $\Phi_{3/2}(\varphi+2\pi)=-\Phi_{3/2}(\varphi)$
   and the function disagrees with itself at every point.

   What is worth extracting is that the answer is $-1$ and not something
   else. Write the lap factor as $e^{2\pi i m}=e^{i\pi(2m)}$. For any
   half-integer $m$, $2m$ is an odd integer, so the factor is
   $e^{i\pi}=-1$ every time: $m=\tfrac12$, $m=\tfrac32$, $m=\tfrac{101}{2}$
   all fail identically. There is no sense in which $m=\tfrac32$ is "more
   wrong" than $m=\tfrac12$, and no half-integer sneaks through by being
   large. Integer $m$ gives $2m$ even and a factor of $+1$.

   That the failure is a clean sign flip is also why spin can live with
   it. A minus sign on a *whole state vector* is unobservable, so an
   object with no position-space wavefunction to be single-valued is free
   to pick one up under a $2\pi$ rotation. The contradiction here is
   specific to a function of position taking two values at one point.

   </details>

2. Using $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac43$, verify $Y_1^1$'s
   normalization coefficient $\sqrt{3/8\pi}$ by hand (the $\varphi$
   integral of $|e^{i\varphi}|^2=1$ over $[0,2\pi)$ is just $2\pi$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $Y_1^1=-\sqrt{3/8\pi}\,\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}$, the modulus
   squared is $|Y_1^1|^2=\tfrac{3}{8\pi}\sin^2\theta$, since
   $|e^{i\varphi}|^2=1$ and the leading minus sign squares away. The
   measure $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ supplies the third
   power of $\sin\theta$:

   $$
   \int|Y_1^1|^2\,d\Omega = \frac{3}{8\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!d\varphi\int_0^\pi \sin^2\theta\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3}{8\pi}(2\pi)\left(\frac43\right) = \frac{3}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = 1
   $$

   Two things in that calculation are the ones people drop.

   The first is the $\sin\theta$ in the measure. Integrating
   $\sin^2\theta$ instead of $\sin^3\theta$ over $[0,\pi]$ gives
   $\pi/2$, not $4/3$, and the normalization comes out to
   $\tfrac{3}{8\pi}(2\pi)(\pi/2)=3\pi/8$, which is not $1$. The extra
   factor is geometry, not algebra: a band of fixed $d\theta$ near the
   pole covers far less solid angle than one at the equator, and
   $\sin\theta$ is that shrinkage.

   The second is the sign. The $-$ in front of $Y_1^{+1}$ is the
   Condon-Shortley phase convention, fixed so that the ladder operators
   $L_\pm$ connect the $Y_l^m$ with positive coefficients throughout.
   It cannot be detected in $|Y_1^1|^2$ and has no effect here, but it is
   not decoration: drop it and the ladder-operator matrix elements pick up
   sign errors that do show up in superpositions.

   The independent check is the platform's numerical integration over the
   sphere, which returns $1.000$ for this harmonic to grid precision
   without ever using the closed-form coefficient.

   </details>

3. Why must $Y_l^m$ and $Y_l^{m'}$ (same $l$, different $m$) automatically
   be orthogonal, using only the fact that they're eigenfunctions of the
   <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian operator</Term> $L_z$ with different eigenvalues (a fact from
   Operators, Observables & Measurement)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the standard Hermitian-operator argument applies verbatim, with
   $L_z$ as the operator: evaluate $\langle Y_l^m|L_z|Y_l^{m'}\rangle$ two
   ways and the two answers can only agree if the overlap vanishes.

   Let $L_z$ act to the right, then to the left:

   $$
   \langle Y_l^m|L_z|Y_l^{m'}\rangle = m'\hbar\,\langle Y_l^m|Y_l^{m'}\rangle, \qquad \langle Y_l^m|L_z|Y_l^{m'}\rangle = (m\hbar)^*\langle Y_l^m|Y_l^{m'}\rangle = m\hbar\,\langle Y_l^m|Y_l^{m'}\rangle
   $$

   Subtracting, $(m-m')\hbar\,\langle Y_l^m|Y_l^{m'}\rangle=0$, and
   $m\neq m'$ forces the overlap to zero.

   Two hypotheses did real work and both are worth stating out loud,
   because the result is false without either.

   Hermiticity was used twice, not once. It let $L_z$ move onto the bra in
   the second evaluation, and it made the eigenvalue real so that
   $(m\hbar)^*=m\hbar$. If $m$ were complex the subtraction would give
   $(m^*-m')$ and no conclusion follows. Hermiticity is also relative to a
   specific inner product: the one with $d\Omega=\sin\theta\,d\theta\,
   d\varphi$ over the sphere. $L_z$ is not Hermitian under some other
   weighting, and orthogonality would fail there too.

   Distinct eigenvalues did the rest. Nothing here says $Y_l^m$ and
   $Y_{l'}^{m}$ with the same $m$ are orthogonal, and that case needs the
   same argument run with $\vec L^{\,2}$ instead, using $l\ne l'$. Two
   functions sharing both labels are the same function.

   You can see the machinery working in the $\varphi$ integral alone. All
   the $m$-dependence sits in $e^{im\varphi}$, so

   $$
   \int_0^{2\pi} e^{-im\varphi}e^{im'\varphi}\,d\varphi = \int_0^{2\pi} e^{i(m'-m)\varphi}\,d\varphi = 0 \quad\text{for integer } m\neq m'
   $$

   and the $\theta$ integral never has to be looked at. That is the same
   fact in explicit clothing, and notice that it needs $m'-m$ to be a
   nonzero *integer*, which single-valuedness has already guaranteed.

   </details>

4. Explain physically why $|Y_0^0|^2$ being constant (independent of
   $\theta,\varphi$) means an electron in an $l=0$ state has no preferred
   direction at all.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the probability of finding the electron inside a small cone of
   solid angle $d\Omega$ is $|Y_0^0|^2\,d\Omega=\tfrac{1}{4\pi}d\Omega$,
   the same number whichever way the cone points. No direction is
   distinguishable from any other by any measurement of where the electron
   is.

   The stronger statement is available here and is worth having, because
   it is what actually rules out a hidden axis. $Y_0^0=1/(2\sqrt\pi)$ is
   constant as a *function*, not merely constant in modulus, so rotating
   the state about any axis returns the identical function, phase
   included. The state is invariant, not just its density. Compare
   $Y_1^{\pm1}$, whose modulus depends only on $\theta$ and is therefore
   symmetric about the $z$ axis, while the phase $e^{\pm i\varphi}$
   changes under a rotation about $z$. Azimuthal symmetry of the density
   is a much weaker property than rotational invariance of the state, and
   only $l=0$ has the latter.

   Where this connects back to Lesson 1: $l=0$ means $\vec L^{\,2}$ has
   eigenvalue $0$, and since $\vec L^{\,2}=L_x^2+L_y^2+L_z^2$ is a sum of
   non-negative terms, each of $L_x,L_y,L_z$ must be zero on this state.
   The $j=0$ state is the single case where all three components are
   simultaneously sharp, and the noncommutation of $L_x$ and $L_y$
   permits it only because all three sharp values are zero.

   The misconception to avoid: reading isotropy as "the angular momentum
   points somewhere, we just do not know where." For $l=0$ there is no
   angular momentum to have a direction. That reading would be closer to
   right for $l=1,m=0$, where the length is $\sqrt2\,\hbar$ and only the
   transverse orientation is indefinite, but even there the transverse
   part is a spread rather than an unknown arrow.

   A second thing not to conclude: $|Y_0^0|^2$ constant says nothing about
   the radial distribution. A hydrogen $1s$ electron is emphatically not
   equally likely at all distances. Only the angular factor is flat, and
   the radial factor is where all the structure of the next course lives.

   </details>

## Summary

- Single-valuedness of a position-space wavefunction under
  $\varphi\to\varphi+2\pi$ forces orbital angular momentum's $m$ (and
  hence $l$) to be an integer. Spin has no such restriction, since it
  has no position-space wavefunction to be single-valued.
- $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ are the simultaneous $\vec L^{\,2}$/$L_z$
  eigenfunctions; explicit formulas for $l=0,1,2$ are implemented and
  checked directly for normalization and orthogonality by numerical
  integration, not assumed.
- The specific shapes of $s$, $p$, $d$ orbitals in the next course follow
  directly from these functions.
