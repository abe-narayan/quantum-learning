## Motivation

Classically, angular momentum is $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, nothing
quantum about the definition itself. What's genuinely new is what happens
when $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ become operators: $L_x,L_y,L_z$ turn out *not*
to commute with each other, and the specific way they fail to commute is
the algebraic seed this entire course grows from.

Picture it concretely first: a particle at position $\vec r=(2,1)$ in the
$xy$-plane, moving with momentum $\vec p=(0,1.5)$ (straight "up" the
page). The classical cross product $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ points
*out of* the page here (its only nonzero component is $L_z=xp_y-yp_x=(2)(1.5)-(1)(0)=3$),
perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\vec p$, not some blend of them:

    { label: "p", x: 0, y: 1.5, color: "accent" }
  ]}
  ariaLabel="Position vector r=(2,1) and momentum vector p=(0,1.5) in the xy-plane; their cross product L points out of the page with magnitude 3"
/>

$\vec L$ itself lives along the axis perpendicular to this page, which is
exactly why quantum mechanically it takes three separate operators,
$L_x,L_y,L_z$, one per spatial direction, to describe it fully, and why
those three operators' mutual commutation structure (derived below) is
the real content of "angular momentum" once $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ become
operators.

## Mathematical Development

### The operators

$$
L_x=yp_z-zp_y,\qquad L_y=zp_x-xp_z,\qquad L_z=xp_y-yp_x
$$

This is the operator version of $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$'s components,
built from position and momentum operators this platform has used since
Wave Mechanics.

### The one fact everything follows from

Position and momentum operators on the *same* axis satisfy
$[x,p_x]=i\hbar$ (derived in Wave Mechanics); operators on *different*
axes always commute: $[x,y]=0$, $[p_x,p_y]=0$, $[x,p_y]=0$, and so on.
There's nothing coupling independent spatial directions. Computing
$[L_x,L_y]$ means expanding $(yp_z-zp_y)(zp_x-xp_z)-(zp_x-xp_z)(yp_z-zp_y)$
into four cross terms; every term where the only position/momentum pair
appearing together is a *different*-axis pair (like $y$ and $p_x$)
vanishes immediately, since those operators commute and can be freely
reordered to cancel between the two products. The **only** surviving
piece comes from the one term containing $z$ and $p_z$ together, using
$p_zz=zp_z-i\hbar$ to reorder it, and collapses to:

$$
[L_x,L_y] = i\hbar(xp_y-yp_x) = i\hbar L_z
$$

Every other commutator combination follows the same pattern
cyclically ($x\to y\to z\to x$):

$$
\boxed{[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z,\qquad [L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x,\qquad [L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y}
$$

### Verified directly, not just derived

`angularMomentumX`, `angularMomentumY`, `angularMomentumZ` (built from
the ladder-operator matrix elements the next lesson derives, applicable
to any $j$ including $j=1/2,1,3/2,\dots$) satisfy
$[J_x,J_y]-i\hbar J_z=0$ to floating-point precision, checked directly
for $j=1/2,1,3/2,2$, not just the orbital case this lesson derived by
hand, confirming the same algebra holds for the abstract $J$ operators
the rest of this course builds on (with $\hbar=1$ in this platform's
convention).

## Physical Interpretation

$[L_x,L_y]\neq0$ means $L_x$ and $L_y$ **cannot be simultaneously known
with arbitrary precision**, exactly the generalized uncertainty relation
(Operators, Observables & Measurement) applied to angular momentum
specifically. You can know $L_z$ and $\vec L^{\,2}$ (the total magnitude)
simultaneously (shown in the next lesson), but never two different
components of $\vec L$ at once. This is a genuinely new kind of
uncertainty, distinct from position-momentum uncertainty, and it's the
reason a classical picture of "the atom's angular momentum vector points
in a definite direction" breaks down completely at the quantum level.

This same algebra, with spin operators in place of orbital ones
($[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z$), is the physics behind nuclear magnetic
resonance and MRI. A proton's spin, placed in an external magnetic field,
precesses precisely because its spin operators obey this commutation
structure; NMR spectroscopy and clinical MRI both work by driving that
precession with a radiofrequency pulse and then measuring how the signal
relaxes over time, turning the operator algebra derived above into an
image of living tissue.

## Worked Example

**Verify $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$ is consistent with cyclically permuting
$x\to y\to z\to x$ in the boxed result $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$.**

Applying the substitution $x\to y,\ y\to z,\ z\to x$ to
$[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ gives $[L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x$, the second boxed
identity. Applying it again: $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$, the third. The
cyclic pattern reproduces exactly the two "remaining" identities from one
derived case, since the original derivation never used any property
special to the $x,y$ pair specifically (only that $x,y,z$ are mutually
orthogonal axes).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming $L_x$ and $L_y$ commute because they're "just" classical cross
products of commuting quantities.** Classically $L_x,L_y$ are ordinary
numbers and always commute; quantum mechanically they're operators built
from $x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z$, and the one nonzero canonical commutator
$[z,p_z]=i\hbar$ survives the cross-product structure to produce a
genuinely nonzero $[L_x,L_y]$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the cyclic order.** $[L_x,L_y]=+i\hbar L_z$, but
$[L_y,L_x]=-i\hbar L_z$ (antisymmetric, like any commutator). Getting the
index order backward flips the sign.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $L_x,L_y,L_z$, built from position and momentum operators, satisfy
  $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ (and cyclic permutations), derived from the one
  surviving canonical commutator $[z,p_z]=i\hbar$, with every cross-axis
  term vanishing.
- This nonzero commutator means no two different components of $\vec L$
  can be simultaneously known precisely, a genuinely new kind of
  quantum uncertainty.
- The identity is checked directly against this platform's general
  $J_x,J_y,J_z$ matrix operators for several values of $j$, not just
  derived symbolically once.
