## Motivation

Classically, angular momentum is $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$. There is
nothing quantum about that definition, and this lesson does not replace
it. The only change is that $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ stop being numbers,
and everything that follows is the consequence of that single
substitution.

Picture it concretely first: a particle at position $\vec r=(2,1)$ in the
$xy$-plane, moving with momentum $\vec p=(0,1.5)$ (straight "up" the
page). The classical cross product $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ points
*out of* the page here (its only nonzero component is $L_z=xp_y-yp_x=(2)(1.5)-(1)(0)=3$),
perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\vec p$, not some blend of them:

<VectorDiagram
  vectors={[
    { label: "r", x: 2, y: 1, color: "brand" },
    { label: "p", x: 0, y: 1.5, color: "accent" }
  ]}
  ariaLabel="Position vector r=(2,1) and momentum vector p=(0,1.5) in the xy-plane; their cross product L points out of the page with magnitude 3"
/>

$\vec L$ itself lives along the axis perpendicular to this page, which is
why quantum mechanically it takes three separate operators,
$L_x,L_y,L_z$, one per spatial direction, to describe it fully, and why
those three operators' mutual commutation structure (derived below) is
the real content of "angular momentum" once $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ become
operators.

## Mathematical Development

### The operators

$$
L_x=yp_z-zp_y,\qquad L_y=zp_x-xp_z,\qquad L_z=xp_y-yp_x
$$

This is the operator version of $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$'s components,
built from the position and momentum operators in use since
Wave Mechanics.

### The one fact everything follows from

Wave Mechanics lived on a line: one $\hat x$, one $\hat p$, one
commutator. Stepping into 3D triples the cast, three position operators
and three momenta, one pair per axis, and everything about how they
interact fits in a single compact statement:

$$
[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}
$$

Same-axis pairs ($i=j$) reproduce the familiar $[x,p_x]=i\hbar$ derived
in Wave Mechanics. Different-axis pairs ($i\ne j$) commute, and so do
positions with positions and momenta with momenta: $[x,y]=0$,
$[p_x,p_y]=0$, $[x,p_y]=0$, and so on.
Nothing couples independent spatial directions. That single fact does all
the work below.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute the definitions and expand the commutator bilinearly. A commutator is linear in each slot, so a difference of two terms in each slot gives four commutators.">
$$
[L_x,L_y] = [yp_z-zp_y,\ zp_x-xp_z] = [yp_z,zp_x] - [yp_z,xp_z] - [zp_y,zp_x] + [zp_y,xp_z]
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Kill the two middle terms. In [yp_z, xp_z] the operators are y, p_z, x, p_z: every pair is either the same operator or a different-axis pair, so all of them commute and the two products are equal. The same is true of z, p_y, z, p_x in the third term. Neither term contains a matched position-momentum pair, so neither survives.">
$$
[yp_z,xp_z] = 0, \qquad [zp_y,zp_x] = 0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Evaluate the first surviving term. In [y p_z, z p_x], the y and the p_x commute with everything in sight, so they come out of the commutator and leave [p_z, z] = −iħ in the middle.">
$$
[yp_z,zp_x] = y\,[p_z,z]\,p_x = -i\hbar\,yp_x
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Same move on the last term: x and p_y come out, leaving [z, p_z] = +iħ. Note the opposite sign, which is where L_z's minus sign ultimately comes from.">
$$
[zp_y,xp_z] = x\,[z,p_z]\,p_y = i\hbar\,xp_y
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Reassemble. The two surviving pieces are exactly the two terms of L_z, with the right relative sign.">
$$
[L_x,L_y] = -i\hbar\,yp_x + i\hbar\,xp_y = i\hbar(xp_y-yp_x) = i\hbar L_z
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Only one canonical commutator was used, $[z,p_z]=i\hbar$, and it appeared
twice with opposite signs. If $\hbar$ were zero, the right-hand side would
vanish and the three components would commute, which is the classical
case: this identity is the whole quantum content of angular momentum,
measured in units of $\hbar$.

Every other <Term id="commutator">commutator</Term> combination follows the same pattern
cyclically ($x\to y\to z\to x$):

$$
\boxed{[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z,\qquad [L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x,\qquad [L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y}
$$

### Verified directly, not just derived

The derivation above used $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, so strictly it
covers orbital angular momentum only. The rest of this course applies the
same algebra to spin, which has no $\vec r$ and no $\vec p$ at all, so
that extension deserves a check rather than an assurance.

The platform's `angularMomentumX`, `angularMomentumY` and
`angularMomentumZ` build the matrices for any $j$, half-integer included.

puts the result next to $iJ_z$, with $\hbar=1$ in this platform's

## Physical Interpretation

$[L_x,L_y]\neq0$ means $L_x$ and $L_y$ **cannot both be sharp in the same
state**: feed the commutator into the <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">generalized uncertainty relation</Term>
from Operators, Observables & Measurement and you get $\Delta L_x\,\Delta
L_y \ge \tfrac{\hbar}{2}|\langle L_z\rangle|$ directly.

That bound behaves differently from the position-momentum one. Its
right-hand side depends on the state, through $\langle L_z\rangle$, so it
can vanish: in a state with $\langle L_z\rangle = 0$ the inequality
collapses to $\Delta L_x\,\Delta L_y \ge 0$ and constrains nothing. Read
that as a statement about the bound, not a promise about the spreads. The
$|1,0\rangle$ state has $\langle L_z\rangle=0$ and still has
$\Delta L_x=\Delta L_y=\hbar$; the inequality has simply gone silent
rather than reporting that both components are sharp. The $j=0$ state,
with all three components zero at once, is the one state where every
component really is sharp simultaneously.

What survives generally is that $L_z$ and $\vec L^{\,2}$ can be known
together, as the next lesson shows, while two different components
cannot. So the classical image of an angular momentum vector pointing
somewhere definite has to go: the length is knowable and one projection
is knowable, and the direction is not.

The same algebra with spin operators in place of orbital ones,
$[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z$, is what makes nuclear magnetic resonance work. A
proton in a magnetic field has $H \propto S_z$, and because $S_x$ does
not commute with $S_z$, a spin prepared along $x$ cannot stay there: it
precesses. NMR spectroscopy and clinical MRI drive that precession with a
radiofrequency pulse and watch how the signal decays, which is how the
commutator derived above ends up producing an image of soft tissue.

## Worked Example

**Verify $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$ is consistent with cyclically permuting
$x\to y\to z\to x$ in the boxed result $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$.**

Applying the substitution $x\to y,\ y\to z,\ z\to x$ to
$[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ gives $[L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x$, the second boxed
identity. Applying it again gives $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$, the third. One
derivation therefore covers all three, because nothing in it used any
property peculiar to the $x,y$ pair; it used only that $x$, $y$ and $z$
are mutually orthogonal and right-handed. Reverse the handedness of the
coordinate system and every sign in the boxed identities flips.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $L_x$ and $L_y$ commute because they're "just" classical cross
products of commuting quantities.** Classically $L_x,L_y$ are ordinary
numbers and always commute; quantum mechanically they're operators built
from $x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z$, and the one nonzero canonical commutator
$[z,p_z]=i\hbar$ survives the cross-product structure to produce a
nonzero $[L_x,L_y]$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the cyclic order.** $[L_x,L_y]=+i\hbar L_z$, but
$[L_y,L_x]=-i\hbar L_z$ (antisymmetric, like any commutator). Getting the
index order backward flips the sign.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $L_x,L_y,L_z$, built from position and momentum operators, satisfy
  $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ (and cyclic permutations), derived from the one
  surviving canonical commutator $[z,p_z]=i\hbar$, with every cross-axis
  term vanishing.
- This nonzero commutator means no two different components of $\vec L$
  can be simultaneously known precisely, a kind of quantum uncertainty
  distinct from the position-momentum kind.
- The identity is checked directly against this platform's general
  $J_x,J_y,J_z$ matrix operators for several values of $j$, not just
  derived symbolically once.
