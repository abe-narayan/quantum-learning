## Same definition, a different kind of object

Classically, angular momentum is $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$. There is
nothing quantum about that definition, and this lesson does not replace
it. The only change is that $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ stop being numbers,
and everything that follows is the consequence of that single
substitution.

Picture it concretely first: a particle at position $\vec r=(2,1)$ in the
$xy$-plane, moving with momentum $\vec p=(0,1.5)$ (straight "up" the
page). The classical cross product $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ points
*out of* the page here (its only nonzero component is $L_z=xp_y-yp_x=(2)(1.5)-(1)(0)=3$),
perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\vec p$, not some blend of them:

    { label: "p", x: 0, y: 1.5, color: "accent" }
  ]}
  ariaLabel="Position vector r=(2,1) and momentum vector p=(0,1.5) in the xy-plane; their cross product L points out of the page with magnitude 3"
/>

$\vec L$ itself lives along the axis perpendicular to this page, which is
why quantum mechanically it takes three separate operators,
$L_x,L_y,L_z$, one per spatial direction, to describe it fully, and why
those three operators' mutual commutation structure (derived below) is
the real content of "angular momentum" once $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ become
operators.

## One identity, and everything after it

### The operators

$$
L_x=yp_z-zp_y,\qquad L_y=zp_x-xp_z,\qquad L_z=xp_y-yp_x
$$

This is the operator version of $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$'s components,
built from the position and momentum operators in use since
Wave Mechanics.

### The one fact everything follows from

Wave Mechanics lived on a line: one $\hat x$, one $\hat p$, one
commutator. Stepping into 3D triples the cast, three position operators
and three momenta, one pair per axis, and everything about how they
interact fits in a single compact statement:

$$
[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}
$$

Here $\delta_{ij}$, the Kronecker delta, is shorthand for a number that is
$1$ when the two indices match and $0$ when they differ, so the equation
above is really two statements packed into one line. Same-axis pairs
($i=j$) reproduce the familiar $[x,p_x]=i\hbar$ derived
in Wave Mechanics. Different-axis pairs ($i\ne j$) commute, and so do
positions with positions and momenta with momenta: $[x,y]=0$,
$[p_x,p_y]=0$, $[x,p_y]=0$, and so on.
Nothing couples independent spatial directions. That single fact does all
the work below.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute the definitions and expand the commutator bilinearly. A commutator is linear in each slot, so a difference of two terms in each slot gives four commutators.">
$$
[L_x,L_y] = [yp_z-zp_y,\ zp_x-xp_z] = [yp_z,zp_x] - [yp_z,xp_z] - [zp_y,zp_x] + [zp_y,xp_z]
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Kill the two middle terms. In [yp_z, xp_z] the operators are y, p_z, x, p_z: every pair is either the same operator or a different-axis pair, so all of them commute and the two products are equal. The same is true of z, p_y, z, p_x in the third term. Neither term contains a matched position-momentum pair, so neither survives.">
$$
[yp_z,xp_z] = 0, \qquad [zp_y,zp_x] = 0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Evaluate the first surviving term. In [y p_z, z p_x], the y and the p_x commute with everything in sight, so they come out of the commutator and leave [p_z, z] = −iħ in the middle.">
$$
[yp_z,zp_x] = y\,[p_z,z]\,p_x = -i\hbar\,yp_x
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Same move on the last term: x and p_y come out, leaving [z, p_z] = +iħ. Note the opposite sign, which is where L_z's minus sign ultimately comes from.">
$$
[zp_y,xp_z] = x\,[z,p_z]\,p_y = i\hbar\,xp_y
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Reassemble. The two surviving pieces are exactly the two terms of L_z, with the right relative sign.">
$$
[L_x,L_y] = -i\hbar\,yp_x + i\hbar\,xp_y = i\hbar(xp_y-yp_x) = i\hbar L_z
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Only one canonical commutator was used, $[z,p_z]=i\hbar$, and it appeared
twice with opposite signs. If $\hbar$ were zero, the right-hand side would
vanish and the three components would commute, which is the classical
case: this identity is the whole quantum content of angular momentum,
measured in units of $\hbar$.

Every other <Term id="commutator">commutator</Term> combination follows the same pattern
cyclically ($x\to y\to z\to x$):

$$
\boxed{[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z,\qquad [L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x,\qquad [L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y}
$$

### Verified directly, not just derived

The derivation above used $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$, so strictly it
covers orbital angular momentum only. The rest of this course applies the
same algebra to spin, which has no $\vec r$ and no $\vec p$ at all, so
that extension deserves a check rather than an assurance.

## Two components that cannot both be sharp

$[L_x,L_y]\neq0$ means $L_x$ and $L_y$ **cannot both be sharp in the same
state**: feed the commutator into the <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">generalized uncertainty relation</Term>
from Operators, Observables & Measurement and you get $\Delta L_x\,\Delta
L_y \ge \tfrac{\hbar}{2}|\langle L_z\rangle|$ directly.

That bound behaves differently from the position-momentum one. Its
right-hand side depends on the state, through $\langle L_z\rangle$, so it
can vanish: in a state with $\langle L_z\rangle = 0$ the inequality
collapses to $\Delta L_x\,\Delta L_y \ge 0$ and constrains nothing. Read
that as a statement about the bound, not a promise about the spreads. The
$|1,0\rangle$ state has $\langle L_z\rangle=0$ and still has
$\Delta L_x=\Delta L_y=\hbar$; the inequality has simply gone silent
rather than reporting that both components are sharp. The $j=0$ state,
with all three components zero at once, is the one state where every
component really is sharp simultaneously.

What survives generally is that $L_z$ and $\vec L^{\,2}$ can be known
together, as the next lesson shows, while two different components
cannot. So the classical image of an angular momentum vector pointing
somewhere definite has to go: the length is knowable and one projection
is knowable, and the direction is not.

The same algebra with spin operators in place of orbital ones,
$[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z$, is what makes nuclear magnetic resonance work. A
proton in a magnetic field has $H \propto S_z$, and because $S_x$ does
not commute with $S_z$, a spin prepared along $x$ cannot stay there: it
precesses. NMR spectroscopy and clinical MRI drive that precession with a
radiofrequency pulse and watch how the signal decays, which is how the
commutator derived above ends up producing an image of soft tissue.

Since the derivation leaned on the right-handed ordering $x\to y\to z$,
it is fair to ask how much of the result was the physics and how much was
the labelling.

## Checking that the cyclic permutation holds

**Verify $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$ is consistent with cyclically permuting
$x\to y\to z\to x$ in the boxed result $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$.**

Applying the substitution $x\to y,\ y\to z,\ z\to x$ to
$[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ gives $[L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x$, the second boxed
identity. Applying it again gives $[L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$, the third. One
derivation therefore covers all three, because nothing in it used any
property peculiar to the $x,y$ pair; it used only that $x$, $y$ and $z$
are mutually orthogonal and cyclically ordered.

Cyclic permutations are even, which is why they leave every sign alone. An
*odd* relabelling, the prediction above, flips the sign of each operator
and of the commutator together, so the identities come through that too.
What genuinely does flip the sign is reversing the order inside a single
bracket: $[L_y,L_x]=-i\hbar L_z$, since a commutator is antisymmetric.

## Operator cross products are not number cross products

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $L_x$ and $L_y$ commute because they're "just" classical cross
products of commuting quantities.** Classically $L_x,L_y$ are ordinary
numbers and always commute; quantum mechanically they're operators built
from $x,y,z,p_x,p_y,p_z$, and the one nonzero canonical commutator
$[z,p_z]=i\hbar$ survives the cross-product structure to produce a
nonzero $[L_x,L_y]$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the cyclic order.** $[L_x,L_y]=+i\hbar L_z$, but
$[L_y,L_x]=-i\hbar L_z$ (antisymmetric, like any commutator). Getting the
index order backward flips the sign.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the canonical commutators, confirm $[y,p_x]=0$ directly (why
   must this vanish, given $y$ and $p_x$ act on independent directions?).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It vanishes because $\partial/\partial x$ treats $y$ as a constant, so
   $p_x$ has nothing to differentiate when it meets $y$.

   Read it off the compact statement first. With $i=y$ and $j=x$ in
   $[x_i,p_j]=i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$, the Kronecker delta is
   $\delta_{yx}=0$, so the commutator is zero. That is correct but
   circular if $\delta_{ij}$ is what you were trying to understand, so
   evaluate it in the position representation, where $p_x=-i\hbar\,
   \partial/\partial x$ and $y$ acts by multiplication:

   $$
   [y,p_x]\psi = y\left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) - \left(-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(y\psi)\right) = -i\hbar\left(y\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial(y\psi)}{\partial x}\right)
   $$

   The product rule on the second term gives
   $\partial_x(y\psi)=y\,\partial_x\psi+\psi\,\partial_x y$, and
   $\partial_x y = 0$ because $x$ and $y$ are independent coordinates.
   The two terms cancel identically:

   $$
   [y,p_x]\psi = -i\hbar\left(y\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} - y\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right) = 0
   $$

   Compare that with the same-axis case. There
   $\partial_x(x\psi)=x\,\partial_x\psi+\psi$, the leftover $\psi$ is the
   whole content of $[x,p_x]=i\hbar$, and it appears only because the
   coordinate being multiplied is the coordinate being differentiated.

   The misconception this question is aimed at: reading noncommutation as
   a blanket property of "positions and momenta" rather than a property of
   a matched pair. Every mismatched pair commutes, and that is what killed
   the two middle terms of the $[L_x,L_y]$ derivation above. If cross-axis
   pairs did not commute, the four-term expansion would not collapse and
   the angular momentum algebra would not close.

   </details>

2. Write out $L_y=zp_x-xp_z$ and confirm it matches the $y$-component of
   $\vec r\times\vec p$ from the classical cross-product formula.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The cross product's $y$-component is
   $(\vec A\times\vec B)_y=A_zB_x-A_xB_z$, so with $\vec A=\vec r$ and
   $\vec B=\vec p$ it reads $z p_x - x p_z$, which is the stated $L_y$.

   The full set, written so the cyclic structure is visible:

   $$
   (\vec A\times\vec B)_x=A_yB_z-A_zB_y,\qquad (\vec A\times\vec B)_y=A_zB_x-A_xB_z,\qquad (\vec A\times\vec B)_z=A_xB_y-A_yB_x
   $$

   Each line is the one above it under $x\to y\to z\to x$, which is the
   same cyclic map that carries $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ into the other two
   boxed identities.

   The tempting wrong turn is the $y$-component specifically. Written in
   "alphabetical" order it looks like it should be $A_xB_z-A_zB_x$, and
   that is the negative of the right answer. The $y$-component is the one
   where alphabetical order and cyclic order disagree, which is why it is
   the component sign errors cluster on. Generate it by rotating the
   indices of the $x$-component, never by guessing.

   One thing is different in the operator version, and it is worth
   noticing that it makes no trouble. Every term in every component pairs
   a position with a momentum along a *different* axis, so by Question 1
   the two factors commute and $zp_x=p_xz$. The ordering in which you
   write them is therefore free, and each $L_i$ is automatically Hermitian
   as written, with no need for the symmetrized form
   $\tfrac12(AB+BA)$ that a product like $xp_x$ would demand. The classical
   formula transcribes into operators without ambiguity, which is not
   something you get for free in general.

   </details>

3. Using the cyclic pattern, state $[L_y,L_z]$ without rederiving it from
   scratch.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $[L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x$.

   Apply $x\to y,\ y\to z,\ z\to x$ to every index in
   $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$: the two entries in the bracket become $L_y$
   and $L_z$, and the operator on the right becomes $L_x$. The
   coefficient $i\hbar$ carries no index and does not move.

   The pattern licenses this only for *cyclic* relabellings. The order
   inside the bracket still matters: reverse it and you get
   $[L_z,L_y]=-i\hbar L_x$, since a commutator is antisymmetric. Rotating
   the indices and swapping the bracket entries are two different
   operations, and only the first is free.

   </details>

4. Explain why $[L_x,L_x]=0$ trivially (any operator commutes with
   itself), and why this is consistent with, not an exception to, the
   general antisymmetric commutation structure.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It is not an exception to antisymmetry. It is what antisymmetry forces
   when both slots hold the same operator.

   Directly from the definition, $[A,A]=AA-AA=0$ for any operator
   whatsoever, Hermitian or not, bounded or not. But the sharper point is
   that you never needed the definition. Antisymmetry alone says
   $[A,B]=-[B,A]$, so setting $B=A$:

   $$
   [A,A]=-[A,A] \implies 2[A,A]=0 \implies [A,A]=0
   $$

   Anything equal to its own negative is zero. So the vanishing diagonal
   is a *consequence* of the antisymmetric structure, in the same way the
   diagonal of an antisymmetric matrix is forced to be zero. The cyclic
   identities say nothing about it either way: all three of them relate
   two *distinct* components, so $[L_x,L_x]$ falls outside their scope
   rather than contradicting them.

   Physically, this is the statement that every observable is compatible
   with itself. Feed it into the general uncertainty relation
   $\Delta A\,\Delta B\ge\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ (the form used
   above gave $\tfrac{\hbar}{2}|\langle L_z\rangle|$ only because
   $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ supplied the $\hbar$) and you get
   $\Delta L_x\,\Delta L_x\ge\tfrac12|\langle[L_x,L_x]\rangle|=0$,
   a bound that constrains nothing, as it should: nothing forbids a state
   from having a perfectly definite $L_x$.

   The trap is reading "$L_x$ commutes with itself" as "$L_x$ is always
   sharp." It is not. What guarantees that a complete basis of $L_x$
   eigenstates exists is $L_x$ being *Hermitian*, not its commuting with
   itself: $[A,A]=0$ holds for every operator, including ones with no
   eigenbasis at all. Given Hermiticity, a state can be prepared with
   definite $L_x$, and a second immediate measurement of $L_x$ repeats the
   first. A generic state is still a superposition of those eigenstates
   and has $\Delta L_x>0$. Compatibility is a statement about which
   observables can share an eigenbasis, never about which states you
   happen to hold.

   </details>

## Summary

- $L_x,L_y,L_z$, built from position and momentum operators, satisfy
  $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ (and cyclic permutations), derived from the one
  surviving canonical commutator $[z,p_z]=i\hbar$, with every cross-axis
  term vanishing.
- This nonzero commutator means no two different components of $\vec L$
  can be simultaneously known precisely, a kind of quantum uncertainty
  distinct from the position-momentum kind.
- The identity is checked directly against this platform's general
  $J_x,J_y,J_z$ matrix operators for several values of $j$, not just
  derived symbolically once.
