## Two spins with no single total

Two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles do not combine into one well-defined total
spin. Depending on which combined state they occupy, the pair behaves as
spin $1$ or as spin $0$, and "spin $0$" for a pair of spinning things is
strange enough on its own to be worth the derivation.

This lesson works out that decomposition, then finds that one of the four
resulting states was already built on this platform, in a different
course, for reasons that had nothing to do with atoms.

## Four states, and which j each one carries

### The total angular momentum operator

For two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles, the total spin operators act on the
combined $4$-dimensional space via
$J_x=S_x^{(1)}\otimes I+I\otimes S_x^{(2)}$ (and similarly for $J_y,J_z$),
each particle's own spin operator, tensored with the identity on the
other particle, then summed. This is the standard way a single-particle
operator extends to a multi-particle system, already used throughout
Quantum Gates & Circuits.

The natural first move is to ask what $\vec J^{\,2}$ looks like in the
basis already in hand, the computational one:
$|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle, |{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle,
|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle, |{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$. Before
looking at the matrix, work out how much of the job that basis already
does.

The outer states $|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$ and
$|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ sit on their own with eigenvalue $2$.
The middle two do not: the off-diagonal $1$s couple
$|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ to $|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$, so
neither has a definite total spin on its own. That $2\times2$ block
$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $0$, and
diagonalizing it means taking the sum and the difference. The four states
listed next are what comes out.

### Four candidate combined states

Using the <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> $|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle,
|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle,|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle,
|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ (this platform's usual 2-qubit
ordering), consider:

$$
|1,1\rangle=|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle,\quad
|1,0\rangle=\frac{|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle+|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2},\quad
|1,-1\rangle=|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle,\quad
|0,0\rangle=\frac{|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

### Verified as exact eigenstates

Applying $\vec J^{\,2}$ and $J_z$, built as above from this platform's
$S_x,S_y,S_z$ at $j=\tfrac12$, to each of the four states gives, to
floating-point precision:

| State | $\vec J^{\,2}$ eigenvalue | $J_z$ eigenvalue | Label |
|---|---|---|---|
| $|1,1\rangle$ | $2\hbar^2$ | $\hbar$ | Triplet, $m=1$ |
| $|1,0\rangle$ | $2\hbar^2$ | $0$ | Triplet, $m=0$ |
| $|1,-1\rangle$ | $2\hbar^2$ | $-\hbar$ | Triplet, $m=-1$ |
| $|0,0\rangle$ | $0$ | $0$ | Singlet |

$2\hbar^2=j(j+1)\hbar^2$ at $j=1$, and $0=j(j+1)\hbar^2$ at $j=0$: both
agree with Lesson 2's formula, and both were computed rather than
inferred from symmetry.

Count dimensions as a check. The combined space is $2\times2=4$
dimensional, and the triplet ($2j+1=3$ states) plus the singlet ($1$
state) accounts for all four. No state is left over and none is
counted twice.

### The classical vector-model picture

Textbooks almost always accompany that table with a picture: draw the
two spin-$\tfrac12$ vectors, each of fixed length $\sqrt3/2\,\hbar$
($\sqrt{s(s+1)}\hbar$ at $s=\tfrac12$), tip to tail, and look at the
resultant $\vec J=\vec S_1+\vec S_2$. For the triplet, $\vec J$ keeps a
fixed length $\sqrt2\,\hbar$ ($\sqrt{j(j+1)}\hbar$ at $j=1$) while its
vertical component runs through $+\hbar,0,-\hbar$ across the three
$m$ values, matching the $J_z$ column above exactly. For the singlet
the two vectors point opposite ways and cancel, matching $\vec J=0$:

The picture earns its place for intuition and should not be trusted
further than that. $\vec S_1$ and $\vec S_2$ are operators, not arrows
with definite directions, and no measurement finds a spin pointing along
the drawn line. What the diagram does encode correctly is each vector's
length and $z$-projection, and those were verified from the operators in
the table above.

### The singlet is a Bell state

Compare $|0,0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle)$
directly to Quantum Gates & Circuits' Bell state
$|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$. Under
$|{\uparrow}\rangle\leftrightarrow|0\rangle$ and
$|{\downarrow}\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle$ they agree term for term.

These are the same vector. The maximally entangled Bell state was reached
by asking what a Hadamard and a CNOT do to $|00\rangle$; the singlet was
reached by asking which two-spin states have zero total angular momentum.
The two questions have no obvious relationship, and they have one answer.
(The triplet's $m=0$ state, $|1,0\rangle$, is $|\Psi^+\rangle$ in the same
way.)

the first place makes the identity concrete rather than just asserted.

$|\Psi^-\rangle$, so reaching $|\Psi^-\rangle$ takes one extra step:
after the Bell preset, target qubit q1 and apply X, then Z. That turns
$(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)/\sqrt2$ into $-(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)/\sqrt2$,

## Zero total spin means no preferred direction

The agreement is not a coincidence of notation, and there is a physical
reason for it. A system with total angular momentum zero has no preferred
direction available to it: rotate the whole state however you like and it
comes back unchanged, since there is no vector in it to rotate.

A state with no preferred direction cannot favour any outcome for a
single-particle measurement along any axis, so each particle alone reads
out at $50/50$. But the total must stay zero, so whatever one particle
gives, the other must cancel. Perfectly random individually, perfectly
anti-correlated jointly, in every basis.

Bell States and Entanglement reached correlations of this kind for
$|\Phi^+\rangle$ by pushing Hadamards through a circuit, one basis at a
time. The singlet's version is both stronger and cheaper. It follows from
a symmetry statement rather than a calculation, and it covers *every* axis
at once: zero angular momentum means no directional information, and no
directional information means no basis can be the special one.

$|\Phi^+\rangle$ carries no such guarantee, and in fact does not have the
property. It is perfectly *correlated* along $z$ and along $x$, and
perfectly *anti*-correlated along $y$, because
$|\Phi^+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|{+}i,{-}i\rangle+|{-}i,{+}i\rangle)$.
The singlet is the one Bell state whose correlation reads the same on
every axis, and its rotational invariance is why.

## Checking a Jz eigenvalue on a superposition

**Verify $|1,0\rangle$'s $J_z$ eigenvalue is exactly $0$, directly from
the individual-particle operators.**

$J_z|1,0\rangle = \left(S_z^{(1)}\otimes I+I\otimes S_z^{(2)}\right)\frac{|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle+|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2}$.
On $|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$: $S_z^{(1)}$ gives $+\tfrac\hbar2$,
$S_z^{(2)}$ gives $-\tfrac\hbar2$, summing to $0$. On
$|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$: $-\tfrac\hbar2+\tfrac\hbar2=0$ as well.
Both terms are annihilated, so the superposition is too, and the table's
$J_z=0$ entry is confirmed.

Note that this calculation cannot distinguish $|1,0\rangle$ from
$|0,0\rangle$: both have $J_z=0$, and the same argument applies to the
minus combination verbatim. Separating them requires $\vec J^{\,2}$,
which is the operator whose off-diagonal block forced the two
states to mix in the first place.

## A product state need not have a total spin

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any two-spin-$\tfrac12$ state has a well-defined total spin.**
Only the four states above, and their linear combinations *within* one
$j$, do. The plainest counterexample is a bare product state:
$|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ has a perfectly definite $J_z$ but is
*not* a $\vec J^{\,2}$ eigenstate at all: the off-diagonal $1$s in the
matrix above send it to
$|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle+|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$, which is
not a multiple of what went in. Contrast
$|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle+|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$, which
*is* one: it combines $|1,1\rangle$ and $|1,-1\rangle$, two members of the
same $j=1$ multiplet, so it inherits their shared eigenvalue $2\hbar^2$
while having no definite $m$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating the Bell-state/singlet connection as a resemblance between two
states.** Under the basis correspondence they are one vector with two
names, and the $\vec J^{\,2}=0$ check above is performed on that vector.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify $|1,1\rangle=|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$'s $J_z$ eigenvalue
   is exactly $\hbar$, directly from the individual operators (no
   superposition to track, unlike the worked example).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Each term of $J_z$ acts on its own particle and both return
   $+\tfrac\hbar2$, so the eigenvalue is their sum:

   $$
   J_z|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle = \left(S_z^{(1)}\otimes I + I\otimes S_z^{(2)}\right)|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle = \frac\hbar2|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle + \frac\hbar2|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle = \hbar\,|{\uparrow}{\uparrow}\rangle
   $$

   Both terms returned the *same* vector, which is what makes this an
   eigenvalue equation rather than a two-term expansion. In the worked
   example the two terms of $J_z$ acting on
   $|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ gave $+\tfrac\hbar2$ and
   $-\tfrac\hbar2$ on the same vector and cancelled, and the
   $|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$ half had to be tracked separately.
   Here there is one basis vector and nothing to track.

   Cross-check it against the labels. $m=1$ with $j=1$ is the top rung of
   the triplet ladder, and the top rung must satisfy $J_+|j,j\rangle=0$.
   That is visibly true: $J_+=S_+^{(1)}\otimes I+I\otimes S_+^{(2)}$, and
   both spins are already up, so each term annihilates the state. Nothing
   above it exists to raise into, which is the same statement in words.

   </details>

2. Work out which of the four Bell states from Quantum Gates & Circuits
   $|1,-1\rangle$ and $|0,0\rangle$ correspond to, if either does, and say
   which Bell state the decomposition supplies in place of any that turns
   out to be missing (Hint:
   $|1,-1\rangle=|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ is a product state, not
   entangled at all).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Only one of the two named states is a Bell state. $|0,0\rangle$ is
   $|\Psi^-\rangle$; $|1,-1\rangle=|{\downarrow}{\downarrow}\rangle=|11\rangle$
   is a plain product state and matches none of the four. The second Bell
   state appearing in this decomposition is the *other* $m=0$ state,
   $|1,0\rangle=|\Psi^+\rangle$.

   Line them up against the definitions:

   $$
   |\Psi^-\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle-|10\rangle}{\sqrt2} = |0,0\rangle, \qquad |\Psi^+\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle+|10\rangle}{\sqrt2} = |1,0\rangle
   $$

   while $|1,1\rangle=|00\rangle$ and $|1,-1\rangle=|11\rangle$ are
   single basis vectors, separable, with zero entanglement by any measure.
   A Bell state is by definition maximally entangled, so a product state
   cannot be one however the labels are matched up.

   The part worth extracting is why $|\Phi^\pm\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}
   (|00\rangle\pm|11\rangle)$ never appear here at all, in any of the four
   slots. $|00\rangle$ has $J_z$ eigenvalue $+\hbar$ and $|11\rangle$ has
   $-\hbar$, so any superposition of the two is a mix of different $J_z$
   eigenvalues and cannot itself be a $J_z$ eigenstate. Every state in the
   $|j,m\rangle$ basis is a $J_z$ eigenstate by construction, so
   $|\Phi^\pm\rangle$ is structurally excluded. That is not an accident of
   which Bell states someone chose to name: the total-spin basis and the
   Bell basis overlap in exactly two vectors, and the other two Bell
   states are legitimate two-qubit states with no definite total spin
   projection.

   </details>

3. Using the antisymmetry of $|0,0\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle)$
   under swapping the two particles, explain in one sentence why swapping
   which particle is "1" and which is "2" leaves the *physical* state
   unchanged (up to an overall sign, which doesn't affect any
   measurement).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the swap returns the same vector multiplied by $-1$, and an
   overall factor of $-1$ cancels out of every Born probability
   $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$ and every expectation value
   $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$, so no measurement can detect it.

   Explicitly, with $P$ the operator exchanging the two particles:

   $$
   P|0,0\rangle = \frac{|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle - |{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2} = -\,|0,0\rangle
   $$

   The distinction that matters, and the one this question is really
   testing: a *global* sign is unobservable, a *relative* sign is not. The
   minus sign sitting between the two terms inside $|0,0\rangle$ is
   entirely observable, and it is the whole difference between the singlet
   and the triplet's $|1,0\rangle$, which have different total spin and
   different measurement statistics. Multiplying the finished state by
   $-1$ is a different operation from flipping the sign of one term.

   One hypothesis is doing quiet work here. The conclusion holds because
   $|0,0\rangle$ happens to be an *eigenstate* of $P$. That is not
   automatic. Swap the labels on $|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle$ and you
   get $|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle$, a genuinely different state that
   predicts different outcomes, and no phase relates them. All four states
   in this lesson's decomposition are $P$ eigenstates, the three triplet
   states with $+1$ and the singlet with $-1$, which is why the labels
   drop out for all of them and not for a generic two-spin state.

   That $\pm1$ split is the entire subject of the Identical Particles
   course later in this track, where the swap eigenvalue stops being a
   convenience and becomes a law: two electrons are *required* to take the
   $-1$ option.

   </details>

4. Why does a spin-zero (singlet) two-particle system necessarily give
   perfectly random single-particle measurement outcomes along *any*
   axis, not just $z$? Connect this to $\vec J^{\,2}=0$ meaning zero
   total angular momentum in every direction simultaneously.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\vec J^{\,2}=0$ forces all three components to annihilate the
   state, which leaves the singlet with no preferred direction at all, and
   a state with no preferred direction cannot favour either outcome on any
   axis you choose.

   Start with the step that makes "zero in every direction" precise.
   $\vec J^{\,2}=J_x^2+J_y^2+J_z^2$ with each $J_i$ Hermitian, so

   $$
   0 = \langle\psi|\vec J^{\,2}|\psi\rangle = \big\|J_x|\psi\rangle\big\|^2 + \big\|J_y|\psi\rangle\big\|^2 + \big\|J_z|\psi\rangle\big\|^2
   $$

   A sum of non-negative numbers vanishes only if each vanishes, so
   $J_x|\psi\rangle=J_y|\psi\rangle=J_z|\psi\rangle=0$ separately, and
   hence $(\vec J\cdot\hat n)|\psi\rangle=0$ for every unit vector
   $\hat n$. Hermiticity is essential to that step; without it the norms
   are not what $\langle\vec J^{\,2}\rangle$ decomposes into.

   Randomness follows. Since $\vec J=\vec S_1+\vec S_2$ annihilates the
   state, and the same is true after any rotation, $\langle\vec S_1\rangle$
   is a vector that every rotation must leave unchanged, and the only such
   vector is zero. So for any axis,
   $\langle \vec S_1\cdot\hat n\rangle=0$. That observable has eigenvalues
   $\pm\hbar/2$ with probabilities $p_+$ and $p_-$, so

   $$
   \frac\hbar2\left(p_+ - p_-\right) = 0, \qquad p_+ + p_- = 1 \implies p_+ = p_- = \frac12
   $$

   Anti-correlation comes from the same equation read differently:
   $(\vec S_1\cdot\hat n)|\psi\rangle = -(\vec S_2\cdot\hat n)|\psi\rangle$,
   so whatever particle 1 gives, particle 2 gives the opposite with
   certainty, on every axis alike.

   The tempting wrong turn is to argue from how the state is *written*.
   $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle)$
   looks balanced between up and down, so $50/50$ along $z$ seems obvious,
   and it is easy to assume the balance carries over to other axes.
   Written form proves nothing here. $|\Phi^+\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}
   (|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ looks every bit as balanced and does give
   $50/50$ on each single qubit, yet its *correlations* are basis
   dependent: correlated along $z$ and $x$, anti-correlated along $y$, as
   the section above showed. The singlet's isotropy is not readable off
   the notation. It comes from $\vec J^{\,2}=0$, which is a statement
   about the state and not about the basis it is displayed in.

   </details>

## Summary

- Two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles combine into a spin-$1$ triplet (3 states)
  and a spin-$0$ singlet (1 state), verified as exact
  $\vec J^{\,2}$/$J_z$ eigenstates, not assumed by symmetry.
- The singlet $|0,0\rangle$ *is* the Bell state $|\Psi^-\rangle$, and the
  triplet's $m=0$ state is $|\Psi^+\rangle$: the same vectors, reached
  from atomic physics and from circuit construction independently.
- Total spin zero forces perfectly random single-particle outcomes along
  any measurement axis, with perfect *anti*-correlation between the two
  particles on every axis alike, the physical content behind Bell-state
  entanglement, arrived at from a completely different starting point.
