## Motivation

The infinite square well's walls are a convenient idealization — no real
potential is actually infinite. Make the walls finite (a well of depth
$V_0$ rather than $\infty$), and the physics changes in a genuinely new
way: the wavefunction no longer vanishes exactly at the boundary — the
Tunneling and the Finite Barrier lesson already showed *why* a classically
forbidden region allows a nonzero, exponentially decaying wavefunction
rather than a zero one. This lesson applies that exact mechanism to a
*bound* state instead of a barrier.

## Intuition

Picture the well the same way as before — a dip in potential energy from
$-a$ to $a$ — but now only $V_0$ deep, not infinitely deep. A particle
with energy $E$ satisfying $-V_0<E<0$ (bound, but not infinitely
confined) has real oscillatory behavior *inside* the well and
exponentially decaying behavior *outside* it, on both sides — no longer
forced to exactly zero at $x=\pm a$, just small.

## Mathematical Development

### Three regions, two kinds of solution

Take the well as $V(x)=-V_0$ for $|x|<a$, $V(x)=0$ for $|x|>a$ (matching
this platform's `finiteSquareWellPotential` convention exactly). A bound
state has $-V_0<E<0$. Define

$$
k \equiv \sqrt{2(E+V_0)} \quad(\text{inside, oscillatory}), \qquad \kappa \equiv \sqrt{-2E} \quad(\text{outside, decaying})
$$

(natural units, $\hbar=m=1$, matching every potential this platform's
engine works in). Inside the well, the time-independent Schrödinger
equation gives $\psi''=-k^2\psi$ (oscillatory, exactly as in the infinite
well); outside, $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$ (decaying, exactly as in the
classically forbidden region of the Tunneling lesson). The physically
sensible (normalizable) solution outside must decay as $|x|\to\infty$,
ruling out the growing exponential on each side.

### Restricting to even-parity solutions

The potential is symmetric ($V(-x)=V(x)$), so its eigenstates can be
chosen with definite parity — even or odd under $x\to-x$ (a general fact
about symmetric potentials, not derived in full here). Take the even
case first:

$$
\psi(x) = \begin{cases} B\,e^{\kappa x} & x<-a \\ A\cos(kx) & |x|\le a \\ B\,e^{-\kappa x} & x>a \end{cases}
$$

(the same $B$ on both sides, by the assumed symmetry).

### Matching at the boundary

Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=a$ (required for the Schrödinger
equation's second derivative to stay well-defined — the same requirement
used in the Tunneling lesson):

$$
A\cos(ka) = B\,e^{-\kappa a} \qquad\text{(continuity of $\psi$)}
$$

$$
-Ak\sin(ka) = -B\kappa\,e^{-\kappa a} \qquad\text{(continuity of $\psi'$)}
$$

Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates both unknown
amplitudes $A,B$ entirely:

$$
\boxed{\;k\tan(ka) = \kappa\;}
$$

This is the **transcendental quantization condition** for even-parity
bound states. (The odd-parity case, using $\psi=A\sin(kx)$ inside,
gives the companion equation $-k\cot(ka)=\kappa$ by an identical
matching argument — not derived in full here, but the same mechanism.)

### Why this has no closed form

Both $k$ and $\kappa$ depend on the *same* energy $E$
($k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$), so this is one transcendental
equation in one unknown ($E$, or equivalently $k$) — but with a
trigonometric function tangled together with a square root, there's no
algebraic manipulation that isolates $E$. This is qualitatively different
from the infinite well, where the boundary condition ($\sin(kL)=0$) was
solvable in closed form ($kL=n\pi$) precisely *because* the outside
region was simply forbidden entirely, removing the $\kappa$ side of the
equation altogether.

## Physical Interpretation

The transcendental equation packages a real physical trade-off: a deeper
or wider well (larger $V_0$ or $a$) pushes $k\tan(ka)$ and $\kappa$ into
agreement at a lower (more negative) $E$ — a more tightly bound state,
matching intuition. Crucially, **a finite well supports only finitely
many bound states** (possibly just one), unlike the infinite well's
infinite ladder — a directly testable, qualitatively different
prediction from the systems already solved.

## Worked Example

**Confirm that as $V_0\to\infty$ (with $a$ fixed), the transcendental
equation forces $k\to\pi/(2a)$ — the infinite well's ground-state
wavenumber.**

As $V_0\to\infty$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}=\sqrt{2(V_0-|E|)}\to\infty$ for any
finite bound-state energy near the bottom of the well. The equation
$k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ then forces $\tan(ka)\to\infty$, which happens exactly
when $ka\to\pi/2$ — i.e. $k\to\pi/(2a)$. Comparing to the infinite well's
formula $k_n=n\pi/(2a)$ for a well of full width $2a$ (Infinite Square
Well lesson, relabeled to this well's centered convention): this is
exactly $n=1$, the ground state. The finite well's transcendental
equation correctly reduces to the already-solved infinite-well result in
the appropriate limit — a genuine consistency check.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting that $k$ and $\kappa$ both depend on the same $E$.** They
look like two independent variables in the boxed equation, but
$k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$ are both functions of the
*one* unknown energy — the equation has exactly one unknown, not two.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming odd and even states satisfy the same transcendental
equation.** They don't — $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ is specifically the
even-parity condition; the odd-parity one has a cotangent instead, with
a different sign.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A finite well's bound states oscillate inside and decay exponentially
  outside, matched via continuity of $\psi,\psi'$ at the boundary.
- The even-parity condition $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ (with
  $k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$) is transcendental — no
  algebraic solution for $E$ exists.
- As $V_0\to\infty$, the equation correctly reduces to the infinite
  well's closed-form ground-state result — a genuine consistency check
  between two courses' derivations.
