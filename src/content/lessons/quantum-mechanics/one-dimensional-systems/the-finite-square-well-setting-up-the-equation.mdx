## Swapping hard walls for walls that leak

This lesson swaps the infinite well's hard walls for walls of finite
depth $V_0$, and the wavefunction responds by leaking into them. The
Tunneling and the Finite Barrier lesson already showed *why* a classically
forbidden region allows a nonzero, exponentially decaying wavefunction
rather than a zero one; this lesson applies that same mechanism to a
*bound* state instead of a barrier.

## Oscillating inside, decaying outside

Picture the well the same way as before, a dip in potential energy from
$-a$ to $a$, but now only $V_0$ deep, not infinitely deep. A particle
with energy $E$ satisfying $-V_0<E<0$ (bound, but not infinitely
confined) has real oscillatory behavior *inside* the well and
exponentially decaying behavior *outside* it, on both sides. At
$x=\pm a$ the wavefunction is no longer forced to zero; it is only
small.

## Three regions, matched at the boundary

### Three regions, two kinds of solution

Take the well as $V(x)=-V_0$ for $|x|<a$, $V(x)=0$ for $|x|>a$ (matching
this platform's `finiteSquareWellPotential` convention exactly). Concretely,
for $a=2$, $V_0=4$:

  energyLine={finiteWellGroundEnergy}
  ariaLabel="A finite square well of depth 4 and half-width 2, flat at V=0 outside and V=-4 inside, with the ground-state energy level and its wavefunction overlaid riding that level: a cosine bump inside the well smoothly meeting decaying exponential tails outside at x=-2 and x=2"
/>

Solving the boxed transcendental equation below for this well's
parameters gives a ground-state energy of $E_0\approx-3.778$ (in these
natural units): only slightly above the well floor at $-4$, consistent
with a state that is bound but leaks substantially into the classically
forbidden region on either side.

Unlike the infinite well's vertical walls, this dip has finite depth,
which is what makes the outside region merely *unlikely* rather than
strictly forbidden. A bound state has $-V_0<E<0$. Define

$$
k \equiv \sqrt{2(E+V_0)} \quad(\text{inside, oscillatory}), \qquad \kappa \equiv \sqrt{-2E} \quad(\text{outside, decaying})
$$

(natural units, $\hbar=m=1$, matching every potential this platform's
engine works in). Inside the well, the time-independent Schrödinger
equation gives $\psi''=-k^2\psi$ (oscillatory, exactly as in the infinite
well); outside, $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$ (decaying, exactly as in the
classically forbidden region of the Tunneling lesson). The physically
sensible (<Term id="normalization">normalizable</Term>) solution outside must decay as $|x|\to\infty$,
ruling out the growing exponential on each side.

### Restricting to even-parity solutions

The potential is symmetric ($V(-x)=V(x)$), so its <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstates</Term> can be
chosen with definite parity, even or odd under $x\to-x$ (a general fact
about symmetric potentials, not derived in full here). Take the even
case first:

$$
\psi(x) = \begin{cases} B\,e^{\kappa x} & x<-a \\ A\cos(kx) & |x|\le a \\ B\,e^{-\kappa x} & x>a \end{cases}
$$

(the same $B$ on both sides, by the assumed symmetry).

### Matching at the boundary

Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=a$ (required for the Schrödinger
equation's second derivative to stay well-defined, the same requirement
used in the Tunneling lesson):

$$
A\cos(ka) = B\,e^{-\kappa a} \qquad\text{(continuity of $\psi$)}
$$

$$
-Ak\sin(ka) = -B\kappa\,e^{-\kappa a} \qquad\text{(continuity of $\psi'$)}
$$

Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates both unknown
amplitudes $A,B$ entirely:

$$
\boxed{\;k\tan(ka) = \kappa\;}
$$

This is the **transcendental quantization condition** for even-parity
bound states. (The odd-parity case, using $\psi=A\sin(kx)$ inside,
gives the companion equation $-k\cot(ka)=\kappa$ by an identical
matching argument, not derived in full here, but the same mechanism.)

### Why this has no closed form

Both $k$ and $\kappa$ depend on the *same* energy $E$
($k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$), so this is one transcendental
equation in one unknown ($E$, or equivalently $k$). But with a
trigonometric function tangled together with a square root, there's no
algebraic manipulation that isolates $E$. This is qualitatively different
from the infinite well, where the boundary condition ($\sin(kL)=0$) was
solvable in closed form ($kL=n\pi$) *because* the outside region was
forbidden outright, which removed the $\kappa$ side of the equation
altogether.

## Deeper or wider, and more tightly bound

The transcendental equation packages a real physical trade-off: a deeper
or wider well (larger $V_0$ or $a$) pushes $k\tan(ka)$ and $\kappa$ into
agreement at a lower (more negative) $E$, a more tightly bound state,
matching intuition. One consequence is easy to miss: **a finite well
supports only finitely many bound states** (possibly just one), unlike
the infinite well's infinite ladder. That's a directly testable,
qualitatively different prediction from the systems already solved.

## Recovering the infinite well as the walls rise

**Confirm that as $V_0\to\infty$ (with $a$ fixed), the transcendental
equation forces $k\to\pi/(2a)$, the infinite well's ground-state
wavenumber.**

Adding the squares of the two definitions above gives
$k^2+\kappa^2=2(E+V_0)+(-2E)=2V_0$, so $\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$. As
$V_0\to\infty$ with $k$ staying finite (as the ground state's $k$ must,
since it stays near the infinite well's finite value), $\kappa\to\infty$.
The equation $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ then forces $\tan(ka)\to\infty$, which
happens exactly when $ka\to\pi/2$, i.e. $k\to\pi/(2a)$. Comparing to the infinite well's
formula $k_n=n\pi/(2a)$ for a well of full width $2a$ (Infinite Square
Well lesson, relabeled to this well's centered convention): that is the
$n=1$ case, the ground state. The finite well's transcendental equation
reduces to the already-solved infinite-well result in the appropriate
limit, which is the consistency check any correct generalization has to
pass.

## k and kappa are one unknown, not two

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting that $k$ and $\kappa$ both depend on the same $E$.** They
look like two independent variables in the boxed equation, but
$k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$ are both functions of the
*one* unknown energy: the equation has exactly one unknown, not two.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming odd and even states satisfy the same transcendental
equation.** They don't. $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ is specifically the
even-parity condition; the odd-parity one has a cotangent instead, with
a different sign.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Derive the odd-parity matching conditions explicitly (using
   $\psi=A\sin(kx)$ inside, $\psi=\pm Be^{\mp\kappa x}$ outside for
   $x\gtrless a$) and confirm the resulting equation is
   $-k\cot(ka)=\kappa$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Write the three regions with the sign pattern the question specifies,
   which is what makes the whole function odd:

   $$
   \psi(x) = \begin{cases} -B\,e^{\kappa x} & x<-a \\ A\sin(kx) & |x|\le a \\ B\,e^{-\kappa x} & x>a \end{cases}
   $$

   Check the parity before matching anything: $\sin$ is odd, and the two
   outside pieces carry opposite signs, so $\psi(-x)=-\psi(x)$
   throughout. Matching at $x=+a$ then settles the whole problem, since
   oddness carries the result to $x=-a$ automatically.

   Continuity of $\psi$ at $x=a$:

   $$
   A\sin(ka) = B\,e^{-\kappa a}
   $$

   Continuity of $\psi'$ at $x=a$, differentiating each piece:

   $$
   Ak\cos(ka) = -B\kappa\,e^{-\kappa a}
   $$

   Divide the second by the first. Both unknown amplitudes cancel, exactly
   as in the even case:

   $$
   \frac{Ak\cos(ka)}{A\sin(ka)} = \frac{-B\kappa e^{-\kappa a}}{B e^{-\kappa a}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k\cot(ka) = -\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -k\cot(ka) = \kappa \;\checkmark
   $$

   Two features are worth pausing on.

   The minus sign is not decoration and not a slip. $\kappa>0$ always, so
   the equation demands $\cot(ka)<0$, which happens only for
   $ka\in(\pi/2,\pi)$ and its later analogues. The even condition
   $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ instead demands $\tan(ka)>0$, putting its solutions
   in $(0,\pi/2)$ and its analogues. Even and odd states therefore live on
   *interleaved* stretches of the $k$ axis, which is why the bound states
   alternate parity as you climb the ladder, exactly as the infinite
   well's $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ alternate.

   The odd solutions can fail to exist. The even branch reaches all the way
   down to $k\to0$, where $k\tan(ka)\to0$ and the quarter circle
   $\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$ starts at $\sqrt{2V_0}>0$: the two must cross,
   which is the content of question 3. The odd branch does not start until
   $ka=\pi/2$, so if $\sqrt{2V_0}\,a<\pi/2$ the quarter circle never
   reaches it and there is no odd bound state at all. A shallow enough
   well binds exactly one particle state, and it is even.

   </details>

2. For a very shallow, wide well, would you expect $k$ to be small or
   large? Justify using the transcendental equation's structure. Then say
   what that implies about the binding energy $|E|$, taking care over
   whether $|E|$ moves with $k$ or against it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $k$ is small. The binding energy $|E|$ is also small in absolute terms,
   because the well is shallow, but relative to the well's own depth the
   state is *tightly* bound, and that is where the two quantities move in
   opposite directions.

   **Why $k$ is small.** Two separate pressures push it down. Wide: the
   even condition's first branch runs over $ka\in(0,\pi/2)$, so every
   solution obeys $k<\pi/(2a)$, and a large $a$ lowers that ceiling
   directly. Shallow: $k^2+\kappa^2=2V_0$, so $k\le\sqrt{2V_0}$, and a
   small $V_0$ lowers that ceiling too. Whichever is smaller does the
   binding, and both are small here. Running the platform's bisection at
   $a=5$, $V_0=0.1$ gives $k\approx0.214$; at $a=1$, $V_0=0.1$ it is
   $\approx0.410$. Widening the well halved the wavenumber, as predicted.

   **What that says about $|E|$, carefully.** The two are linked by

   $$
   |E| = V_0 - \frac{k^2}{2}
   $$

   which is the definition $k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$ rearranged. Read it and
   the two effects separate cleanly.

   At *fixed* $V_0$, a smaller $k$ means a *larger* $|E|$: the state sits
   closer to the well's floor and is more tightly bound. That is the
   widening effect, and it is why the answers above give $|E|=0.077$ at
   $a=5$ against $|E|=0.016$ at $a=1$, both at $V_0=0.1$: the wider well
   binds nearly $77\%$ of its own depth where the narrow one binds $16\%$.
   So $|E|$ moves *against* $k$ here.

   Lowering $V_0$ pushes both down at once, since $|E|\le V_0$ caps the
   binding energy outright. That is the shallowing effect, and it is why
   the absolute binding energy is tiny in this regime whatever the width.

   The reading to avoid is "small $k$ means weakly bound." It does not.
   Small $k$ means a long wavelength inside the well, which is what a
   low-lying state looks like; how *weakly* bound it is depends on how
   much of $V_0$ is left over, and in the widening case that fraction goes
   up. The absolute answer here is "weakly bound" only because $V_0$
   itself is small.

   </details>

3. Explain, without solving anything numerically, why a finite well must
   have *at least* one bound state, no matter how shallow (hint: consider
   $k\to0$ in the boxed equation).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the two sides of $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ start on opposite sides of
   each other and both are continuous, so they must cross somewhere in
   between. The Intermediate Value Theorem does the rest.

   Define $g(k)=k\tan(ka)-\kappa(k)$ with $\kappa(k)=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$, and
   look at the ends of the interval $k\in(0,\sqrt{2V_0})$ where bound
   states can live.

   **At the left end.** As $k\to0^+$, $\tan(ka)\approx ka$, so
   $k\tan(ka)\approx k^2a\to0$, while $\kappa\to\sqrt{2V_0}$, which is
   strictly positive for any $V_0>0$ however small. So $g(0^+)<0$.

   **At the right end.** Two cases, and both give $g>0$. If the tangent
   diverges first, that is if $\pi/(2a)<\sqrt{2V_0}$, then
   $k\tan(ka)\to+\infty$ as $ka\to\pi/2^-$ while $\kappa$ stays finite, so
   $g>0$ there. If the quarter circle runs out first, that is if
   $\sqrt{2V_0}<\pi/(2a)$, then as $k\to\sqrt{2V_0}^-$ we have
   $\kappa\to0^+$ while $k\tan(ka)$ is a strictly positive finite number
   (since $ka<\pi/2$ keeps the tangent positive), so again $g>0$.

   $g$ is continuous on the interval between, so it has a root. That root
   is a bound state, and the argument used no numbers at all.

   The graphical statement of the same thing is the picture the next
   lesson draws: the quarter circle starts *above* the first tangent
   branch at $k=0$ and ends *below* it, so the two must cross once. What
   makes the crossing unavoidable is that the tangent branch begins at
   the origin, at zero height, no matter what, while the quarter circle
   begins at a strictly positive height for any nonzero depth.

   Two boundaries on the claim, both worth carrying.

   It is a statement about *one* dimension. The same argument fails in
   three dimensions, where a sufficiently shallow spherically symmetric
   well genuinely has no bound state at all: the radial equation's
   effective boundary condition at the origin kills the analogue of the
   branch that starts at zero. That is why the deuteron being only barely
   bound is a fact worth remarking on, and why the one-dimensional
   intuition should not be exported.

   And "at least one" is exactly right. The count is finite: the quarter
   circle has radius $\sqrt{2V_0}$ and can only reach finitely many
   tangent and cotangent branches, so a finite well never has the infinite
   well's endless ladder. Deepening the well adds states one at a time,
   which is how the infinite ladder is recovered in the limit.

   </details>

## Summary

- A finite well's bound states oscillate inside and decay exponentially
  outside, matched via continuity of $\psi,\psi'$ at the boundary.
- The even-parity condition $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ (with
  $k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$) is transcendental: no
  algebraic solution for $E$ exists.
- As $V_0\to\infty$, the equation reduces to the infinite well's
  closed-form ground-state result, a consistency check across two
  courses' derivations.
