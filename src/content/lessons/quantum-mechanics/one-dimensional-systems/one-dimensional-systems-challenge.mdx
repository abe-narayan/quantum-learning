## A complete inventory, after two courses

Two courses of one-dimensional quantum mechanics leave a complete
inventory: the infinite well (exactly, closed form), the
<Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term> (exactly, closed form, by two independent methods),
<Term id="quantum-tunneling">tunneling</Term> through a barrier below its height (approximately, but with a
precise, derived exponential estimate), the finite well's bound states
(exactly characterized, solved numerically), and scattering off a step
or barrier above its height (exactly, closed form, with a real
resonance phenomenon).

## Recap: Every 1D System Solved So Far

| System | Bound or scattering? | Solution method |
|---|---|---|
| Infinite square well | Bound only | Closed form (boundary conditions alone) |
| Harmonic oscillator | Bound only | Closed form (two independent derivations) |
| Finite square well | Bound only | Transcendental equation, solved numerically |
| Barrier, $E<V_0$ | Scattering only | Approximate (exponential estimate), exact via simulation |
| Step / barrier, $E>V_0$ | Scattering only | Closed form (plane-wave matching) |

Every bound-state system has a **discrete** spectrum (the infinite
well's and oscillator's infinite ladders, the finite well's small finite
set); every scattering system has a **continuous** spectrum (any
$E>V_0$ is an allowed scattering energy). The bound/continuous split is
general, not a coincidence of these five examples: it comes from whether
the boundary conditions at infinity are restrictive enough to pick out
isolated energies.

**1. A finite well is made deeper and deeper (larger $V_0$, fixed
width). What happens to its number of bound states, and why, using the
graphical picture from Solving the Finite Well Numerically?**

The number of bound states grows. The quarter-circle
$\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$ has radius $\sqrt{2V_0}$, growing with $V_0$.
It eventually sweeps past more and more branches of the (fixed) tangent
and cotangent curves, adding one new bound state each time it crosses
another branch. This is the mechanism behind the earlier
observation that a shallow well supports few states while a very deep
one approaches the infinite well's endless ladder.

**2. A particle scatters off a barrier at an energy away from resonance
(neither $E\gg V_0$ nor near any $k_2L=n\pi$). Which earlier result, the
step's or the resonance's, better estimates its transmission
probability? Make the comparison quantitative.**

Neither is exact, but the step-potential intuition is the better starting
point: away from a resonance, the barrier's transmission is dominated by
the same kind of impedance mismatch that produces step reflection, just
computed for two boundaries instead of one. The resonance condition is a
special, narrow enhancement layered on top of that baseline behavior,
useful for predicting where $T$ peaks, not for a generic energy.

the chart below (the nearest resonance sits at $E=7$, where

baseline, and `barrierScatteringTransmission(6, 4, 1.2825)` the exact
two-boundary answer:

**3. Both the finite well and the barrier ($E>V_0$) involve the *same*
oscillatory-inside-a-region behavior. Why does one produce a discrete
spectrum and the other a continuum?**

The finite well requires the wavefunction to *also* decay (not oscillate)
outside on *both* sides, a much more restrictive requirement,
satisfiable only at special, discrete energies (the transcendental
equation's roots). The barrier's scattering state is oscillatory on
*both* outer sides too, a propagating wave rather than a decaying one.
Any energy is compatible with *some* valid oscillatory solution outside,
so nothing forces discreteness.

## Why one formula could never cover both

<Callout type="note">
**Expecting a single unified formula to cover both regimes.** Bound
states and scattering states are different kinds of mathematical
problems (an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> condition vs. a boundary-value problem with a
continuous parameter). The curriculum deliberately built separate tools
for each rather than forcing an artificial unification.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "numerically solved" as less rigorous than "closed form."**
The finite well's bisection-derived energy is as correct, to machine
precision, as the barrier's closed-form transmission formula.
The difference is which tool the underlying mathematics demands, not
which one is more trustworthy.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the recap table, identify which of the five systems is the only
   one whose solution method combined *both* a closed-form derivation and
   an approximate/numerical cross-check within the same course. (Hint:
   revisit the Tunneling lesson's approach.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The barrier below its height, $E<V_0$: the tunneling row.

   It is the only row whose entry names two methods of different kinds.
   The derivation is closed form as far as it goes: solving
   $\psi''=\kappa^2\psi$ inside the barrier and matching at both edges
   gives the transmission scale $e^{-2\kappa a}$ analytically, with
   $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$ written down exactly. What it does not
   give is the exact transmission, because the algebraic prefactor was not
   carried through, so the lesson cross-checks against the Wavefunction
   Explorer, which evolves a real packet and reports
   $P(\text{transmitted})$ and $P(\text{reflected})$ numerically. Analytic
   scaling plus numerical value, in the same lesson.

   Compare each of the other four to see why none qualifies.

   The infinite well is closed form and nothing else; the numbers are
   exact and there is nothing left to cross-check against. The harmonic
   oscillator has two derivations, ladder operators and a Gaussian
   substitution, but both are closed form, so it is a cross-check between
   two exact methods rather than between an exact one and an approximate
   one. The step and the over-the-barrier case are closed form outright,
   with $R+T=1$ verified as an algebraic identity rather than numerically.

   The finite well is the interesting near-miss and worth ruling out
   explicitly. It is solved numerically, by bisection, and it *is*
   cross-checked against a closed form: the $V_0\to\infty$ limit reduces
   the transcendental equation to the infinite well's exact
   $k\to\pi/(2a)$. But the direction is reversed. There the primary answer
   is numerical and the closed form is a limiting check on it, whereas the
   question asks for a primary derivation that is closed form with the
   numerics playing the checking role. The hint settles which one is
   meant.

   The general point behind the question is that the two kinds of method
   are complements, not a hierarchy. The Common Mistakes note at the top
   of this lesson says it directly: a bisection root is correct to machine
   precision, and which tool a problem demands is a fact about the
   equation, not about how much the answer can be trusted.

   </details>

2. If a well were deep enough to support exactly 3 even-parity bound
   states, roughly how many times would the growing quarter-circle from
   Worked Example 1 need to cross a tangent branch?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Three times: one crossing per even-parity state, since each root of
   $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ *is* one bound state.

   The counting is worth making precise, because "roughly" hides an exact
   condition. The even branches of $k\tan(ka)$ start at $ka=0,\pi,2\pi,
   \ldots$ and each diverges upward a quarter period later, at
   $ka=\pi/2,3\pi/2,5\pi/2$. The quarter circle
   $\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$ runs from $(0,\sqrt{2V_0})$ down to
   $(\sqrt{2V_0},0)$, so its reach along the $k$ axis is $\sqrt{2V_0}$.
   It crosses the $n$-th even branch exactly when its reach passes that
   branch's starting point:

   $$
   \sqrt{2V_0} > \frac{(n-1)\pi}{a}
   $$

   Three even states therefore need $\sqrt{2V_0}\,a>2\pi$, that is

   $$
   V_0 > \frac{2\pi^2}{a^2}
   $$

   which at $a=1$ is $V_0>19.74$. The earlier worked well, $a=1$ with
   $V_0=5$, is the cautionary case for this criterion. Its reach
   $\sqrt{2V_0}=3.1623$ clears the second even branch's start at
   $\pi=3.1416$ by two hundredths, so it carries *two* even states rather
   than one, the second of them barely bound at $E\approx-0.002$. A margin
   that thin is exactly what a drawn graph cannot resolve, which is why the
   inequality above is worth having in symbols.

   Two refinements keep the count honest.

   Each branch is crossed *once*, not more. The tangent branch rises
   monotonically from zero to infinity across its quarter period while the
   quarter circle falls monotonically, so they meet at most once per
   branch. That monotonicity is what turns "count the crossings" into a
   reliable method rather than a picture.

   And the question asks only about *even* states. The odd states satisfy
   $-k\cot(ka)=\kappa$, whose branches interleave with the even ones,
   starting at $ka=\pi/2,3\pi/2,5\pi/2,\ldots$. A well with *exactly* three
   even states has $\sqrt{2V_0}\,a$ just past $2\pi\approx6.28$, which
   clears the odd branches at $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$ but falls well short of
   the third at $5\pi/2\approx7.85$. So it holds two odd states, five in
   total, alternating even, odd, even, odd, even up the ladder. The general
   count agrees: a well of half-width $a$ and depth $V_0$ holds
   $\lceil2a\sqrt{2V_0}/\pi\rceil$ bound states, and here that argument is
   $4^{+}$, giving $5$. Counting one parity and reporting it as the total
   is the error to avoid, and so is assuming the two parities contribute
   equally.

   </details>

3. A future course extends these tools to 3 spatial dimensions (e.g.
   the Hydrogen Atom). Which single idea from *this* course, bound vs.
   continuous spectra, do you expect to reappear essentially unchanged,
   and which technique (transcendental-equation bisection, plane-wave
   matching) do you expect will need the most rework?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The bound-versus-continuous split survives untouched. Plane-wave
   matching needs the most rework; bisection on a transcendental
   condition transfers almost as it stands.

   **What survives.** The split is not a property of one dimension. It
   comes from the boundary condition at infinity: a state required to
   decay in every direction is over-constrained, and only isolated
   energies satisfy it, while a state allowed to oscillate out to infinity
   is not, and every energy above the threshold works. That argument never
   mentioned how many dimensions there are. Hydrogen bears it out exactly:
   $E<0$ gives the discrete Rydberg ladder, $E>0$ gives a continuum of
   ionized scattering states, and the two meet at $E=0$ where the electron
   is just barely free.

   **What transfers.** Separating a central potential into radial and
   angular parts leaves a one-dimensional equation in $r$, with an
   effective potential that includes a centrifugal term. That is this
   course's problem in a new variable: match the solution's behaviour at
   the origin and at infinity, get a transcendental condition on the
   energy, and bisect. The root-finder does not care that $r$ came from
   three dimensions, which is why the same bisection strategy is reused
   again in the WKB lesson later in this track.

   **What needs rework.** Plane-wave matching. In one dimension a
   scattering problem has two channels, left-moving and right-moving, so
   two boundary conditions settle everything and $r$ and $t$ come out in
   two lines. In three dimensions an incoming plane wave scatters into a
   *continuum of directions*, and there is no single reflected wave to
   match. The technique is replaced by a partial-wave expansion: decompose
   the incident wave into angular-momentum components, solve each one
   radially, and describe the whole result by a *phase shift* per partial
   wave rather than by one reflection amplitude. Quantum Mastery's
   three-dimensional scattering lesson is where that reconstruction
   happens, and it is a genuine rebuild rather than a relabelling.

   The pattern is the same one the Wave Mechanics capstone found for its
   own numerical machinery: what generalizes is the physical
   classification and the one-dimensional radial reduction, and what
   breaks is anything that assumed there were only two directions to go
   in.

   </details>

## Summary

- Every 1D potential solved across Wave Mechanics and this course splits
  cleanly into bound (discrete spectrum) or scattering (continuous
  spectrum) systems, a general distinction rather than a feature of any
  one potential.
- The finite well (bound, numerical) and the barrier (scattering, closed
  form) are complementary case studies in *how* to solve a system when
  no single method (purely algebraic, purely numerical) suffices for
  everything.
- Resonant transmission is a new phenomenon this course introduced, with
  a direct, non-coincidental analogy to classical wave optics.
