## Motivation

A ball rolling toward a downhill (or gentle uphill, as long as it has
enough energy) step always makes it across, classically, with
certainty. A quantum particle does not: some of its probability always
reflects, no matter how much energy it has above the step. This lesson
derives exactly how much, from nothing but plane waves and boundary
matching, and this time the result is fully closed-form; no numerics
needed.

## Intuition

The Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson built normalizable wave packets
from superpositions of plane waves; scattering problems instead work
directly with individual plane waves (idealized, not normalizable on
their own, but the standard and cleanest tool for describing a steady
beam of particles). A step in $V(x)$ changes the particle's wavenumber
abruptly, and just like light partially reflecting at any interface
where its speed changes (a glass-air boundary, for instance), a matter
wave partially reflects wherever its wavenumber changes discontinuously.

## Mathematical Development

### Setting up the two regions

Let $V(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $V(x)=V_0>0$ for $x\ge0$, with a particle of
energy $E>V_0$ incident from the left (over-the-step scattering: the
particle has enough energy to classically continue past $x=0$).
Define $k_1=\sqrt{2E}$ (region 1) and $k_2=\sqrt{2(E-V_0)}$ (region 2, a
real, smaller wavenumber since $E>V_0$). The general stationary-state
solutions:

$$
\psi_1(x) = e^{ik_1x} + r\,e^{-ik_1x} \quad(x<0), \qquad \psi_2(x) = t\,e^{ik_2x} \quad(x\ge0)
$$

Here $e^{ik_1x}$ is an incident wave (amplitude 1, normalized this way by
convention), $r\,e^{-ik_1x}$ a reflected wave (amplitude $r$), and
$t\,e^{ik_2x}$ a transmitted wave (amplitude $t$). There's no left-moving
wave in region 2, since nothing is incident from the right.

### Matching at $x=0$

Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=0$ gives two equations.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Continuity of ψ at x=0: both pieces must agree at the boundary.">
$$
1+r = t
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Continuity of ψ′ at x=0: differentiate each region's plane waves and match, cancelling the shared factor of i.">
$$
ik_1(1-r) = ik_2t \;\Longrightarrow\; k_1(1-r)=k_2t
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute t=1+r from the first equation into the second, then collect terms in r.">
$$
k_1-k_1r = k_2(1+r) = k_2+k_2r \;\Longrightarrow\; k_1-k_2 = r(k_1+k_2)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Solve for r, then recover t from t=1+r.">
$$
r = \frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}, \qquad t = \frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Reflection and transmission probabilities

The reflection probability is simply $R=|r|^2$. The transmission
probability needs an extra factor of $k_2/k_1$, accounting for the
different *probability flux*: a wave with smaller wavenumber carries
probability more slowly, so equal amplitude doesn't mean equal flux.

$$
R = \left(\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad T = \frac{k_2}{k_1}|t|^2 = \frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}
$$

**Verify $R+T=1$ directly** (probability conservation, the same
requirement checked throughout Wave Mechanics):

$$
R+T = \frac{(k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = \frac{k_1^2+2k_1k_2+k_2^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = \frac{(k_1+k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = 1 \;\checkmark
$$

using $(k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2=k_1^2-2k_1k_2+k_2^2+4k_1k_2=k_1^2+2k_1k_2+k_2^2=(k_1+k_2)^2$,
a genuine algebraic identity, not an assumption.

worth seeing directly:

$R$ drops fast as $E/V_0$ grows past 1, but the curve only approaches
the axis asymptotically. It never actually touches $R=0$ at any finite
energy, the visual form of the $R>0$ claim the next section makes
explicit.

That curve is the *aggregate* reflection probability; the actual
mechanism producing it is a standing-wave ripple in $|\psi(x)|^2$ for
$x<0$, from $\psi_1=e^{ik_1x}+r\,e^{-ik_1x}$ interfering with itself,
meeting a flat, uniform $|t|^2$ for $x\ge0$ where only the single
transmitted wave $t\,e^{ik_2x}$ survives:

Near $E/V_0=1$ the ripple amplitude is largest (large $r$, strong
interference); as $E/V_0$ grows the ripples shrink toward the flat
$|t|^2\to1$ line, the spatial picture of $R\to0$ only asymptotically.

## Physical Interpretation

$R=0$ only in the trivial case $k_1=k_2$ (no step at all, $V_0=0$).
Otherwise $R>0$ always: however small the step, however large the
particle's energy above it, *some* reflection survives. This is exactly
analogous to a light wave partially reflecting at any interface between
media with different refractive indices, however similar those indices
are. It's the same physics, a wave equation with a discontinuous
coefficient, producing the same qualitative behavior.

## Worked Example

**For $E=10$, $V_0=4$ (natural units), find $R$ and $T$.**

$$
k_1=\sqrt{20}\approx4.4721, \qquad k_2=\sqrt{12}\approx3.4641
$$

$$
R = \left(\frac{4.4721-3.4641}{4.4721+3.4641}\right)^{\!2} \approx (0.1270)^2 \approx 0.01613
$$

$$
T = 1-R \approx 0.98387
$$

So even with the particle's energy $2.5\times$ the step height, about
$1.6\%$ of an incident beam still reflects: small, but never zero, and
exactly what this platform's `stepPotentialScattering` function computes
and its test suite confirms sums to exactly 1 with $R$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $k_2/k_1$ factor in $T$.** Probability flux, not
amplitude-squared alone, is what must be conserved. $|t|^2$ alone
doesn't equal the transmission probability unless $k_1=k_2$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting classical behavior to reappear for large $E$.** $R\to0$ only
in the limit $E\to\infty$ (where $k_2\to k_1$), never exactly at any
finite energy. The reflection persists at every finite energy; it isn't
a small-energy artifact that a fast enough particle escapes.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Matching plane waves at a step potential gives closed-form
  $r=(k_1-k_2)/(k_1+k_2)$, $t=2k_1/(k_1+k_2)$.
- $R=r^2$ and $T=(k_2/k_1)t^2$ satisfy $R+T=1$ exactly, an algebraic
  identity.
- Quantum particles partially reflect off *any* potential step, however
  much energy they have above it: a genuine, persistent departure from
  classical mechanics.
