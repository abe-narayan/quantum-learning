export const stepHeight = 4;
export const stepK1 = (ratio) => Math.sqrt(2 * ratio * stepHeight);
export const stepK2 = (ratio) => Math.sqrt(2 * (ratio * stepHeight - stepHeight));
export const stepReflection = (ratio) => ((stepK1(ratio) - stepK2(ratio)) / (stepK1(ratio) + stepK2(ratio))) ** 2;

export const stepR = (ratio) => (stepK1(ratio) - stepK2(ratio)) / (stepK1(ratio) + stepK2(ratio));
export const stepT = (ratio) => (2 * stepK1(ratio)) / (stepK1(ratio) + stepK2(ratio));

export const stepDensityAt = (x, ratio) =>

<LessonHook eyebrow="Enough energy, and it still bounces back">
  Give a quantum particle more than enough energy to clear a step and it
  still, some of the time, bounces back. No amount of extra energy

## Classically certain, quantum mechanically not

A ball rolling toward a downhill (or gentle uphill, as long as it has
enough energy) step always makes it across, classically, with
certainty. A quantum particle does not: some of its probability always
reflects, no matter how much energy it has above the step. This lesson
derives exactly how much, from nothing but plane waves and boundary
matching, and this time the result is fully closed-form; no numerics
needed.

The result is not a curiosity confined to textbook potentials. Wherever a
material's conduction-band edge jumps at an interface, an electron
crossing it meets a step of exactly this kind, and the reflection derived
below is a standard ingredient in modelling carrier injection across a
semiconductor heterojunction.

## Working with plane waves on purpose

The Free-Particle Wave Packets lesson built <Term id="normalization">normalizable</Term> wave packets
from superpositions of plane waves; scattering problems instead work
directly with individual plane waves (idealized, not normalizable on
their own, but the standard and cleanest tool for describing a steady
beam of particles). A step in $V(x)$ changes the particle's wavenumber
abruptly, and just like light partially reflecting at any interface
where its speed changes (a glass-air boundary, for instance), a matter
wave partially reflects wherever its wavenumber changes discontinuously.

## Matching at the step, then counting flux

### Setting up the two regions

Let $V(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $V(x)=V_0>0$ for $x\ge0$, with a particle of
energy $E>V_0$ incident from the left (over-the-step scattering: the
particle has enough energy to classically continue past $x=0$).
Define $k_1=\sqrt{2E}$ (region 1) and $k_2=\sqrt{2(E-V_0)}$ (region 2, a
real, smaller wavenumber since $E>V_0$). The general stationary-state
solutions:

$$
\psi_1(x) = e^{ik_1x} + r\,e^{-ik_1x} \quad(x<0), \qquad \psi_2(x) = t\,e^{ik_2x} \quad(x\ge0)
$$

Here $e^{ik_1x}$ is an incident wave (<Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> 1, normalized this way by
convention), $r\,e^{-ik_1x}$ a reflected wave (amplitude $r$), and
$t\,e^{ik_2x}$ a transmitted wave (amplitude $t$). There's no left-moving
wave in region 2, since nothing is incident from the right.

### Matching at $x=0$

Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=0$ gives two equations.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Continuity of ψ at x=0: both pieces must agree at the boundary.">
$$
1+r = t
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Continuity of ψ′ at x=0: differentiate each region's plane waves and match, cancelling the shared factor of i.">
$$
ik_1(1-r) = ik_2t \;\Longrightarrow\; k_1(1-r)=k_2t
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute t=1+r from the first equation into the second, then collect terms in r.">
$$
k_1-k_1r = k_2(1+r) = k_2+k_2r \;\Longrightarrow\; k_1-k_2 = r(k_1+k_2)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Solve for r, then recover t from t=1+r.">
$$
r = \frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}, \qquad t = \frac{2k_1}{k_1+k_2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### The probability current

Amplitudes alone can't be converted straight into probabilities here,
because the two sides of the step carry probability at different
speeds. The right bookkeeping quantity is the **probability current**
$j$, and it comes from asking how $|\psi|^2$ can change in time at all:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Differentiate the probability density and substitute the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for each factor. The V terms cancel because V is real.">
    $$
    \frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = \psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} + \psi\frac{\partial\psi^*}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\psi^*\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} - \psi\frac{\partial^2\psi^*}{\partial x^2}\right)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The bracket is a total x-derivative, so the density obeys a continuity equation: probability drains out of a region only by flowing through its edges, at rate j.">
    $$
    \frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial j}{\partial x}, \qquad j \equiv \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\psi^*\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Evaluate j for a single plane wave ψ = Ae^(ikx): the derivative pulls down ik, and Im(ik|A|²) = k|A|². Flux is amplitude-squared times speed, with ħk/m playing the role of velocity.">
    $$
    j = \frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Reflection and transmission probabilities

Now apply that plane-wave result to the three waves in the problem. The
incident wave (amplitude 1) carries $j_{\text{inc}}=\hbar k_1/m$, the
reflected wave carries $|j_{\text{ref}}|=\hbar k_1|r|^2/m$ back toward
the source, and the transmitted wave carries
$j_{\text{tr}}=\hbar k_2|t|^2/m$. Reflection and transmission
probabilities are ratios of currents, not of amplitudes:

$$
R = \frac{|j_{\text{ref}}|}{j_{\text{inc}}} = |r|^2, \qquad T = \frac{j_{\text{tr}}}{j_{\text{inc}}} = \frac{k_2}{k_1}|t|^2
$$

The factor $k_2/k_1$ is not a convention. The transmitted wave moves
slower ($k_2<k_1$), so equal amplitude-squared would carry less
probability per unit time, and the current ratio corrects for it.
Substituting $r$ and $t$ from the boundary matching:

$$
R = \left(\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad T = \frac{k_2}{k_1}|t|^2 = \frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2}
$$

**Verify $R+T=1$ directly** (probability conservation, the same
requirement checked throughout Wave Mechanics):

$$
R+T = \frac{(k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = \frac{k_1^2+2k_1k_2+k_2^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = \frac{(k_1+k_2)^2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} = 1 \;\checkmark
$$

using $(k_1-k_2)^2+4k_1k_2=k_1^2-2k_1k_2+k_2^2+4k_1k_2=k_1^2+2k_1k_2+k_2^2=(k_1+k_2)^2$,
an algebraic identity, not an assumption.

Plotted against the energy ratio $E/V_0$, the shape of this falloff is
worth seeing directly:

That curve is the *aggregate* reflection probability; the actual

Near $E/V_0=1$ the ripple amplitude is largest (large $r$, strong
interference); as $E/V_0$ grows the ripples shrink toward the flat
$|t|^2\to1$ line, the spatial picture of $R\to0$ only asymptotically.

One detail in that picture is not decoration. The ripple's crests reach
exactly the height of the flat transmitted line, in every frame, because
$t=1+r$ makes $(1+r)^2$ and $|t|^2$ the same number. That is continuity of
$\psi$ at $x=0$, the first boundary condition of the derivation, showing
up as a geometric fact about the plot.

## However small the step, some reflection survives

$R=0$ only in the trivial case $k_1=k_2$ (no step at all, $V_0=0$).
Otherwise $R>0$ always: however small the step, however large the
particle's energy above it, *some* reflection survives. This is directly
analogous to a light wave partially reflecting at any interface between
media with different refractive indices, however similar those indices
are. The underlying situation is identical in both cases: a wave equation
whose coefficient jumps discontinuously, and a wave that cannot cross the
jump without leaving part of itself behind.

## R and T for a step of height four

**For $E=10$, $V_0=4$ (natural units), find $R$ and $T$.**

$$
k_1=\sqrt{20}\approx4.4721, \qquad k_2=\sqrt{12}\approx3.4641
$$

$$
R = \left(\frac{4.4721-3.4641}{4.4721+3.4641}\right)^{\!2} \approx (0.1270)^2 \approx 0.01613
$$

$$
T = 1-R \approx 0.98387
$$

So even with the particle's energy $2.5\times$ the step height, about
$1.6\%$ of an incident beam still reflects: small, but never zero. The
engine's `stepPotentialScattering` function computes these same
numbers, and its test suite confirms $R+T=1$.

## Flux is what is conserved, not amplitude squared

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $k_2/k_1$ factor in $T$.** Probability flux, not
amplitude-squared alone, is what must be conserved. $|t|^2$ alone
doesn't equal the transmission probability unless $k_1=k_2$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting classical behavior to reappear for large $E$.** $R\to0$ only
in the limit $E\to\infty$ (where $k_2\to k_1$), never exactly at any
finite energy. The reflection persists at every finite energy; it isn't
a small-energy artifact that a fast enough particle escapes.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $E=8$, $V_0=2$, compute $k_1,k_2,R,T$ and confirm $R+T=1$. Then
   show that $R$ depends only on the ratio $E/V_0$, not on $E$ and $V_0$
   separately, and name two other $(E,V_0)$ pairs that give this same $R$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   k_1=\sqrt{2(8)}=4, \qquad k_2=\sqrt{2(8-2)}=\sqrt{12}\approx3.4641
   $$

   $$
   R = \left(\frac{4-3.4641}{4+3.4641}\right)^{\!2} = (0.071797)^2 \approx 0.0051548
   $$

   $$
   T = \frac{4k_1k_2}{(k_1+k_2)^2} \approx 0.9948452, \qquad R+T = 1.0000000 \;\checkmark
   $$

   matching the engine's `stepPotentialScattering(8, 2)` to eight decimal
   places. Just under $0.52\%$ of the beam reflects off a step it carries
   four times the energy to clear.

   **Why only the ratio matters.** Divide numerator and denominator of $R$
   by $k_1$ and the absolute scale drops out:

   $$
   R = \left(\frac{1-k_2/k_1}{1+k_2/k_1}\right)^{\!2}, \qquad \frac{k_2}{k_1} = \sqrt{\frac{2(E-V_0)}{2E}} = \sqrt{1-\frac{V_0}{E}}
   $$

   Everything on the right depends on $E$ and $V_0$ only through $V_0/E$.
   So $R$ is a function of one variable, and the pair $(8,2)$ is just one
   representative of the whole family with $E/V_0=4$.

   **Two others with the same $R$.** $(E,V_0)=(4,1)$ and $(40,10)$. The
   engine returns $R=0.00515478$ for all three, with completely different
   wavenumbers: $k_1$ runs $2.828$, $4.000$, $8.944$ across them. Only
   their *ratio* $k_2/k_1=\sqrt{3}/2$ is shared, and that is what $R$
   sees.

   The lesson worth taking is that there is no absolute energy scale in
   this problem. Nothing in the setup supplies a length or a mass to
   compare $E$ against, so the answer cannot depend on $E$ alone; the only
   dimensionless combination available is $E/V_0$, and the reflection has
   to be a function of it. That is also why the lesson's plot of $R$ can
   be drawn once, against $E/V_0$, and be correct for every step height at
   once.

   The predictable slip is comparing $R$ across problems by comparing
   energies. A particle with $E=40$ is not more likely to cross than one
   with $E=8$; it depends entirely on what it is crossing.

   </details>

2. Show that $R\to0$ as $V_0\to0$ directly from the boxed formula (not by
   re-deriving from scratch).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   As $V_0\to0$, $k_2=\sqrt{2(E-V_0)}\to\sqrt{2E}=k_1$, so the numerator
   of

   $$
   R = \left(\frac{k_1-k_2}{k_1+k_2}\right)^{\!2}
   $$

   goes to zero while the denominator goes to $2k_1\ne0$. Hence $R\to0$,
   and $T\to1$.

   The physical reading is that a step of zero height is no step at all.
   With $V(x)$ constant everywhere, the wavefunction is a single
   uninterrupted plane wave with nothing to match at $x=0$, so nothing
   reflects. The formula reproduces that, which is a sanity check the
   derivation had to pass.

   It is worth seeing *how fast* it goes, because the rate is the
   interesting part. Expanding for small $V_0/E$:

   $$
   \frac{k_2}{k_1}=\sqrt{1-\frac{V_0}{E}} \approx 1-\frac{V_0}{2E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R \approx \left(\frac{V_0/2E}{2}\right)^{\!2} = \frac{V_0^2}{16E^2}
   $$

   quadratic in the step height. A step ten times smaller reflects a
   hundred times less, which is why a gentle potential gradient is nearly
   invisible to a fast particle and why the curve in the plot above hugs
   the axis so tightly at large $E/V_0$.

   The distinction the question is guarding is between a limit and a
   value. $R\to0$ *as* $V_0\to0$ does not say $R=0$ for small $V_0$. At
   any nonzero step height, however tiny, $k_1\ne k_2$ strictly and $R>0$
   strictly. The lesson's warning makes the same point at the other end of
   the range: $R\to0$ as $E\to\infty$ too, and never actually arrives
   there either. Reflection off a discontinuity is a wave phenomenon that
   scales down smoothly and never switches off.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why $t=2k_1/(k_1+k_2)$ can be *greater* than 1
   (amplitude, not probability) without violating anything, and name the
   quantity that is guaranteed to be $\le1$. Then find the largest value
   $t$ can reach for an over-the-step problem, and say what limit produces
   it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $t$ is an *amplitude*, and no conservation law bounds an
   amplitude. What is bounded is the probability current ratio
   $T=(k_2/k_1)|t|^2$, and the factor $k_2/k_1<1$ is exactly what pulls
   the oversized amplitude back below one.

   The physical picture is the same as a wave slowing down. Past the step
   the particle moves more slowly, $k_2<k_1$, so the same flow of
   probability per unit time has to be carried by a *taller* wave: fewer
   arrivals per second at each crest, so more amplitude per crest. Water
   waves piling up as they run into shallows do the same thing, and so
   does light entering a slower medium. Nothing is being created; the
   bookkeeping quantity is flux, not height.

   Check it on the worked example. At $E=10$, $V_0=4$, $t\approx1.1270$ so
   $|t|^2\approx1.2701$, comfortably over one, while

   $$
   T = \frac{k_2}{k_1}|t|^2 = \frac{3.4641}{4.4721}(1.2701) \approx 0.9839 < 1
   $$

   and $R+T=1$ exactly. It is $T$, never $|t|^2$, that the conservation
   law constrains.

   **The largest $t$ can get.** Write it as
   $t=2/(1+k_2/k_1)$ and note $k_2/k_1\in(0,1)$ for an over-the-step
   problem, so $t\in(1,2)$. It approaches $2$ as $k_2/k_1\to0$, that is as
   $E\to V_0^+$: a particle with only just enough energy to clear the
   step. The engine bears it out: at $E=2.0001$, $V_0=2$, it returns
   $t\approx1.98596$.

   That endpoint is worth reading carefully, because it is the most
   counter-intuitive corner of the problem. As $t$ climbs toward its
   maximum of $2$, the transmission $T$ falls toward *zero* and $R$ climbs
   toward $1$: in the same limit, $R\to0.9721$ at $E=2.0001$. The
   transmitted wave is at its tallest exactly where almost nothing gets
   through, because $k_2\to0$ means it is barely moving. A tall,
   near-stationary wave carries almost no current. Reading the amplitude
   as a probability would give precisely the wrong answer about this
   limit, which is why the current factor is in the formula.

   </details>

## Summary

- Matching plane waves at a step potential gives closed-form
  $r=(k_1-k_2)/(k_1+k_2)$, $t=2k_1/(k_1+k_2)$.
- $R=r^2$ and $T=(k_2/k_1)t^2$ satisfy $R+T=1$ exactly, an algebraic
  identity.
- Quantum particles partially reflect off *any* potential step, however
  much energy they have above it. The effect shrinks with energy but
  never vanishes.
