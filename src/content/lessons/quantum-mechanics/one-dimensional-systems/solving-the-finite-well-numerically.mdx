## Motivation

The last lesson derived a real physical result, $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$, with
no algebraic way to isolate $E$. That doesn't mean the problem is stuck;
it means it's the kind of equation numerical methods exist for. This
lesson solves it directly, the same way the split-operator method from
Wave Mechanics was a legitimate numerical technique rather than a
workaround.

## Intuition

Plot both sides of $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ as functions of $k$ (for fixed
well parameters). The left side is a repeating, diverging tangent
curve; the right side, $\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$, is a single quarter-circle
arc from $(0,\sqrt{2V_0})$ down to $(\sqrt{2V_0},0)$. Wherever the two
curves cross is a valid bound-state wavenumber, the classic **graphical
solution** every introductory course shows a picture of.

Drag the slider and watch the quarter-circle grow with $V_0$ while the
tangent branch stays fixed (it depends only on $a$, not $V_0$): the
crossing point moves to larger $k$, a deeper well pulling the bound
state to a larger wavenumber. A finite well always has at least this one
crossing, no matter how shallow: at $V_0=1$ it already sits at
$k\approx0.890$ ($E\approx-0.604$), just further down the first branch;
by $V_0=5$ (this lesson's own worked example below) the crossing has
moved out to $k\approx1.186$, clearly visible. Bisection is just that same graphical idea,
automated: repeatedly halve a bracket known to contain a sign change in
$k\tan(ka)-\kappa$, converging on the crossing point.

## Mathematical Development

### The bisection method, briefly

Define $g(k)\equiv k\tan(ka)-\kappa(k)$ (natural units). Near $k=0^+$,
$g(k)\to-\infty$ (since $\kappa(0)=\sqrt{2V_0}>0$ while $k\tan(ka)\to0$).
As $k$ increases toward the first point where $\tan(ka)$ diverges (at
$ka=\pi/2$) or where $\kappa(k)$ reaches zero (at $k=\sqrt{2V_0}$),
whichever comes first, $g(k)$ eventually becomes positive. Since $g$ is
continuous on that interval and changes sign, the Intermediate Value
Theorem guarantees a root. Bisection just repeatedly evaluates $g$ at
the midpoint of a shrinking bracket and keeps the half containing the
sign change, converging geometrically fast (each step halves the
uncertainty). This platform's `finiteSquareWellGroundStateEnergy`
function implements exactly this, tested directly against the
quantization condition it's supposed to solve.

There isn't a dedicated preset for this well in the Wavefunction
Explorer, and deliberately so: without a general numerical eigensolver
(this platform's documented, honest limitation), there's no closed-form
eigenstate to hand the simulator the way the infinite well and harmonic
oscillator presets do. Bisection is a different, complementary numerical
technique, the same one the graphical crossing above already showed you
by hand: it solves one transcendental equation for a single number
rather than evolving a full wavefunction, and it's exactly as legitimate
a piece of numerical physics for that narrower job.

### A worked well

Take $a=1$, $V_0=5$ (natural units). Running the bisection (100
iterations, more than enough for machine precision) gives

$$
k \approx 1.1863, \qquad E \approx -4.2964
$$

**Verify this actually satisfies the quantization condition**: computing
$k\tan(ka)$ and $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$ from the *unrounded* bisection output
(not the truncated decimals above, which accumulate rounding error in a
tangent function this sensitive) gives agreement to better than $10^{-6}$,
exactly what this platform's own test suite checks directly, rather
than trusting a by-hand verification with rounded inputs.

### Comparing to the infinite well

For the *same width* ($2a=2$), the infinite well's ground state is
$E_1^{\rm(infinite)}=\pi^2/(2\cdot2^2)\approx1.2337$ above the bottom of
its well (Infinite Square Well lesson). This finite well's ground state
sits at $E+V_0\approx0.7036$ above *its* well's bottom,
**noticeably lower** than the infinite well's, even though both wells
have the same width and this one has a depth ($V_0=5$) much larger than
either ground energy.

## Physical Interpretation

The finite well's ground state sits lower (relative to its own floor)
because the wavefunction isn't forced to zero at the walls. It leaks
into the classically forbidden region on both sides, effectively
"seeing" a well slightly wider than its literal boundaries. Confinement
that isn't perfectly rigid always costs a little less zero-point energy
than confinement that is, a real, general trend, not specific to this
one example.

## Worked Example

**How many bound states (of either parity) does the $a=1,V_0=5$ well
have, at minimum, based on the graphical picture?**

The quarter-circle $\kappa=\sqrt{10-k^2}$ spans $k\in(0,\sqrt{10}
\approx3.162)$. The tangent curve $k\tan(ka)$ (even states) has branches
starting at $ka=0,\pi,2\pi,\ldots$ and diverging as $ka\to\pi/2,3\pi/2,
\ldots$; with $a=1$, that's branches near $k\approx1.571,4.712,\ldots$.
Only the first branch ($k\in(0,1.571)$) overlaps the quarter-circle's
domain, giving exactly **one** even bound state (confirmed: the
bisection found it at $k\approx1.186$, safely inside that branch). The
odd-parity equation would need its own check, but by the same reasoning
(its first branch spans roughly $k\in(\pi/2a,\ldots)$) there is likely
exactly one odd state too for these parameters. This well supports a
small, finite number of bound states in total, not the infinite ladder
the infinite well has.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Treating bisection as an approximation to be distrusted.** For a
continuous function with a confirmed sign change, bisection converges to
the *exact* root to within any desired tolerance. It's not a heuristic,
it's a rigorous, provably-convergent method.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the finite well's ground state to exceed the infinite well's
(same width).** It's always the *opposite*: a finite well's confinement
is always "gentler," giving a lower ground energy above its own floor,
never higher.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The graphical method for a transcendental quantization condition finds
  crossings between a repeating tangent curve and a fixed quarter-circle
  arc; bisection automates finding one such crossing rigorously.
- For $a=1,V_0=5$: ground state $E\approx-4.2964$, sitting
  $\approx0.7036$ above the well's floor, lower than the same-width
  infinite well's $\approx1.2337$.
- A finite well supports only finitely many bound states, a
  qualitatively different prediction from the infinite well's endless
  ladder.
