## A statement about algebra, not about physics

The last lesson derived a real physical result, $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$, and
then admitted there is no algebraic way to isolate $E$ from it. That is a
statement about algebra, not about physics. The energy is a definite
number; the equation simply does not surrender it to symbol manipulation.

Wave Mechanics already made this move once, when it evolved the
Schrödinger equation numerically because no closed-form propagator was
available. The same standard applies here: a numerical answer is a real
answer when the method is provably convergent and its error is bounded.
Bisection is both.

## A tangent curve crossing a quarter circle

Plot both sides of $k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ as functions of $k$ (for fixed
well parameters). The left side is a repeating, diverging tangent
curve; the right side, $\kappa=\sqrt{2V_0-k^2}$, is a single quarter-circle
arc from $(0,\sqrt{2V_0})$ down to $(\sqrt{2V_0},0)$. Wherever the two
curves cross is a valid bound-state wavenumber, the classic **graphical
solution** every introductory course shows a picture of.

tangent branch stays fixed (it depends only on $a$, not $V_0$): the
crossing point moves to larger $k$, a deeper well pulling the bound
state to a larger wavenumber. A finite well always has at least this one
crossing, no matter how shallow: at $V_0=1$ it already sits at
$k\approx0.890$ ($E\approx-0.604$), just further down the first branch;
by $V_0=5$ (this lesson's own worked example below) the crossing has
moved out to $k\approx1.186$, clearly visible. Bisection is just that same graphical idea,
automated: repeatedly halve a bracket known to contain a sign change in
$k\tan(ka)-\kappa$, converging on the crossing point.

## Bisection, run on one concrete well

### The bisection method, briefly

Define $g(k)\equiv k\tan(ka)-\kappa(k)$ (natural units). Near $k=0^+$,
$g(k)\to-\infty$ (since $\kappa(0)=\sqrt{2V_0}>0$ while $k\tan(ka)\to0$).
As $k$ increases toward the first point where $\tan(ka)$ diverges (at
$ka=\pi/2$) or where $\kappa(k)$ reaches zero (at $k=\sqrt{2V_0}$),
whichever comes first, $g(k)$ eventually becomes positive. Since $g$ is
continuous on that interval and changes sign, the Intermediate Value
Theorem guarantees a root. Bisection just repeatedly evaluates $g$ at
the midpoint of a shrinking bracket and keeps the half containing the
sign change, converging geometrically fast (each step halves the
uncertainty). This platform's `finiteSquareWellGroundStateEnergy`
function is that loop, tested against the quantization condition it is
supposed to solve.

The Wavefunction Explorer has no preset for this well, by design. Without
a general numerical eigensolver, which this platform documents as a
limitation rather than papering over, there is no closed-form
<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> to hand the simulator the way the infinite well and harmonic
oscillator presets do. Bisection does a narrower job: it solves one
transcendental equation for one number rather than evolving a full
<Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term>. For that job it is the right tool, and it is the same
tool the graphical crossing above already applied by eye.

### A worked well

Take $a=1$, $V_0=5$ (natural units). Running the bisection (100
iterations, more than enough for machine precision) gives

$$
k \approx 1.1863, \qquad E \approx -4.2964
$$

**Does that satisfy the quantization condition?** Computing $k\tan(ka)$
and $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$ from the *unrounded* bisection output (not the
truncated decimals above, which accumulate rounding error in a tangent
function this sensitive) gives agreement to better than $10^{-6}$. That
comparison is what this platform's test suite asserts, rather than a
by-hand check on rounded inputs.

### Comparing to the infinite well

For the *same width* ($2a=2$), the infinite well's ground state is
$E_1^{\rm(infinite)}=\pi^2/(2\cdot2^2)\approx1.2337$ above the bottom of
its well (Infinite Square Well lesson). This finite well's ground state
sits at $E+V_0\approx0.7036$ above *its* well's bottom,
**noticeably lower** than the infinite well's, even though both wells
have the same width and this one has a depth ($V_0=5$) much larger than
either ground energy.

## Why the finite well's ground state sits lower

The finite well's ground state sits lower (relative to its own floor)
because the wavefunction isn't forced to zero at the walls. It leaks
into the classically forbidden region on both sides, effectively
"seeing" a well slightly wider than its literal boundaries. Confinement
that isn't perfectly rigid always costs a little less zero-point energy
than confinement that is, a real, general trend, not specific to this
one example.

## Counting bound states off the graph

**How many bound states, of either parity, does the $a=1,V_0=5$ well
have?**

The quarter-circle $\kappa=\sqrt{10-k^2}$ spans $k\in(0,\sqrt{10}
\approx3.1623)$, and everything happens inside that domain. The
even-parity curve $k\tan(ka)$ rises from zero on each branch that starts
at $ka=0,\pi,2\pi,\ldots$ and diverges as $ka\to\pi/2,3\pi/2,\ldots$;
with $a=1$ those branches begin at $k=0$ and $k=\pi\approx3.1416$.

The first branch crosses the arc in the obvious place, and the bisection
found it: $k\approx1.1863$, $E\approx-4.2964$, the ground state.

The second branch is the one worth slowing down for. It begins at
$k=3.1416$, only $0.02$ short of where the arc runs out at $3.1623$, so
the two overlap on a sliver almost too narrow to see on a sketch. On that
sliver $k\tan(ka)$ climbs from zero while $\kappa$ falls to zero, so they
must cross, and they do, at $k\approx3.1616$ with
$E\approx-0.0020$. That is a genuine bound state, barely.

The odd-parity condition $-k\cot(ka)=\kappa$ has its first branch on
$ka\in(\pi/2,\pi)$, comfortably inside the arc's domain, and contributes
one crossing at $k\approx2.3186$, $E\approx-2.3121$.

So this well holds **three** bound states, alternating even, odd, even,
and the standard count agrees: a well of half-width $a$ and depth $V_0$
holds $\left\lceil 2a\sqrt{2V_0}/\pi\right\rceil$ of them, here
$\lceil2.0132\rceil=3$. The near-miss on the third is the lesson inside
the lesson. Whether that state exists at all turns on comparing $3.1416$
against $3.1623$, and no graphical reading settles a margin that thin.
Finitely many bound states, yes; the exact count is arithmetic, not
eyeballing.

## Bisection is rigorous, not a heuristic

<Callout type="note">
**Treating bisection as an approximation to be distrusted.** For a
continuous function with a confirmed sign change, bisection converges to
the *exact* root to within any desired tolerance. It's not a heuristic,
it's a rigorous, provably-convergent method.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the finite well's ground state to exceed the infinite well's
(same width).** It's always the *opposite*: a finite well's confinement
is always "gentler," giving a lower ground energy above its own floor,
never higher.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $a=2,V_0=3$, this platform's engine finds the ground state at
   $E\approx-2.7882$. Confirm this is between $-V_0$ and $0$, as any
   bound state must be.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $-3 < -2.7882 < 0$, so yes.

   The condition $-V_0<E<0$ is not a formality; each inequality rules out
   a different kind of nonsense, and checking them is the cheapest test a
   numerical root has to pass.

   $E>-V_0$ says the energy sits above the well's floor. If it did not,
   then $E+V_0<0$ and $k=\sqrt{2(E+V_0)}$ would be imaginary, so the
   wavefunction would decay rather than oscillate *inside* the well. Here
   $E+V_0\approx0.2118>0$, comfortably above, and $k\approx0.6509$ is
   real.

   $E<0$ says the state is bound. If it were not, $\kappa=\sqrt{-2E}$
   would be imaginary and the "decaying tails" would oscillate instead,
   making the state a scattering state with a continuous spectrum rather
   than one of a discrete set. Here $\kappa\approx2.3614$ is real.

   The quantization condition itself gives a stronger check, and it costs
   one line: $k\tan(ka)$ with these numbers is $2.3614$, matching
   $\kappa=2.3614$ to six figures. That is what the platform's test suite
   asserts, and it verifies the root rather than just its bracket.

   Worth noting how little of the well's depth this state uses. It sits
   only $0.2118$ above a floor $3$ deep, so $|E|/V_0\approx93\%$: a wide
   well ($a=2$) confines gently, the wavefunction is long and slow inside,
   and the state settles close to the bottom. Compare the lesson's own
   $a=1,V_0=5$ well, whose ground state sits $0.7036$ above its floor. The
   narrower well costs far more zero-point energy, which is the
   $E\propto1/a^2$ scaling of the infinite well showing through.

   </details>

2. Explain in one sentence why bisection needs a *confirmed sign change*
   in its starting bracket, and what could go wrong if you picked a
   bracket without checking this first.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the sign change is the entire guarantee: the Intermediate Value
   Theorem promises a root inside a bracket where $g$ changes sign, and
   with no sign change there is no promise at all, so the method still
   converges, to a number that need not be a root of anything.

   That is the failure worth naming: bisection does not error out. It
   halves the bracket, keeps a half by the same rule as always, and
   returns a perfectly precise answer, which is simply wrong. There is no
   diagnostic in the output to distinguish it from a correct run, which is
   why the check belongs at the start rather than the end.

   Two concrete ways to pick a bad bracket here.

   Bracketing no root at all, for instance by starting above
   $k=\sqrt{2V_0}$ where $\kappa$ has already gone imaginary and $g$ is
   not even defined. The physical statement is that no bound state exists
   there, and the numerics have no way to say so unprompted.

   Bracketing a *pole* rather than a root. $g(k)=k\tan(ka)-\kappa$ jumps
   from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$ across $ka=\pi/2$, so a bracket spanning
   that point contains a sign change and no root. Bisection will happily
   converge on the pole, reporting a "solution" at $k=\pi/(2a)$ where the
   equation is not satisfied at all. This is the standard trap for
   tangent-based conditions and the reason the lesson's bracket stops at
   the first divergence rather than running past it.

   Both are caught by the same habit: evaluate $g$ at the two endpoints,
   confirm the signs differ, and then confirm afterwards that $g$ at the
   returned root really is near zero rather than near infinity. The second
   check is what separates a root from a pole, since only one of them has
   $|g|$ small.

   </details>

3. Without recomputing anything, would you expect a *deeper* well (same
   width, larger $V_0$) to push the ground state's energy above its
   floor higher or lower, relative to the $V_0=5$ example? Justify using
   the trend toward the infinite-well limit.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Higher. Deepening the well raises the ground state's energy above its
   own floor, climbing toward the infinite well's $\approx1.2337$ from
   below.

   The reasoning uses only the limit already established. As
   $V_0\to\infty$ at fixed $a$, the transcendental equation forces
   $k\to\pi/(2a)$, the infinite well's ground-state wavenumber, and
   the energy above the floor is

   $$
   E+V_0 = \frac{k^2}{2} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2a}\right)^{\!2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8a^2}
   $$

   which at $a=1$ is $\approx1.2337$. The finite well always sits below
   that value, because its wavefunction leaks and so behaves like a
   slightly wider box, and deepening the well suppresses the leak. So the
   energy above the floor rises monotonically toward the ceiling and never
   passes it.

   The platform's bisection confirms the trend at $a=1$: $V_0=5$ gives
   $0.7036$ above the floor, $V_0=12$ gives $0.8474$, $V_0=50$ gives
   $1.0190$, and $V_0=200$ gives $1.1189$, all approaching $1.2337$
   without reaching it. Notice how slowly: quadrupling the depth from
   $50$ to $200$ closes only about half the remaining gap.

   Two things the answer is *not*, since the question invites both
   confusions.

   It is not a claim that the state becomes less bound. The absolute
   energy $E$ plunges: $-4.296$ at $V_0=5$, $-198.881$ at $V_0=200$. The
   state is far more tightly bound in absolute terms while sitting higher
   above its own, much lower, floor. "Above the floor" and "below zero"
   are two different reference points, and the question fixes the first.

   And it is not a violation of the Common Mistakes warning that a finite
   well's ground state is always lower than the infinite well's. It stays
   lower at every finite depth. The limit is approached, not crossed, which
   is exactly what makes the infinite well the ceiling rather than a case
   the finite well can beat.

   </details>

## Summary

- The graphical method for a transcendental quantization condition finds
  crossings between a repeating tangent curve and a fixed quarter-circle
  arc; bisection automates finding one such crossing rigorously.
- For $a=1,V_0=5$: ground state $E\approx-4.2964$, sitting
  $\approx0.7036$ above the well's floor, lower than the same-width
  infinite well's $\approx1.2337$.
- A finite well supports only finitely many bound states, a
  qualitatively different prediction from the infinite well's endless
  ladder.
