## What this course added, and what it deliberately skipped

This course deliberately avoided re-deriving density matrices,
entanglement, or Bell inequalities. Entanglement, Mixed States & Bell
Tests already built that foundation thoroughly. What this course added
were two new directions instead: open-system evolution (going beyond
<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term> dynamics) and an entirely different formulation of quantum
mechanics itself (the path integral). This capstone connects both back
to the bigger picture, and says plainly where the course's honesty about
open questions leaves us.

## Closed to open, operators to paths

### From closed to open systems

Every prior course's dynamics, Wave Mechanics' time evolution,
Entanglement's `evolveDensityMatrix`, assumed a closed, isolated
system. This course's <Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus operators</Term>, $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$,
generalize that to open systems, with unitary evolution as the special
one-operator case. Nothing here contradicts what came before; it's a
strict extension, verified directly (trace-preservation condition)
rather than assumed.

Both decoherence channels this course built start from the same
$|+\rangle$ and drive it to different long-run fates. Dephasing settles
at purity $0.5$; amplitude damping dips and then returns to $1$, because
it drains the qubit toward $|0\rangle$, which is itself a pure state.

That return is worth interrogating, because it is easy to read the wrong
conclusion off it.

Dephasing destroys phase alone, so it settles into a genuine statistical
mixture at <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> $0.5$, the maximally mixed qubit. Amplitude damping
destroys phase *and* drains population toward one specific pure state, so
its purity recovers to $1$.

The two are structurally different processes that happen to share a name,
and the pair makes a useful point about vocabulary. "Decoherence" is
sometimes used as a synonym for purity loss, and these two channels show
why that identification fails: both destroy the superposition
irreversibly, and only one of them leaves a mixed state behind.

### From operators to paths

Where Kraus operators extend the formalism, the path integral replaces
it. It computes the same physical predictions (transition amplitudes)
through a completely different mathematical object: a sum over histories,
where the operator picture has an operator acting on a state. This course verified their consistency in
one specific, tractable case (the free particle, Euclidean time) via
the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law, rather than asserting the
equivalence in general.

### What decoherence resolves, and what it doesn't

The two claims get blurred often enough to be worth separating one final
time.

Decoherence explains why coherent superpositions do not survive contact
with an environment. That is a computed, checkable result, and this
course produced the numbers.

It does not explain why a measurement yields one definite outcome. After
full decoherence the state is a probabilistic mixture, and a mixture only
ever describes not knowing which outcome occurred. Where in it does one
outcome actually occur? Getting from "one of these, unknown" to "this one" requires
something the formalism above does not supply.

What fills the gap is a choice of interpretation, and the candidates
disagree with each other rather than refining each other. Copenhagen
treats collapse as primitive; many-worlds denies that a unique outcome
needs explaining, since every branch is realized; objective-collapse
models modify the dynamics and are, unlike the others, experimentally
testable in principle. This course takes no position, and the honest
summary is that a century of work has made the question sharper without
settling it.

## Decoherence does not reach that far

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating decoherence as "solving" the measurement problem.** Decoherence
explains coherence loss, not outcome selection. It is a strong result and
it does not reach that far.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the path integral and operator formalism are competing
theories.** They're the same physics in different mathematical clothing.
This course's numerical check (Chapman-Kolmogorov) is evidence of
consistency in one case, not a claim that one approach is "more
fundamental" than the other.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. In one sentence each, state what a Kraus channel generalizes, and
   what the path integral reframes, using this course's specific
   terminology.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   A Kraus channel $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ generalizes unitary
   evolution $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$ from closed systems to open ones,
   with the unitary case recovered as the single-operator instance
   $K_0=U$.

   The path integral reframes the whole formulation: it computes the same
   transition amplitudes as a sum over every history weighted by
   $e^{iS/\hbar}$, in place of an operator acting on an evolving state.

   The pairing is worth keeping distinct, because the two additions are
   different in kind. The Kraus form is a strict *extension*: it produces
   maps no unitary can, so it makes predictions the earlier framework
   could not make, and the purity curves are the evidence. The path
   integral is a *reformulation*: it predicts nothing new, agrees on
   everything, and earns its place by making different facts obvious.
   Confusing the two leads to asking which formulation is correct, which
   is not a question either one answers.

   </details>

2. Why is "decoherence solves the measurement problem" an overclaim, per
   this course's explicit discussion?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because decoherence delivers a mixture, and a mixture is a statement
   about not knowing which outcome obtains rather than an account of one
   obtaining.

   The two claims separate cleanly, and it is worth having both in front
   of you.

   What decoherence does establish, with numbers this course computed: a
   superposition in contact with an environment loses its off-diagonal
   density-matrix elements geometrically fast, the resulting state is
   experimentally indistinguishable from a classical probability
   distribution, and the basis that survives is fixed by the coupling
   rather than by anyone's choice. That is a real result and it disposes
   of the cat.

   What it does not establish: that one outcome occurs. The dynamics
   producing the mixture were linear and unitary on system plus
   environment, and no branch was discarded at any step. Nothing in the
   calculation converts "one of these, unknown" into "this one," and there
   is no line of it where that conversion could be inserted.

   The gap is filled only by choosing an interpretation, and the
   candidates disagree rather than refine each other: Copenhagen takes
   collapse as primitive, many-worlds denies there is a unique outcome
   needing explanation, and objective-collapse models change the dynamics
   and are testable in principle for exactly that reason.

   The overclaim is common enough to be worth naming as a habit rather
   than a slip. A tidy final density matrix is persuasive, and it is easy
   to let the arrival of a familiar-looking classical object stand in for
   a derivation of classical definiteness.

   </details>

3. Name the two specific engine functions from this course that will be
   reused directly (not re-derived) in the future Noise, Decoherence &
   Scaling course.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `amplitudeDampingChannel` and `dephasingChannel`.

   They are reused rather than rebuilt because they are already the
   quantities that course measures. Amplitude damping is energy relaxation
   toward $|0\rangle$, whose time constant is $T_1$; dephasing is phase
   randomization at fixed populations, whose time constant is $T_2$. The
   $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ parameters this course slid around are those
   decay rates in dimensionless form, so the abstract channel and the
   number quoted on a device datasheet are the same object.

   That is also why the ordering $T_2\le 2T_1$ holds on real hardware:
   losing energy destroys phase as a side effect, so the dephasing
   timescale can never much exceed twice the relaxation one, while pure
   dephasing can and does make it shorter.

   </details>

4. Looking back across this entire course, which single numerical result
   most convincingly demonstrates that "sum over paths" and "solve the
   Schrödinger equation" give the same physics, in the one case actually
   tested here?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The Chapman-Kolmogorov check: the discretized two-slice path sum for
   $x_f=3$, $x_i=-1$, $\tau=2$ matched the closed-form Euclidean
   propagator to a relative difference of about $1.3\times10^{-15}$.

   What makes it evidence rather than a tautology is where the two sides
   come from. The right-hand side was built by the path-integral recipe:
   integrate over every intermediate position, summing amplitudes for
   two-segment histories. The left-hand side is the free-particle
   propagator in closed form, which is a solution of the imaginary-time
   Schrödinger equation. Two constructions with no step in common,
   agreeing to machine precision.

   Being honest about the strength of the test matters more here than
   claiming the win, and the lesson is careful about this. It is one case,
   and a friendly one. The particle is free, so there is no potential; the
   time is Euclidean, so there are no oscillatory phases to cancel; and
   the identity being checked is at bottom a Gaussian convolution, which
   is exact for any number of slices rather than converging as slices are
   added. So it confirms that the construction is correctly implemented
   and reproduces the known answer. It does not test the equivalence where
   the equivalence is hard, which is with an interacting potential in real
   time, and this course states the general equivalence rather than
   proving it.

   That is worth appreciating as a pattern rather than a limitation. A
   check you can actually run on a system you already know the answer to
   is how you calibrate a method before pointing it at something unknown,
   the same move the time-dependent perturbation theory lesson made when
   it put Runge-Kutta beside a first-order formula on a two-level system
   nobody needed either for.

   </details>
