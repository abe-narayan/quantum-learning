## Motivation

This course deliberately avoided re-deriving density matrices,
entanglement, or Bell inequalities. Entanglement, Mixed States & Bell
Tests already built that foundation thoroughly. What this course added
were two new directions instead: open-system evolution (going beyond
<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term> dynamics) and an entirely different formulation of quantum
mechanics itself (the path integral). This capstone connects both back

open questions leaves us.

## Synthesis

### From closed to open systems

Every prior course's dynamics, Wave Mechanics' time evolution,
Entanglement's `evolveDensityMatrix`, assumed a closed, isolated
system. This course's <Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus operators</Term>, $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$,
generalize that to open systems, with unitary evolution as the special
one-operator case. Nothing here contradicts what came before; it's a
strict extension, verified directly (trace-preservation condition)
rather than assumed.

Both decoherence channels this course built start from the same
$|+\rangle$ and drive it to different long-run fates. Dephasing settles
at purity $0.5$; amplitude damping dips and then returns to $1$, because
it drains the qubit toward $|0\rangle$, which is itself a pure state.

That return is worth interrogating, because it is easy to read the wrong
conclusion off it.

Dephasing destroys phase alone, so it settles into a genuine statistical
mixture at <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> $0.5$, the maximally mixed qubit. Amplitude damping
destroys phase *and* drains population toward one specific pure state, so
its purity recovers to $1$.

The two are structurally different, not two labels for the same process,
and the pair makes a useful point about vocabulary. "Decoherence" is
sometimes used as a synonym for purity loss, and these two channels show
why that identification fails: both destroy the superposition
irreversibly, and only one of them leaves a mixed state behind.

### From operators to paths

The path integral is a different formulation altogether, not an
extension in the same sense as Kraus operators are. It computes the
same physical predictions (transition amplitudes) via a completely
different mathematical object: a sum over histories, rather than an
operator acting on a state. This course verified their consistency in
one specific, tractable case (the free particle, Euclidean time) via
the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law, rather than asserting the
equivalence in general.

### What decoherence resolves, and what it doesn't

The two claims get blurred often enough to be worth separating one final
time.

Decoherence explains why coherent superpositions do not survive contact
with an environment. That is a computed, checkable result, and this
course produced the numbers.

It does not explain why a measurement yields one definite outcome. After
full decoherence the state is a probabilistic mixture, and a mixture is a
description of not knowing which outcome occurred, not an account of one
occurring. Getting from "one of these, unknown" to "this one" requires
something the formalism above does not supply.

What fills the gap is a choice of interpretation, and the candidates
disagree with each other rather than refining each other. Copenhagen
treats collapse as primitive; many-worlds denies that a unique outcome
needs explaining, since every branch is realized; objective-collapse
models modify the dynamics and are, unlike the others, experimentally
testable in principle. This course takes no position, and the honest
summary is that a century of work has made the question sharper without
settling it.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating decoherence as "solving" the measurement problem.** Decoherence
explains coherence loss, not outcome selection. It is a strong result and
it does not reach that far.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the path integral and operator formalism are competing
theories.** They're the same physics in different mathematical clothing.
This course's numerical check (Chapman-Kolmogorov) is evidence of
consistency in one case, not a claim that one approach is "more
fundamental" than the other.
</Callout>
