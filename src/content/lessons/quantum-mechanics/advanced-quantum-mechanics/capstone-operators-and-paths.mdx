## Synthesis

### From closed to open systems

Every prior course's dynamics, Wave Mechanics' time evolution,
Entanglement's `evolveDensityMatrix`, assumed a closed, isolated
system. This course's <Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus operators</Term>, $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$,
generalize that to open systems, with unitary evolution as the special
one-operator case. Nothing here contradicts what came before; it's a
strict extension, verified directly (trace-preservation condition)
rather than assumed.

Both decoherence channels this course built started from the very same
$|+\rangle$ state, yet drive it toward completely different long-run
fates. Watch what each one does to the SAME starting state as the
coupling strength $\eta$ (used as both channel's parameter) changes:

Dephasing destroys phase only, so the state it settles into is a true
statistical mixture (purity 0.5, exactly the maximally mixed state).
Amplitude damping destroys phase AND drains population toward a specific
pure state, so <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> actually recovers to 1 in the long run. That's a
real structural difference between the two channels, not just two
examples of "decoherence" with the same eventual behavior.

### From operators to paths

The path integral is a different formulation altogether, not an
extension in the same sense as Kraus operators are. It computes the
same physical predictions (transition amplitudes) via a completely
different mathematical object: a sum over histories, rather than an
operator acting on a state. This course verified their consistency in
one specific, tractable case (the free particle, Euclidean time) via
the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law, rather than asserting the
equivalence in general.

### What decoherence resolves, and what it doesn't

Stated plainly, one more time, because the two get blurred so often:
decoherence (this course's Kraus-channel demonstration) explains why
*coherent superpositions* don't survive contact with an environment, a
real, checkable, and important physical fact. It does *not*, by itself,
explain why a measurement yields one *definite* outcome rather than a
probabilistic mixture of possibilities. That remains an open question in
the foundations of quantum mechanics: interpretations like Copenhagen,
many-worlds, or objective collapse models each answer it differently,
and this course takes no position on which is correct.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating decoherence as "solving" the measurement problem.** As stated
repeatedly in this course: decoherence explains coherence loss, not
outcome selection. Claiming otherwise overstates what was actually shown.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the path integral and operator formalism are competing
theories.** They're the same physics in different mathematical clothing.
This course's numerical check (Chapman-Kolmogorov) is evidence of
consistency in one case, not a claim that one approach is "more
fundamental" than the other.
</Callout>
