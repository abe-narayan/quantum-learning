## Motivation

This course deliberately avoided re-deriving density matrices,
entanglement, or Bell inequalities. Entanglement, Mixed States & Bell
Tests already built that foundation thoroughly. What this course added
were two genuinely new directions: open-system evolution (going beyond
unitary dynamics) and an entirely different formulation of quantum
mechanics itself (the path integral). This capstone connects both back
to the bigger picture.

## Synthesis

### From closed to open systems

Every prior course's dynamics, Wave Mechanics' time evolution,
Entanglement's `evolveDensityMatrix`, assumed a closed, isolated
system. This course's Kraus operators, $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$,
generalize that to open systems, with unitary evolution as the special
one-operator case. This isn't a contradiction of anything learned
before. It's a strict extension, verified directly (trace-preservation
condition) rather than assumed.

### From operators to paths

The path integral is a genuinely different formulation, not an extension
in the same sense. It computes the same physical predictions (transition
amplitudes) via a completely different mathematical object (a sum over
histories, rather than an operator acting on a state). This course
verified their consistency in one specific, tractable case (the free
particle, Euclidean time) via the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law,
rather than asserting the equivalence in general.

### What decoherence resolves, and what it doesn't

Stated plainly, one more time, because it's easy to blur: decoherence
(this course's Kraus-channel demonstration) explains why *coherent
superpositions* don't survive contact with an environment, a real,
checkable, and important physical fact. It does *not*, by itself, explain
why a measurement yields one *definite* outcome rather than a
probabilistic mixture of possibilities. That remains a genuinely open
question in the foundations of quantum mechanics (interpretations like
Copenhagen, many-worlds, or objective collapse models each answer it
differently, and this course takes no position on which is correct).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating decoherence as "solving" the measurement problem.** As stated
repeatedly in this course: decoherence explains coherence loss, not
outcome selection. Claiming otherwise overstates what was actually shown.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking the path integral and operator formalism are competing
theories.** They're the same physics in different mathematical clothing.
This course's numerical check (Chapman-Kolmogorov) is evidence of
consistency in one case, not a claim that one approach is "more
fundamental" than the other.
</Callout>

## Summary

- This course extended (not repeated) Entanglement, Mixed States & Bell
  Tests: open quantum systems via Kraus operators, a numerical
  demonstration of decoherence, and Feynman's path integral as a genuinely
  different formulation, verified consistent with the operator picture in
  one tractable case.
- Decoherence explains coherence loss into an environment; it does not
  resolve which single outcome is observed upon measurement, an honest,
  explicitly-stated open question.
- This course's Kraus-channel machinery is built to be reused directly in
  the Quantum Computing pillar's hardware-noise and error-correction
  courses, not just as a one-off demonstration.
