<LessonHook eyebrow="No observer anywhere in the argument">
Why don't we see cats in superpositions of alive and dead, or coins spinning in superpositions of heads and tails? Decoherence is the standard, well-established (if partial) answer: a macroscopic system is never fully isolated.
</LessonHook>

## The cat question, answered quantitatively

The previous lesson's <Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus-channel machinery</Term> is enough to answer the
cat question quantitatively, and the answer needs no measurement and no
observer anywhere in it.

The argument runs in two parts. First, repeated contact with an
environment drives a superposition's off-diagonal density-matrix entries
to zero, which this lesson computes step by step. Second, the rate at
which that happens scales brutally with size: the more degrees of freedom
a system has and the more strongly they couple outward, the faster the
coherence goes. For anything macroscopic the timescale is short enough
that the superposition was never available to observe in the first place.

A cat is not protected from superposition by a law forbidding it. It is
in one for an interval too short to matter.

## Tracking the off-diagonals as they go

### Off-diagonal coherence, tracked numerically

Starting from $|+\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$'s density
matrix (off-diagonal entries $\pm0.5$, <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> exactly 1, a pure state),
consider applying the dephasing channel from the previous lesson
($\lambda=0.3$) over and over.

The summary at the top of this page already gives away where the purity
lands. The diagonal is the part it does not mention, so commit to that
one first: $P(0)$ and $P(1)$ both start at $0.5$, and the channel is
about to run thirty times.

Applying the channel repeatedly gives:

| Applications | Off-diagonal magnitude | Purity |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.500 | 1.000 |
| 1 | 0.350 | 0.745 |
| 2 | 0.245 | 0.620 |
| 5 | 0.084 | 0.514 |
| 10 | 0.014 | 0.500 |
| 20 | 0.0004 | 0.500 |

Off-diagonal coherence decays geometrically toward $0$, and purity toward
$0.5$, the maximally mixed state of a single qubit. What is left carries
no phase information at all, only classical diagonal probabilities.

Note that the decay is geometric rather than sudden: each application
multiplies the coherence by a fixed factor, so it never reaches zero in
finitely many steps. In the laboratory this shows up as an exponential
$T_2$ decay curve, and "decoherence time" always means the time constant
of that exponential rather than a moment when something switches off.

applications, sampled across that range by the same

### What survives: the diagonal

Throughout, the diagonal entries $P(0)=P(1)=0.5$ never move. Dephasing,
true to its name, destroys phase while leaving populations alone.

What is left at the end is a density matrix identical, entry for entry,
to the one describing "the system was in $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ all
along and nobody knew which." Not similar to it: identical. No
measurement whatsoever can distinguish the two, because a density matrix
fixes every prediction there is.

That is the quantum-to-classical transition, and it is worth noticing how
unmysterious the mechanism turned out to be. Nothing collapsed and
nothing was observed. Phase information moved into correlations with an
environment nobody is tracking, and tracking is the only thing that could
have brought it back.

## Coherence is spread, rather than destroyed

Environmental interaction does not destroy superposition by any special
mechanism. It spreads the coherence into correlations with degrees of
freedom nobody is tracking, and tracing those out is what leaves the
system's own density matrix without off-diagonal terms. The information
is not gone from the universe; it is gone from anything the experimenter
can reach.

The rates involved are what settle the cat. A qubit in a good trap holds
coherence for seconds. A dust grain in air is struck by molecules
continuously, each collision recording its position, and the standard
estimates (Joos and Zeh, 1985) put its coherence time many orders of
magnitude below anything observable; removing the air leaves thermal
photons doing the same job more slowly but still far too fast. Macroscopic
superpositions are not forbidden. They are short-lived beyond any
possibility of noticing.

**What decoherence does not do** is explain why a single definite outcome
is observed. The post-decoherence state is a probabilistic mixture, and a
mixture is a description of ignorance about which outcome occurred, not
an account of one occurring. Decoherence converts "both at once" into
"one of these, unknown"; the step from there to "this one" remains open,
and this course states that rather than papering over it.

## How many applications to reach 0.01

**After how many dephasing applications ($\lambda=0.3$) does $|+\rangle$'s
off-diagonal magnitude first drop below 0.01?**

From the table above: at 10 applications, off-diagonal $\approx0.014$
(still above 0.01); at 20 applications, $\approx0.0004$ (well below).
Since each application multiplies the off-diagonal magnitude by exactly
$(1-\lambda)=0.7$ (a direct consequence of the dephasing Kraus operators'
structure), the threshold is crossed at
$n=\log(0.01/0.5)/\log(0.7)\approx10.9$, i.e. the 11th application,
directly checkable against `applyChannelRepeatedly`'s engine output.

## Coherence loss is not outcome selection

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing decoherence with <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">wavefunction collapse</Term>.** Decoherence turns a
pure superposition into a mixed (probabilistic) state. It does NOT, by
itself, pick out one definite outcome. The state after full decoherence
is still "50% this, 50% that," not "definitely this." What actually
selects one outcome upon measurement remains an open question in the
foundations of quantum mechanics, not settled by decoherence alone.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking decoherence only matters for exotic macroscopic superpositions.**
The same mathematics governs any qubit weakly coupled to any uncontrolled
environment, and it is the reason quantum computers need both careful
isolation and error correction. Isolation lengthens the decay constant;
it cannot make it infinite, which is why error correction is not
optional.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify that each dephasing application multiplies the off-diagonal
   magnitude by exactly $(1-\lambda)$, using the Kraus operators from the
   previous lesson.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $K_0=\sqrt{1-\lambda/2}\,I$ and $K_1=\sqrt{\lambda/2}\,Z$, the
   channel is a weighted mixture of doing nothing and applying $Z$:

   $$
   \mathcal E(\rho) = \left(1-\frac\lambda2\right)\rho + \frac\lambda2\,Z\rho Z
   $$

   Now look at what $Z\rho Z$ does to a single entry. Since $Z$ is
   diagonal with entries $+1$ and $-1$, the $(0,1)$ element picks up one
   factor from each side:

   $$
   \left(Z\rho Z\right)_{01} = Z_{00}\,\rho_{01}\,Z_{11} = (+1)\rho_{01}(-1) = -\rho_{01}
   $$

   Substituting into the mixture, the two contributions partly cancel:

   $$
   \mathcal E(\rho)_{01} = \left(1-\frac\lambda2\right)\rho_{01} - \frac\lambda2\rho_{01} = (1-\lambda)\,\rho_{01}
   $$

   So each application scales the coherence by $(1-\lambda)$, and $n$
   applications by $(1-\lambda)^n$. Starting from $|+\rangle$ with
   $\rho_{01}=0.5$ and $\lambda=0.3$, that predicts
   $0.5(0.7)^n$: $0.350$, $0.245$, $0.084$ at $n=1,2,5$, which is the
   table.

   Two things fall out of the same formula. The factor of two in
   $\lambda/2$ is why maximal decay happens at $\lambda=1$ rather than
   somewhere else, since $1-\lambda$ hits zero there. And the decay is
   geometric, so the coherence never reaches zero in finitely many steps.
   In the laboratory this is why $T_2$ is quoted as a time constant of an
   exponential and never as a moment when coherence ends.

   </details>

2. Why does the diagonal of the density matrix stay exactly constant
   under repeated dephasing, while the off-diagonal decays?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because both Kraus operators are diagonal in the computational basis,
   so neither can move population between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.
   Run the same calculation on the $(0,0)$ entry:

   $$
   \left(Z\rho Z\right)_{00} = Z_{00}\,\rho_{00}\,Z_{00} = (+1)\rho_{00}(+1) = \rho_{00}
   $$

   so $\mathcal E(\rho)_{00}=(1-\tfrac\lambda2)\rho_{00}+\tfrac\lambda2
   \rho_{00}=\rho_{00}$, unchanged. The off-diagonal saw a relative minus
   sign between the two branches and the diagonal did not, and that single
   sign is the entire asymmetry.

   Physically, dephasing is the environment learning *phase* without
   exchanging *energy*. Nothing about the channel takes the qubit from
   $|1\rangle$ toward $|0\rangle$, which is why relaxation is a separate
   channel, amplitude damping, with a separate timescale $T_1$. A real
   device measures both, and $T_2\le 2T_1$ precisely because energy loss
   destroys phase as a side effect while phase loss costs no energy.

   The deeper name for what is happening is worth having. The
   computational basis here is the *pointer basis*: the basis the
   environment is effectively monitoring, whose states are left untouched
   while everything else decays. Which basis that is depends on the
   coupling, not on anyone's choice, and it is why the classical world
   comes out with a definite set of alternatives rather than an arbitrary
   one. Explaining the selection of the basis is decoherence's real
   achievement, and the next question is about what it still does not
   explain.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why "the system decohered into a probabilistic
   mixture" is not the same claim as "the system's measurement outcome
   was determined."

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a mixture is a description of not knowing which outcome
   obtains, and saying "it is $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, each with
   probability one half" is not saying that either one has happened.

   The gap is easy to lose because the arithmetic is so convincing. After
   enough dephasing, $\rho=\tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0|
   +\tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|$ is identical, entry for entry, to the
   density matrix of a coin that was heads or tails all along, and no
   measurement can tell the two situations apart. That much decoherence
   genuinely delivers, and it is why nobody sees interference from a cat.

   What it does not deliver is a mechanism producing one result. The
   evolution that got you here was linear and unitary on system plus
   environment; nothing anywhere in it ever discarded a branch. The
   universal state still contains both, now correlated with an environment
   too large to undo. Reading the resulting mixture as "one of these is
   real and the others are not" is an additional claim, and it is exactly
   the claim that different interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree
   about.

   So the honest ledger: decoherence explains why superpositions are
   unobservable, why the classical alternatives come in the basis they do,
   and why the transition is fast enough to be invisible. It does not
   explain outcome selection, and treating it as if it did is the most
   common overreach in this subject. The lesson says so above rather than
   letting the tidy final density matrix imply otherwise.

   </details>

4. If $\lambda=1$ (maximal dephasing per application), how many
   applications are needed to reach purity 0.5? Why does this differ from
   the $\lambda=0.3$ case's gradual, many-application decay?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   One. At $\lambda=1$ the scaling factor $(1-\lambda)$ is zero, so a
   single application sends the coherence to exactly zero and the state
   is maximally mixed immediately.

   Purity follows directly, since for this starting state
   $\rho_{00}=\rho_{11}=\tfrac12$ throughout:

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2) = \frac12 + 2|\rho_{01}|^2 = \frac12 + \frac12(1-\lambda)^{2n}
   $$

   At $\lambda=1$ and $n=1$ that is exactly $0.5$. At $\lambda=0.3$ it is
   $0.5+0.5(0.7)^{2n}$, which is $0.5004$ at $n=10$ and
   $0.5+2.5\times10^{-10}$ at $n=30$: approaching $0.5$ and never arriving.
   The table's $0.500$ at ten applications is rounding, not an exact hit.

   The difference between the two cases is the difference between
   multiplying by zero and multiplying by a number less than one.
   Repeatedly multiplying by $0.7$ is a geometric sequence, which has no
   finite step at which it becomes zero; multiplying by $0$ finishes in
   one step. Nothing qualitative changed in the channel, only the value of
   one parameter, and the qualitative behaviour changed anyway, which is
   the usual signature of a limit point rather than a smooth trend.

   One thing worth being careful about: $\lambda=1$ is the *most*
   destructive setting, and it does not mean "always apply $Z$." It means
   applying $Z$ with probability $\lambda/2=\tfrac12$. A channel that
   applied $Z$ with probability $1$ would be the deterministic unitary
   $\rho\to Z\rho Z$, which preserves purity perfectly and destroys no
   coherence at all. Maximal decoherence is maximal *randomness* about
   whether the phase flip happened, not a maximally strong disturbance.
   That is the same lesson as the previous one's amplitude-damping curve:
   what mixes a state is the branch nobody tracked, never the size of the
   kick.

   </details>

## Summary

- Repeated dephasing drives $|+\rangle$'s off-diagonal coherence toward 0
  and its purity toward 0.5, a direct numerical demonstration of
  decoherence, not an assertion.
- The diagonal (populations) are unaffected; only phase information is
  lost, so the resulting state looks exactly like a classical probabilistic
  mixture.
- Decoherence explains the *loss of quantum coherence*, but does not by
  itself explain *definite measurement outcomes*, an honest, explicitly
  stated scope limitation, not a resolved question.
