## Motivation

Ask a quantum particle how it got from A to B, and Feynman's answer is:
every way imaginable, all at once. Not just the straight line, or
whatever trajectory Newton's laws would pick out — literally every
conceivable path between the two points, including ones that zigzag
wildly, double back on themselves, or wander off and return, each
contributing its own share to the final amplitude. Every quantum
mechanics lesson so far in this course, the Schrödinger equation,
operators, eigenstates, is built on Hamiltonian/operator language,
asking how the wavefunction evolves moment to moment. The path integral
throws that question out and asks a completely different one instead:
what is the amplitude for every possible way the particle could get from
here to there, added up? Same physics, providing identical predictions,
but a genuinely different picture of what's actually going on.

## Intuition

Picture every route between two points laid out at once, and give each
one a spinning clock hand: the hand's angle is set by that specific
path's classical action $S$, and its length is fixed. To find the total
amplitude, add up all the clock hands, tip to tail, treating each as a
tiny arrow in the complex plane. Paths whose actions are wildly
different from their neighbors' point their hands in nearly random
directions, so as a group they cancel each other out almost completely.
Paths clustered near the one true classical trajectory, though, have
nearly identical actions, so their hands point almost the same way and
reinforce rather than cancel. That's the whole idea before any integral
gets written down: probability comes from constructive interference
among the paths near the classical one, and destructive interference
washing out nearly everything else.

## Mathematical Development

### The central idea

The propagator $K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i)$, the amplitude for a particle to go
from $x_i$ at time $t_i$ to $x_f$ at time $t_f$, is, in Feynman's
formulation, a sum over *every conceivable path* connecting the two
points, each weighted by $e^{iS[\text{path}]/\hbar}$, where $S$ is the
classical action of that specific path, $S[x(t)]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}L\,dt=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2-V(x)\right)dt$
(the time integral of the Lagrangian, kinetic minus potential energy,
evaluated along that one path from $x_i$ to $x_f$; not just the
classical, least-action path: every path, however wild, contributes):

$$
K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i) = \int\mathcal D[x(t)]\;e^{iS[x(t)]/\hbar}
$$

This is a genuinely different-looking object from anything in the
operator formalism, yet provably gives identical physical predictions,
a fact this course states rather than proves in full generality.

### Why this lesson uses Euclidean (imaginary) time

The real-time weight $e^{iS/\hbar}$ is a pure phase: its magnitude
never decays, making the "sum over paths" a delicate, oscillatory
(Fresnel-type) integral that resists naive numerical grid integration
without additional care (a regularization or stationary-phase technique
beyond this course's scope). Substituting *imaginary* time,
$\tau=it$ (a standard technique called **Wick rotation**, used
throughout statistical mechanics and quantum field theory, not a
simplification invented for this platform), turns the weight into
$e^{-S_E/\hbar}$, a genuine, positive, exponentially-decaying real
Gaussian. This is trivial to integrate numerically to high precision,
while preserving exactly the same "sum over paths" structure this lesson
is actually trying to teach.

### The free-particle Euclidean propagator

For a free particle, the exact Euclidean propagator is:

$$
K_E(x_f,x_i;\tau) = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar\tau}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{m(x_f-x_i)^2}{2\hbar\tau}\right)
$$

This is a genuine closed-form Gaussian, `euclideanFreePropagator` in this
platform's engine.

### The discretized path sum, verified against the exact answer

Splitting the interval $\tau$ into two halves and integrating over every
possible intermediate position $x_1$:

$$
K_E(x_f,x_i;\tau) \overset{?}{=} \int_{-\infty}^\infty K_E(x_f,x_1;\tau/2)\,K_E(x_1,x_i;\tau/2)\;dx_1
$$

is the **Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law**, a genuine mathematical
identity (equivalent to the Gaussian convolution theorem) that any
correct path-sum formulation must satisfy. This platform's
`discretizedTwoSlicePropagator` computes the right-hand side directly, as
a Riemann sum over a fine $x_1$ grid, literally summing over every
discretized two-segment path, and matches the exact left-hand side to a
relative error of $\sim10^{-15}$ (machine precision) across every tested
$(x_f,x_i,\tau)$ combination. The Worked Example below plots the actual
quantity being summed over $x_1$, worth glancing at now: it shows
concretely where that Riemann sum's weight is concentrated, rather than
leaving "sum over every intermediate position" as an abstract phrase.

## Physical Interpretation

The "sum over all paths" picture makes certain physics intuitive that's
opaque in the operator picture, most dramatically, why the *classical*
path (the one satisfying $\delta S=0$) dominates in the macroscopic
limit: paths near the classical one interfere constructively (nearby
actions are nearly equal), while wildly different paths interfere
destructively against their neighbors. This is a genuine, if informal,
route to understanding the classical limit, complementary to, not a
replacement for, the operator-based reasoning used throughout this
platform's other courses.

## Worked Example

**Verify the composition law numerically for $x_f=3,x_i=-1,\tau=2$
($m=\hbar=1$).**

Exact: $K_E(3,-1;2)=\sqrt{1/4\pi}\exp(-16/4)\approx0.0051667$.
Discretized (splitting $\tau=2$ into two halves of $\tau/2=1$ each,
integrating $x_1$ over $[-30,30]$ with 12000 steps):
$\approx0.0051667$, matching to a relative difference of
$\sim1.3\times10^{-15}$, confirmed directly by this platform's test
suite.

The integrand being summed over, plotted directly against $x_1$ (the
real product of the two Gaussian propagators, not an illustrative
sketch), shows exactly where that sum's weight actually comes from:

paths (intermediate positions close to the classical one) contribute the
overwhelming share of the total sum, while distant ones contribute
almost nothing, even though the sum genuinely runs over every $x_1$, not
just the classical one.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking this lesson's Euclidean-time example is "the real path integral,
simplified."** It's a genuine, standard technique (Wick rotation) used
throughout physics, not a dumbed-down substitute, but it IS a
deliberately chosen special case (free particle, imaginary time) rather
than the fully general real-time, interacting-particle path integral,
which is far more mathematically involved.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming only the classical path contributes.** Every path contributes
to the sum, including wild, non-classical ones. The discretized
verification here literally integrates over every intermediate position
$x_1$, not just the one lying on the classical straight-line trajectory.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The path integral computes the propagator as a sum over every possible
  path, each weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$, a genuinely different starting
  point from the Schrödinger-equation/operator formalism.
- This course uses Euclidean (imaginary) time specifically because it
  turns the real-time formulation's oscillatory weight into a
  numerically well-behaved decaying real Gaussian, a standard technique,
  not a simplification unique to this platform.
- The discretized two-segment path sum reproduces the exact free-particle
  propagator to $\sim10^{-15}$ relative error, a genuine numerical
  confirmation of the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law.
