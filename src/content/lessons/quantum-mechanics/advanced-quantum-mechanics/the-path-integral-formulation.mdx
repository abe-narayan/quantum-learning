## Motivation

Every lesson before this one used the same language. There is a state,
there is a Hamiltonian, and the <Term id="schrodinger-equation">Schrödinger equation</Term> says how the state
changes from one instant to the next. Operators and <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstates</Term> all hang
off that framing.

Feynman's formulation asks a different question and never mentions a
state evolving. Given that the particle was at $x_i$ and ended at $x_f$,
what is the <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> for the transition? The answer is a sum over every
conceivable path connecting them, with the straight classical line
carrying no special status: it contributes one term among infinitely
many.

The two formulations agree on every prediction. What differs is what each
one makes obvious, and the classical limit is the clearest case: in the
operator picture it takes work to see why macroscopic objects follow
Newton's laws, and in the path integral it falls out of an interference
argument you can sketch on a napkin.

## Intuition

Picture every route between two points laid out at once, and give each
one a spinning clock hand: the hand's angle is set by that specific
path's classical action $S$, and its length is fixed. To find the total
amplitude, add up all the clock hands, tip to tail, treating each as a
tiny arrow in the complex plane. Paths whose actions are wildly
different from their neighbors' point their hands in nearly random
directions, so as a group they cancel each other out almost completely.
Paths clustered near the one true classical trajectory, though, have
nearly identical actions, so their hands point almost the same way and
reinforce rather than cancel.

That is the whole idea before any integral is written down. Probability
comes from constructive interference among paths near the classical one
and destructive interference washing out everything else. Notice what
this explains: the classical trajectory is not selected by a law that
forbids the others, it is what survives when the others cancel. Shrink
$\hbar$ and the cancellation becomes more brutal, which is why large
objects appear to follow one path exactly.

The panels below make that clock-hand picture concrete, though only
illustratively: the left panel draws a batch of sample paths between two
fixed endpoints, each one's "wiggliness" standing in for how different it
is from the straight classical path, and colors every path by its
resulting phase. The right panel takes those same phases as unit-length
clock hands and adds them tip to tail, with the resultant arrow's length
read off as the total amplitude. Drag the effective-$\hbar$ slider down
and each path's phase gets amplified, sending the wilder paths' hands
spinning off in nearly random directions that cancel almost completely;
drag it up and those phases compress back toward the classical path's,
so the hands line up and the resultant arrow grows, the same
classical-limit argument stated in words above. (This is a simplified
stand-in for the metaphor, not the lesson's real Euclidean path-integral
computation, which the Worked Example below plots directly.)

## Mathematical Development

### The central idea

The propagator $K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i)$, the amplitude for a particle to go
from $x_i$ at time $t_i$ to $x_f$ at time $t_f$, is, in Feynman's
formulation, a sum over *every conceivable path* connecting the two
points, each weighted by $e^{iS[\text{path}]/\hbar}$, where $S$ is the
classical action of that specific path, $S[x(t)]=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}L\,dt=\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2-V(x)\right)dt$
(the time integral of the Lagrangian, kinetic minus potential energy,
evaluated along that one path from $x_i$ to $x_f$; not just the
classical, least-action path: every path, however wild, contributes):

$$
K(x_f,t_f;x_i,t_i) = \int\mathcal D[x(t)]\;e^{iS[x(t)]/\hbar}
$$

Nothing in the operator formalism resembles this, and yet the two are
provably equivalent. This course states that equivalence rather than
proving it in general; the numerical check further down verifies a
specific consequence of it instead.

### Why this lesson uses Euclidean (imaginary) time

The real-time weight $e^{iS/\hbar}$ is a pure phase: its magnitude
never decays, making the "sum over paths" a delicate, oscillatory
(Fresnel-type) integral that resists naive numerical grid integration
without additional care (a regularization or stationary-phase technique
beyond this course's scope). Substituting *imaginary* time,
$\tau=it$ (a standard technique called **Wick rotation**, used
throughout statistical mechanics and quantum field theory, not a
simplification invented for this platform), turns the weight into
$e^{-S_E/\hbar}$, a positive, exponentially decaying real Gaussian.
That integrates numerically to high precision with no special handling,
while leaving the sum-over-paths structure intact. The price is that
phases become weights: in Euclidean time paths are suppressed rather than
cancelled, so the interference story from the Intuition section survives
as an analogy rather than as arithmetic. The classical path still
dominates, for the related reason that it minimizes $S_E$ and so carries
the largest weight.

### The free-particle Euclidean propagator

For a free particle, the exact Euclidean propagator is:

$$
K_E(x_f,x_i;\tau) = \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar\tau}}\;\exp\!\left(-\frac{m(x_f-x_i)^2}{2\hbar\tau}\right)
$$

This is a closed-form Gaussian, computed directly by this platform's
`euclideanFreePropagator`.

### The discretized path sum, verified against the exact answer

Splitting the interval $\tau$ into two halves and integrating over every
possible intermediate position $x_1$:

$$
K_E(x_f,x_i;\tau) \overset{?}{=} \int_{-\infty}^\infty K_E(x_f,x_1;\tau/2)\,K_E(x_1,x_i;\tau/2)\;dx_1
$$

is the **Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law**, a mathematical identity
(equivalent to the Gaussian convolution theorem) that any correct
path-sum formulation must satisfy. This platform's
`discretizedTwoSlicePropagator` computes the right-hand side as a Riemann
sum over a fine $x_1$ grid, which is a sum over every discretized
two-segment path, and it matches the exact left-hand side to a relative
error around $10^{-15}$, machine precision, for every tested
$(x_f,x_i,\tau)$.

Two segments is the smallest nontrivial case of the real construction. A
genuine path integral repeats the split $N$ times and takes $N\to\infty$,
integrating over $N-1$ intermediate positions; the worked example below
does the $N=2$ version and plots the integrand, so "sum over every
intermediate position" becomes a curve rather than a phrase.

## Physical Interpretation

The sum-over-paths picture makes one thing easy that the operator picture
makes hard: why the classical path, the one satisfying $\delta S=0$,
dominates for macroscopic objects. Paths near it have nearly equal
actions, so their phases align and add. Paths far from it have actions
that differ wildly between neighbours, so their phases scatter and
cancel. Stationary action is not a separate principle here; it is the
condition for a path to have neighbours that agree with it.

The formulation also travels further than this course does. Path
integrals are the standard language of quantum field theory, where
Feynman diagrams are a bookkeeping scheme for terms in such a sum, and of
statistical mechanics, where the Euclidean version below is not a
computational trick but the partition function itself.

## Worked Example

**Verify the composition law numerically for $x_f=3,x_i=-1,\tau=2$
($m=\hbar=1$).**

Exact: $K_E(3,-1;2)=\sqrt{1/4\pi}\exp(-16/4)\approx0.0051667$.
Discretized (splitting $\tau=2$ into two halves of $\tau/2=1$ each,
integrating $x_1$ over $[-30,30]$ with 12000 steps):
$\approx0.0051667$, matching to a relative difference of
$\sim1.3\times10^{-15}$, confirmed directly by this platform's test
suite.

$x_1$ ranges over every real number, given that $x_i=-1$ and $x_f=3$.

That is the computed version of the Physical Interpretation's claim.
Every $x_1$ is in the sum, including $x_1=-8$, which corresponds to a
particle that sprints backwards and then returns. Its contribution is not
zero; it is small. The classical answer emerges from weighting, not from
exclusion, and that distinction is the entire content of the
formulation.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking this lesson's Euclidean-time example is "the real path integral,
simplified."** It's a standard technique (Wick rotation) used throughout
physics, not a dumbed-down substitute. It IS, however, a deliberately
chosen special case (free particle, imaginary time) rather than the
fully general real-time, interacting-particle path integral, which is
far more mathematically involved.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming only the classical path contributes.** Every path contributes
to the sum, including wild, non-classical ones. The discretized
verification here literally integrates over every intermediate position
$x_1$, not just the one lying on the classical straight-line trajectory.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The path integral computes the propagator as a sum over every possible
  path, each weighted by $e^{iS/\hbar}$, a fundamentally different
  starting point from the Schrödinger-equation/operator formalism.
- This course uses Euclidean (imaginary) time specifically because it
  turns the real-time formulation's oscillatory weight into a
  numerically well-behaved decaying real Gaussian, a standard technique,
  not a simplification unique to this platform.
- The discretized two-segment path sum reproduces the exact free-particle
  propagator to $\sim10^{-15}$ relative error, a direct numerical
  confirmation of the Chapman-Kolmogorov composition law.
