<LessonHook eyebrow="Nobody has ever built an isolated system">
A perfectly isolated system evolving unitarily forever is an idealization nobody has ever built in a lab. Every real system eventually interacts with something outside itself: stray photons, a measuring apparatus, thermal vibrations in the mounting hardware.
</LessonHook>

## The property that makes unitary evolution fatal here

Entanglement, Mixed States & Bell Tests built density matrices and gave
them a <Term id="unitary-operator">unitary evolution</Term> rule, $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$. That rule has a
property which turns out to be fatal for describing anything real: it
cannot change $\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)$. A state that starts pure stays pure
for ever.

Real states do not. Left alone for long enough, every laboratory qubit
decoheres, and its purity is measurably lower afterwards than before. So
the rule is not merely incomplete; applied to an open system it is
wrong, and the correction is the subject of this lesson.

The plot below is the whole problem in one picture. All three curves
start from $|+\rangle$ and apply one step of evolution at a time: a
unitary rotation, an amplitude-damping channel, and a dephasing channel.

worth working out before reading on.

## Past unitary evolution, and the one constraint left

### Why unitary evolution alone isn't enough

$\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$ preserves <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> exactly, and the one-line proof is
worth having: $\mathrm{Tr}\big((U\rho U^\dagger)^2\big) = \mathrm{Tr}(U\rho
U^\dagger U\rho U^\dagger) = \mathrm{Tr}(U\rho^2U^\dagger) =
\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)$, using $U^\dagger U=I$ once and the cyclic property
of the trace once.

No choice of $U$ escapes that. So if purity drops in the laboratory, the
system's evolution is not being generated by any unitary acting on the
system alone. The resolution is not to abandon unitarity: the system plus
its environment does evolve unitarily. It is that discarding the
environment, by tracing it out, leaves behind a map on the system that
need not be unitary at all.

Amplitude damping models a qubit losing energy to a cold environment, so
its destination is not much of a mystery: it ends up in $|0\rangle$,
which is pure, and that is the $1$ the curve climbs back to. The dip on
the way there is the part worth stopping for.

### The Kraus operator sum representation

Any physically valid (completely positive, trace-preserving) evolution
of an open system can be written as:

$$
\rho \to \mathcal E(\rho) = \sum_k K_k\,\rho\,K_k^\dagger
$$

for some set of **<Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus operators</Term>** $\{K_k\}$ satisfying $\sum_kK_k^\dagger
K_k=I$, the **trace-preservation condition**, which guarantees
$\mathrm{Tr}(\mathcal E(\rho))=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho)$ so that total
probability stays $1$.

A single unitary is the one-operator case, $K_0=U$, where the condition
reduces to $U^\dagger U=I$ and is satisfied automatically. So the Kraus
form contains unitary evolution rather than competing with it, and the
interesting channels are the ones needing two or more operators. Purity
can only change when there is more than one term in the sum, which is
visible directly in the formula: with one term the map is a similarity
transformation, and with several it is a mixture.

### Verified directly against the trace-preservation condition

This platform's `isTracePreserving` checks $\sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k=I$ entry
by entry. It confirms both channels this course builds, amplitude damping
and dephasing, and it also rejects a deliberately broken example: a
single Kraus operator scaled by $2$, whose sum comes out $4I$. That
second test matters more than the first, because a check that only ever
returns `true` proves nothing.

The condition has a geometric consequence you can watch. A qubit's Bloch
vector has length $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, and a length above $1$ would not
be a density matrix at all. Amplitude damping starting from $|+\rangle$
drags the vector from the equator up to the north pole, and along the way
the length dips below $1$ and comes back: $0.866$ at its shallowest,
around step $7$, the same place purity bottomed out.

## Each operator is one thing the environment might have done

Each $K_k$ stands for one thing the environment could have done. For
amplitude damping, $K_1$ is "a photon was emitted" and $K_0$ is "no
photon was emitted"; nothing measures which, and no record is kept. The
sum $\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ is the system's state averaged over the
branches, weighted by their amplitudes.

That averaging is the whole source of purity change. A single branch
would be a unitary-like map and would preserve purity; a mixture over
branches nobody tracked is exactly what a mixed state is.

On hardware this is not a metaphor. A superconducting qubit couples to
its control lines and to phonons in the substrate; a trapped ion couples
to stray electric-field noise. In both cases the environment records
which branch occurred and the experimenter cannot read it, which is why
the $T_1$ and $T_2$ decay curves measured routinely on every processor
are these channels in another notation.

## Checking amplitude damping is trace-preserving

**Confirm the amplitude damping channel, $K_0=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&\sqrt{1-\gamma}\end{pmatrix}$,
$K_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sqrt\gamma\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, is trace-preserving.**

$$
K_0^\dagger K_0 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1-\gamma\end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1^\dagger K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\gamma\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
K_0^\dagger K_0 + K_1^\dagger K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I
$$

This holds for every $\gamma\in[0,1]$, confirmed directly by this
platform's `isTracePreserving(amplitudeDampingChannel(gamma))`, which
returns `true` across the full range.

The same identity, seen rather than derived. Each preset below shows
$K_0$, $K_1$, and $K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1$ for a channel the
platform computes numerically, with the sum's diagonal highlighted. Note
how different the two channels' individual operators are, and how
identical their sums come out.

## Not every set of matrices is a channel

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any set of matrices can serve as Kraus operators.** The
trace-preservation condition $\sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k=I$ is a real
constraint. An arbitrarily chosen matrix set (like a single operator
scaled by 2, this lesson's counter-example) generally fails it and does
not describe a physical channel.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking open-system evolution "violates" quantum mechanics' unitarity
postulate.** It doesn't. The FULL system-plus-environment evolves
unitarily; the Kraus representation is exactly what that global unitary
evolution looks like once the environment's degrees of freedom are
traced out (not tracked). Nothing about quantum mechanics' foundations
is being bent.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show that a single unitary $K_0=U$ automatically satisfies the
   trace-preservation condition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With one term in the sum, the condition reads

   $$
   \sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = K_0^\dagger K_0 = U^\dagger U = I
   $$

   which is the definition of unitarity. Nothing further to check.

   The reason this is worth doing rather than assuming is that it settles
   the relationship between the two frameworks. The Kraus form is not a
   rival to unitary evolution or a correction applied on top of it. It is
   a generalization that contains it as the one-operator case, so nothing
   proved earlier about closed systems is at risk here.

   It also isolates where non-unitary behaviour can come from. With a
   single term the map is $\rho\to K\rho K^\dagger$, a similarity
   transformation that cannot change purity. Everything interesting about
   channels lives in the sum having two or more terms.

   </details>

2. Why does $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$ always preserve purity exactly,
   while a general Kraus channel need not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because with one unitary the $U^\dagger U$ pairs cancel in the middle
   and the trace's cyclic property carries the rest:

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}\big((U\rho U^\dagger)^2\big) = \mathrm{Tr}(U\rho \underbrace{U^\dagger U}_{I}\rho U^\dagger) = \mathrm{Tr}(U\rho^2U^\dagger) = \mathrm{Tr}(\rho^2)
   $$

   With several Kraus operators there is nothing to cancel. Squaring
   $\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ produces cross terms
   $K_j\rho K_j^\dagger K_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ with $j\ne k$, and no
   identity collapses them.

   The physical account is the one worth carrying. Unitarity is not being
   abandoned: the system together with its environment evolves under a
   single unitary and the *joint* purity is preserved exactly. What the
   channel does is trace out the environment, discarding the correlations
   the interaction built between the two. Purity of the whole is
   conserved; purity of a part is not, and the missing purity has gone
   into entanglement with something you are no longer describing.

   One correction to a common overstatement. A channel does not always
   *reduce* purity. Amplitude damping is the counterexample plotted at the
   top of this lesson: it drags every state toward $|0\rangle$, which is
   pure, so purity ends up back near $1$. Channels that can never decrease
   entropy are the unital ones, those satisfying
   $\sum_kK_kK_k^\dagger=I$ as well, which is true of dephasing and false
   of amplitude damping. Purity is preserved under unitaries and merely
   unconstrained under channels.

   </details>

3. Verify by direct matrix multiplication that the dephasing channel's
   $K_0,K_1$ (given in this course's engine) satisfy
   $K_0^\dagger K_0+K_1^\dagger K_1=I$ for $\lambda=0.4$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The engine's dephasing operators are
   $K_0=\sqrt{1-\lambda/2}\,I$ and $K_1=\sqrt{\lambda/2}\,Z$. At
   $\lambda=0.4$ that is $\sqrt{0.8}$ and $\sqrt{0.2}$:

   $$
   K_0 = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{0.8}&0\\0&\sqrt{0.8}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{0.2}&0\\0&-\sqrt{0.2}\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Both are real and diagonal, so the dagger is the transpose and each
   product just squares the diagonal entries. The minus sign in $K_1$
   squares away:

   $$
   K_0^\dagger K_0 = \begin{pmatrix}0.8&0\\0&0.8\end{pmatrix}, \qquad K_1^\dagger K_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0.2&0\\0&0.2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{sum} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I
   $$

   The general statement is cleaner than the numbers. Since $I^2=Z^2=I$,
   the sum is $(1-\lambda/2)I+(\lambda/2)I=I$ for every
   $\lambda\in[0,1]$, so $0.4$ is not a special case and the check is
   really about the identity $Z^2=I$.

   Notice that the sign the calculation discards is the entire physical
   content of the channel. $K_1$ applies $Z$, flipping the phase of
   $|1\rangle$ relative to $|0\rangle$, and it is that random phase flip
   applied with probability $\lambda/2$ that destroys the off-diagonal
   elements of $\rho$. Trace preservation is blind to it, which is exactly
   why passing the trace-preservation test tells you a channel is legal
   and nothing about what it does.

   </details>

4. Physically, what does each individual $K_k$ represent, and why does
   summing over all of them (rather than picking just one) correctly
   describe the system when no measurement of the environment actually
   occurs?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Each $K_k$ is one thing the environment could have done, and the sum is
   the state you are entitled to write down when nobody looked to see
   which.

   For amplitude damping the two are concrete: $K_1$ is "a photon was
   emitted" and $K_0$ is "no photon was emitted." If you *did* watch the
   environment and saw outcome $k$, the right description would be a
   single branch, renormalized:

   $$
   \rho \to \frac{K_k\rho K_k^\dagger}{\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\right)} \quad\text{with probability } \mathrm{Tr}\!\left(K_k\rho K_k^\dagger\right)
   $$

   and that map preserves purity, being a single-operator map. The
   unconditional state is those branches weighted by their probabilities,
   which is what $\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$ is: the probability factors
   cancel against the renormalizations and leave the plain sum. Purity
   falls because averaging over unread branches is precisely what makes a
   state mixed.

   Picking just one term would be asserting a fact you do not have. It
   would predict a pure state where the laboratory measures a mixed one,
   and it would get the $T_1$ and $T_2$ curves wrong on every real device.

   There is a caveat that keeps this from being taken too literally, and
   it is the most interesting thing in this lesson. The Kraus
   decomposition of a channel is not unique. Any set
   $K'_j=\sum_k u_{jk}K_k$ with $u$ unitary gives the identical map on
   every $\rho$, so the same physical channel can be told as "emitted or
   not emitted" or as a completely different pair of alternatives. The
   channel is a fact; the story about which branch the environment took is
   not. It depends on what basis you would have measured the environment
   in, and if you never measured it, no such basis was picked. Read $K_k$
   as one useful way to decompose the map, not as a hidden record of what
   really happened.

   </details>

## Summary

- Open (environment-coupled) systems cannot, in general, evolve purely
  unitarily: real purity loss requires going beyond
  $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$.
- The Kraus representation $\rho\to\sum_kK_k\rho K_k^\dagger$, subject to
  $\sum_kK_k^\dagger K_k=I$, is the general framework, a strict
  generalization of unitary evolution, not a replacement.
- Purity falling is a symptom of decoherence, not a definition of it:
  amplitude damping destroys a superposition while returning the qubit
  to a pure state.
- This platform's `isTracePreserving` verifies the condition directly,
  confirmed for amplitude damping and dephasing and rejected for a
  deliberately invalid counter-example.
