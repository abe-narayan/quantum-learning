## Motivation

The Hydrogen Atom course found $n^2$ degenerate orbitals for shell $n$
(Hydrogen Energy Levels). The Pauli Exclusion Principle course found
that each single-particle state, spatial orbital *and* spin, can hold
at most one electron. Multiplying these two already-established facts
together, with no new physics, gives the entire logical basis for the
periodic table's shell structure.

## Mathematical Development

### Shell capacity: 2n²

Shell $n$ has $n^2$ distinct spatial orbitals (Hydrogen Energy Levels'
degeneracy count: $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2$). Each spatial orbital,
by exclusion, can hold at most 2 electrons, one spin-up, one spin-down
(these are genuinely different complete single-particle states, as the
previous lesson established). So shell $n$ holds at most:

$$
2n^2 \text{ electrons}
$$

$n=1$: $2(1)^2=2$ electrons (fills 1s). $n=2$: $2(2)^2=8$ electrons
(fills 2s and 2p together). $n=3$: $2(3)^2=18$, though in real multi-electron
atoms the filling order gets more complicated starting around here (a
genuine complication this introduction names but doesn't resolve; see
below).

### What's exactly hydrogen-like, and what isn't

The orbital *shapes* and *degeneracy counts* used above come directly
from Hydrogen Energy Levels and Orbitals & Quantum Numbers, genuinely
reused, not re-derived. What's *not* reused: hydrogen's exact
$l$-independence of energy (Hydrogen Energy Levels' special result for
the pure $1/r$ potential) breaks down in a multi-electron atom, because
each electron now also feels the other electrons' repulsion, not just
the nucleus's attraction. This repulsion is not spherically symmetric
in the same simple $1/r$ way, and, critically, it does depend on $l$
(a $2s$ electron, closer to the nucleus, feels the nuclear attraction
more directly than a $2p$ electron of the same $n$, which is
"screened" more by inner electrons). This is exactly why real multi-electron
filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, ... rather than simply
increasing $n$.

## Physical Interpretation

This course does not attempt to solve the true multi-electron
Hamiltonian (which has no exact closed-form solution even for helium,
the two-electron case: a genuinely hard many-body problem, active
research territory for larger atoms). What this introduction provides is
the *qualitative* structure, shell capacities and the shape of the
filling logic, using hydrogen's exact orbitals as an approximate
single-particle basis, which is both an honest, standard approximation
and as far as this course's scope goes.

## Worked Example

**Using the shell-filling picture, write the electron configuration for
carbon (6 electrons).**

Fill lowest shells first: 1s holds 2 (fills, using its full $2(1)^2=2$
capacity). Remaining 4 electrons go into $n=2$: 2s holds 2 more (full,
$2(0+1)^2$... more precisely 2s alone holds 2, its own sub-shell
capacity), leaving 2 electrons for 2p. Configuration:
$1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2$, matching carbon's known configuration, built here
entirely from the $n^2$-orbital-count-times-2-for-spin logic, no
periodic table lookup required.

carbon's outer shell isn't closed and it forms 4 bonds rather than being
chemically inert.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming multi-electron atoms fill strictly by increasing n.** Real
atoms deviate starting around $n=3$ (4s fills before 3d, for instance),
a direct consequence of electron-electron screening breaking hydrogen's
exact $l$-independence, as this lesson's Mathematical Development
section explains. This course's $2n^2$ formula is exactly right for
*capacity*, but filling *order* needs the screening correction this
introduction only names, not derives.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking multi-electron atoms are "solved" the same way hydrogen was.**
Hydrogen's Schrödinger equation has an exact closed-form solution;
multi-electron atoms, even helium, do not. This course uses hydrogen's
orbitals only as an approximate starting basis, an honest scope
limitation stated explicitly rather than glossed over.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons: $n^2$ spatial orbitals
  (Hydrogen Energy Levels) times 2 (exclusion's spin-up/spin-down limit
  per orbital).
- This reuses hydrogen's orbitals as an approximate single-particle
  basis, genuinely useful for electron configurations, but not an exact
  solution to the true multi-electron Hamiltonian.
- Electron-electron repulsion (screening) breaks hydrogen's exact
  $l$-independence of energy, which is why real filling order departs
  from strict increasing-$n$ starting around $n=3$.
