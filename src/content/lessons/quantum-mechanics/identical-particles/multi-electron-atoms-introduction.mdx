## Motivation

Two results from earlier in this pillar are about to be multiplied
together. Hydrogen Energy Levels counted $n^2$ degenerate spatial
orbitals in shell $n$. The previous lesson showed that each complete
single-particle state, spatial orbital *and* spin together, admits at
most one electron.

Neither result mentions chemistry. Their product is $2n^2$, and it gives
$2, 8, 18, 32$: the row lengths of the periodic table, obtained from a
degeneracy count and a sign flip under exchange.

This lesson does that multiplication carefully, then is equally careful
about what it does not deliver. Capacities come out exactly right;
filling *order* does not, and the reason why is more interesting than the
formula.

## Mathematical Development

### Shell capacity: 2n²

Shell $n$ has $n^2$ distinct spatial orbitals (Hydrogen Energy Levels'
degeneracy count: $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2$). Each spatial orbital,
by <Term id="pauli-exclusion-principle">exclusion</Term>, can hold at most 2 electrons, one spin-up, one spin-down
(these count as different complete single-particle states, as the
previous lesson established). So shell $n$ holds at most:

$$
2n^2 \text{ electrons}
$$

Within a shell, chemists split the $n^2$ orbitals by $l$ into
**subshells** labeled s, p, d ($l=0,1,2$), each holding $2(2l+1)$
electrons; the shell totals below are just those pieces added up.
$n=1$: $2(1)^2=2$ electrons, filling 1s. $n=2$: $2(2)^2=8$, filling 2s
and 2p together. $n=3$: $2(3)^2=18$, and this is where filling order
starts to depart from shell order in real atoms, for reasons taken up
below.

Finite capacity is most of chemistry's answer to why noble gases exist.
An atom whose outermost occupied subshell is completely full, helium at
$2$, neon at $10$, has no partly-occupied orbital through which to share
or accept an electron, so it sits inertly at the end of its row. Carbon,
with 2p only two-sixths full, has four vacancies and bonds in four
directions.

It is also why reactivity is *periodic* rather than a separate fact about
each element. The pattern of "one short of closing" and "one past
closing" repeats every time a set of slots fills up, so elements at the
same position in successive rows behave alike. Mendeleev arranged the
table by that repetition sixty years before anyone could explain it, and
the explanation, when it came, was the two lines of this section.

### What's hydrogen-like here, and what isn't

The orbital *shapes* and *degeneracy counts* used above come directly
from Hydrogen Energy Levels and Orbitals & Quantum Numbers, carried over
wholesale, not re-derived. What's *not* reused: hydrogen's exact
$l$-independence of energy (Hydrogen Energy Levels' special result for
the pure $1/r$ potential) breaks down in a multi-electron atom, because
each electron now also feels the other electrons' repulsion, not just
the nucleus's attraction. This repulsion is not spherically symmetric
in the same simple $1/r$ way, and, critically, it does depend on $l$
(a $2s$ electron, closer to the nucleus, feels the nuclear attraction
more directly than a $2p$ electron of the same $n$, which is
"screened" more by inner electrons). This is why the real
filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, ... rather than simply
increasing $n$.

## Physical Interpretation

This course does not solve the true multi-electron Hamiltonian, and
neither does anyone else in closed form. Helium, with two electrons, is
already beyond exact solution, and estimating ground-state energies for
larger atoms remains active research; it is one of the main targets
quantum computing hopes to address.

What this introduction supplies is the structure: shell capacities, and
the logic of filling, with hydrogen's orbitals borrowed as an approximate
single-particle <Term id="basis">basis</Term>. That borrowing is the standard first move in
atomic physics, and it is worth being clear that it is an approximation
rather than a derivation, because the next section is about exactly where
it starts to fail.

## Worked Example

**Using the shell-filling picture, write the electron configuration for
carbon (6 electrons).**

Work subshell by subshell, using the capacity $2(2l+1)$ for a subshell of
angular momentum $l$.

1s ($l=0$) holds $2(1)=2$ electrons and fills, leaving 4. 2s ($l=0$)
holds $2$ and fills, leaving 2. 2p ($l=1$) has capacity $2(3)=6$ and
receives the last 2, so it stops one-third full. The configuration is

$$
1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2
$$

which is carbon's, arrived at from the orbital count and the factor of
two for spin, with no periodic table consulted. Note that the subshell
capacities $2, 2, 6$ sum to the shell capacities the section above
derived: $2$ for $n=1$ and $2+6=8$ for $n=2$.

### Why 4s slips under 3d

The bar chart above shows *that* 4s fills before 3d; this diagram shows
*why*, using the same screening argument from Mathematical Development.
In a pure $1/r$ potential, energy depends only on $n$, so 3s, 3p and 3d
are degenerate and 4s sits above all three. Toggle to "Real
multi-electron atom" and screening splits that degeneracy: a 3s electron
spends more time near the nucleus, sees less shielding from the inner
electrons, and drops; 3d, which is held away from the nucleus by its own
angular momentum barrier, is shielded most and drops least. The ordering
rearranges far enough that 4s ends up below 3d and fills first, despite
the larger $n$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming multi-electron atoms fill strictly by increasing n.** Real
atoms deviate starting around $n=3$ (4s fills before 3d, for instance),
a direct consequence of electron-electron screening breaking hydrogen's
exact $l$-independence, as this lesson's Mathematical Development
section explains. This course's $2n^2$ formula is exactly right for
*capacity*, but filling *order* needs the screening correction this
introduction only names, not derives.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking multi-electron atoms are "solved" the same way hydrogen was.**
Hydrogen's Schrödinger equation has an exact closed-form solution;
multi-electron atoms, even helium, do not. This course uses hydrogen's
orbitals only as an approximate starting basis, an honest scope
limitation stated explicitly rather than glossed over.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons: $n^2$ spatial orbitals
  (Hydrogen Energy Levels) times 2 (exclusion's spin-up/spin-down limit
  per orbital).
- This reuses hydrogen's orbitals as an approximate single-particle
  basis, useful for electron configurations, but not an exact solution
  to the true multi-electron Hamiltonian.
- Electron-electron repulsion (screening) breaks hydrogen's exact
  $l$-independence of energy, which is why real filling order departs
  from strict increasing-$n$ starting around $n=3$.
