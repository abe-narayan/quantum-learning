## Two earlier results, about to be multiplied

Two results from earlier in this track are about to be multiplied
together. Hydrogen Energy Levels counted $n^2$ degenerate spatial
orbitals in shell $n$. The previous lesson showed that each complete
single-particle state, spatial orbital *and* spin together, admits at
most one electron.

Neither result mentions chemistry. Their product is $2n^2$, which gives
$2, 8, 18, 32$: every row length the periodic table uses, obtained from a
degeneracy count and a sign flip under exchange.

*Every length it uses*, note, not the order it uses them in. The table's
rows actually run $2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32$, with each of the larger
numbers arriving a row later than a shell count alone would put it. So
this lesson does the multiplication carefully, then is equally careful
about what it does not deliver. Capacities come out exactly right;
filling *order* does not, and the reason why is more interesting than the
formula.

## Where 2n-squared comes from

### Shell capacity: 2n²

Shell $n$ has $n^2$ distinct spatial orbitals (Hydrogen Energy Levels'
degeneracy count: $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2$). Each spatial orbital,
by <Term id="pauli-exclusion-principle">exclusion</Term>, can hold at most 2 electrons, one spin-up, one spin-down
(these count as different complete single-particle states, as the
previous lesson established). So shell $n$ holds at most:

$$
2n^2 \text{ electrons}
$$

Within a shell, chemists split the $n^2$ orbitals by $l$ into
**subshells** labeled s, p, d ($l=0,1,2$), each holding $2(2l+1)$
electrons; the shell totals below are just those pieces added up.
$n=1$: $2(1)^2=2$ electrons, filling 1s. $n=2$: $2(2)^2=8$, filling 2s
and 2p together. $n=3$: $2(3)^2=18$, and this is where filling order
starts to depart from shell order in real atoms, for reasons taken up
below.

Finite capacity is most of chemistry's answer to why noble gases exist.
An atom whose outermost occupied subshell is completely full, helium at
$2$, neon at $10$, has no partly-occupied orbital through which to share
or accept an electron, so it sits inertly at the end of its row. Carbon,
with 2p only two-sixths full, has four vacancies and bonds in four
directions.

It is also why reactivity is *periodic* rather than a separate fact about
each element. The pattern of "one short of closing" and "one past
closing" repeats every time a set of slots fills up, so elements at the
same position in successive rows behave alike. Mendeleev arranged the
table by that repetition sixty years before anyone could explain it, and
the explanation, when it came, was the two lines of this section.

### What's hydrogen-like here, and what isn't

The orbital *shapes* and *degeneracy counts* used above come directly
from Hydrogen Energy Levels and Orbitals & Quantum Numbers, carried over
wholesale, not re-derived. What's *not* reused: hydrogen's exact
$l$-independence of energy (Hydrogen Energy Levels' special result for
the pure $1/r$ potential) breaks down in a multi-electron atom, because
each electron now also feels the other electrons' repulsion on top of
the nucleus's attraction. This repulsion is not spherically symmetric
in the same simple $1/r$ way, and, critically, it does depend on $l$
(a $2s$ electron, closer to the nucleus, feels the nuclear attraction
more directly than a $2p$ electron of the same $n$, which is
"screened" more by inner electrons). This is why the real
filling order is 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, ... rather than simply
increasing $n$.

## Helium is already past exact solution

This course does not solve the true multi-electron Hamiltonian, and
neither does anyone else in closed form. Helium, with two electrons, is
already beyond exact solution, and estimating ground-state energies for
larger atoms remains active research; it is one of the main targets
quantum computing hopes to address.

What this introduction supplies is the structure: shell capacities, and
the logic of filling, with hydrogen's orbitals borrowed as an approximate
single-particle <Term id="basis">basis</Term>. That borrowing is the standard first move in
atomic physics, and it is worth being clear that it is an approximation
and stops short of a derivation, because the next section is about exactly where
it starts to fail.

Which is exactly where the shell count and the periodic table come apart,
and it is worth committing to a number before reading on.

## Filling six electrons into carbon

**Using the shell-filling picture, write the electron configuration for
carbon (6 electrons).**

Work subshell by subshell, using the capacity $2(2l+1)$ for a subshell of
angular momentum $l$.

1s ($l=0$) holds $2(1)=2$ electrons and fills, leaving 4. 2s ($l=0$)
holds $2$ and fills, leaving 2. 2p ($l=1$) has capacity $2(3)=6$ and
receives the last 2, so it stops one-third full. The configuration is

$$
1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^2
$$

which is carbon's, arrived at from the orbital count and the factor of
two for spin, with no periodic table consulted. Note that the subshell
capacities $2, 2, 6$ sum to the shell capacities the section above
derived: $2$ for $n=1$ and $2+6=8$ for $n=2$.

### Why 4s slips under 3d

The bar chart above shows *that* 4s fills before 3d; this diagram shows
*why*, using the same screening argument set out two sections above.
In a pure $1/r$ potential, energy depends only on $n$, so 3s, 3p and 3d
are degenerate and 4s sits above all three. Toggle to "Real
multi-electron atom" and screening splits that degeneracy: a 3s electron
spends more time near the nucleus, sees less shielding from the inner
electrons, and drops; 3d, which is held away from the nucleus by its own
angular momentum barrier, is shielded most and drops least. The ordering
rearranges far enough that 4s ends up below 3d and fills first, despite
the larger $n$.

## Real atoms do not fill strictly by n

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming multi-electron atoms fill strictly by increasing n.** Real
atoms deviate starting around $n=3$ (4s fills before 3d, for instance),
a direct consequence of electron-electron screening breaking hydrogen's
exact $l$-independence, as the level diagram above shows directly. This
course's $2n^2$ formula is exactly right for
*capacity*, but filling *order* needs the screening correction this
introduction only names, not derives.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking multi-electron atoms are "solved" the same way hydrogen was.**
Hydrogen's Schrödinger equation has an exact closed-form solution;
multi-electron atoms, even helium, do not. This course uses hydrogen's
orbitals only as an approximate starting basis, an honest scope
limitation stated explicitly instead of glossed over.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the shell capacity for $n=3$ using $2n^2$, and explain what
   each factor of the formula represents physically.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $2n^2=2(3)^2=18$ electrons.

   The formula is a product of three separate results, and each factor
   came from a different course.

   The $2l+1$ inside the sum is the number of $m$ values at a given $l$,
   which came from the angular momentum ladder running from $-l$ to $+l$
   in unit steps. It is a consequence of rotational symmetry and holds for
   any central potential.

   The range $l=0$ to $n-1$ came from the Coulomb radial equation, where
   $n$ was defined by the series-termination condition as
   $j_{\max}+l+1$, so $l$ can never reach $n$. Summing $2l+1$ over that
   range gives $n^2$ spatial orbitals: at $n=3$, that is $1+3+5=9$.

   The factor of $2$ is spin plus exclusion together. An electron's
   complete state is a spatial orbital tensored with a spin state, there
   are two orthogonal spin states, and exclusion allows one electron per
   complete state. Two per orbital, and no more.

   $9\times2=18$. Worth noticing that the $18$ is a capacity and not a row
   length: the third row of the periodic table holds eight elements, not
   eighteen, because filling moves on to $4s$ before the $n=3$ shell has
   finished. Capacity is exact; the order things fill in is not something
   this counting decides.

   </details>

2. Write the electron configuration for neon (10 electrons), following
   the worked example's method.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6
   $$

   Filling in order with capacity $2(2l+1)$ per subshell: 1s takes $2$,
   leaving $8$; 2s takes $2$, leaving $6$; 2p has capacity
   $2(2\cdot1+1)=6$ and takes all six, leaving none. Every subshell
   involved is filled to capacity, with nothing partly occupied.

   The shell arithmetic closes exactly here: $2$ for $n=1$ plus $8$ for
   $n=2$ is $10$, so neon completes both shells with no electrons left
   over and none missing. That is why it sits at the end of its row,
   glowing inertly in the discharge tube photographed above. With no
   partly filled orbital there is nothing to share, donate, or accept, and
   the next electron would have to start a new shell much further out.

   Neon is also the last element for which the tidy story holds without
   qualification. Its row length and its shell capacity agree because the
   $n=2$ shell happens to finish before filling moves on. From $n=3$
   onward they part company permanently.

   </details>

3. Why can't the shell-filling picture developed here be used to compute
   an *exact* ground-state energy for a multi-electron atom, even though it
   correctly predicts electron configurations?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the picture assumes each electron occupies its own orbital in a
   fixed potential, and the real Hamiltonian contains a term that couples
   the electrons' coordinates and makes that assumption false.

   The term is the mutual repulsion:

   $$
   H = \sum_i\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla_i^2 - \frac{Ze^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r_i}\right) + \sum_{i<j}\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|}
   $$

   Everything in the first sum is a sum of independent single-particle
   Hamiltonians, and for that piece alone the eigenstates really are
   antisymmetrized products of hydrogen-like orbitals, with energies that
   add. The second sum is what spoils it: it depends on two positions at
   once, so it cannot be split among the electrons, $H$ does not separate,
   and the exact eigenstate is not built from one-electron orbitals at
   all. Real electrons avoid each other in a way no product of independent
   orbitals can express, and the energy that discrepancy accounts for has
   a name, the correlation energy.

   The shell picture handles the repulsion only as an averaged screening,
   which is a real approximation and not a bookkeeping convenience. The
   configuration labels survive as names for the dominant term in an
   expansion; the exact state is a superposition of several
   configurations.

   The distinction to hold onto is which conclusions of this lesson are
   which. The capacities $2n^2$ are exact, because they rest on dimension
   counting and antisymmetry, neither of which the repulsion term touches.
   The energies are approximate, because they rest on the independent
   orbital picture, which the repulsion term breaks. Helium, with two
   electrons, is already past exact solution, and getting ground-state
   energies for larger molecules to chemical accuracy is an open problem
   that variational methods, the ones two lessons back, are still the main
   tool for.

   </details>

4. Name the one physical effect, absent from the pure hydrogen
   treatment, that's responsible for the 4s-before-3d filling anomaly.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Screening: the inner electrons partly shield the nuclear charge, and
   how much shielding an outer electron feels depends on $l$, so the
   energy stops depending on $n$ alone.

   The $l$-dependence is the working part, and it traces back to the
   radial equation. A low-$l$ orbital has no centrifugal barrier keeping
   it away from the nucleus, so it penetrates inside the inner shells,
   sees a nuclear charge closer to the bare $Z$, and is bound more
   tightly. A $3d$ orbital, held out by $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ with
   $l=2$, stays outside the core, sees a well-screened nucleus, and is
   bound most weakly of the $n=3$ set. The ordering shifts far enough that
   $4s$ ends up below $3d$ despite the larger $n$, which is the toggle in
   the diagram above.

   Note that this is not a new force. It is the same Coulomb interaction
   the Hamiltonian always had, now acting between electrons rather than
   only between each electron and the nucleus, and it is the effect the
   previous question identified as making the problem unsolvable in closed
   form. What was fatal there is exactly what is explanatory here.

   Two things it is not. It is not relativity or spin-orbit coupling:
   those are fine-structure effects, smaller by roughly $\alpha^2$, and
   nowhere near large enough to reorder subshells. And it is not exclusion,
   which sets capacities and never energies.

   One refinement, because the $4s$ story is often told too firmly. The
   $4s$ and $3d$ levels sit very close together, and which is lower
   depends on how many electrons are present. In neutral scandium $4s$
   fills first; in transition-metal cations the $3d$ orbitals lie below
   $4s$ and it is the $4s$ electrons that leave first, which is why
   $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[\text{Ar}]3d^6$ rather than
   $[\text{Ar}]3d^44s^2$. The ordering is a property of the whole atom in
   a particular charge state, not a fixed ranking of the orbitals.

   </details>

## Summary

- Shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons: $n^2$ spatial orbitals
  (Hydrogen Energy Levels) times 2 (exclusion's spin-up/spin-down limit
  per orbital).
- This reuses hydrogen's orbitals as an approximate single-particle
  basis, useful for electron configurations, but not an exact solution
  to the true multi-electron Hamiltonian.
- Electron-electron repulsion (screening) breaks hydrogen's exact
  $l$-independence of energy, which is why real filling order departs
  from strict increasing-$n$ starting around $n=3$.
