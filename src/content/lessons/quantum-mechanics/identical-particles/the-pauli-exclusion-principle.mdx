## Motivation

Push your hand against a table and something stops it, even though both
your hand and the table are, by volume, almost entirely empty space:
nuclei are tiny, electrons are effectively points, and the rest is
vacuum. Electromagnetic repulsion alone can't account for the wall you
feel; overlapping electron clouds aren't charged nearly densely enough
for that. What actually stops your hand is the electrons' flat refusal to
crowd into the same quantum state as each other. That refusal has a name,
the Pauli exclusion principle, and the previous lesson already derived it
without quite announcing what it had found: it noted, almost in passing,
that
$|\psi_A\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle-|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=0$
whenever both fermions are assigned the same single-particle state. This
lesson takes that observation seriously: it *is* the Pauli exclusion
principle, not a separate physical law layered on top of
antisymmetrization.

Pauli reached this rule while trying to explain the anomalous Zeeman
effect and the fixed numbers at which electron shells close, submitting
the paper stating it on January 16, 1925. Lacking any physical mechanism
for the necessary extra property, he described it only as "a particular
two-valuedness of the quantum theoretic properties of the electron,
which cannot be described from a classical point of view," a placeholder
filled in physically later that same year by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit's
proposal of electron spin.

## Intuition

Swapping two identical fermions has to flip the sign of the wavefunction,
that's antisymmetry, established last lesson. Now ask what happens if the
two fermions being swapped are already in the exact same state. Swapping
identical things changes nothing about the physical configuration, so the
wavefunction has to come out equal to itself *and* equal to its own
negative at once. The only number equal to its own negative is zero.
There's no clever workaround or approximation here: forcing a sign flip
onto a state that's already identical to its own swap doesn't shrink the
wavefunction a little, it erases it completely.

## Mathematical Development

### The vanishing is exact, not approximate

$$
|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle - |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle = \vec 0
$$

This is exactly the zero vector, algebraically, for any $|a\rangle$
whatsoever. There is no normalization constant that turns a zero vector
into a unit-norm state; `normalizeVector` in this platform's engine
checks the norm before dividing and throws precisely because no valid
state exists to return. This is the *correct* behavior of a
mathematically honest implementation, not an error to work around.

### Statement of the principle

**No two identical fermions can occupy the same single-particle quantum
state.** For electrons specifically, "state" means a full specification
including spin. Two electrons *can* share the same spatial orbital
(like hydrogen's 1s) provided their spins differ (one spin-up, one
spin-down), since a spin-up electron and a spin-down electron are, in
the full state description, distinct single-particle states even with
identical spatial wavefunctions.

### No such restriction for bosons

The symmetric combination $|\psi_S\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle+|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=2|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
is manifestly nonzero and normalizes just fine. Bosons face no
analogous restriction, and in fact tend to *prefer* clustering into the
same state at low temperature (Bose-Einstein condensation, well beyond
this course's scope but a direct large-N consequence of this same
symmetric-combination mathematics).

## Physical Interpretation

Exclusion is why atoms have structure at all: it's the reason a multi-electron
atom's electrons fill successive shells (Multi-Electron Atoms, next
lesson) rather than all collapsing into the lowest-energy 1s orbital, and
ultimately why ordinary matter takes up space and resists compression.
None of this needs to be added as extra physics; it falls entirely out
of demanding the two-electron wavefunction be antisymmetric.

It's worth being precise about what triggers exclusion, because it's
easy to over-apply: it's antisymmetry under exchange of *identical*
particles, not some general property of two-level quantum systems. A
qubit in a quantum computer, a specific superconducting circuit, a
specific trapped ion, a specific electron spin pinned to a specific
quantum dot, is addressable and distinguishable from every other qubit
in the device by its location or its control wiring, even if the two
qubits are built from physically identical hardware. Nothing forces two
such qubits' joint state to antisymmetrize, so there's no exclusion-like
restriction stopping them both from sitting in $|0\rangle$ at once.
Exclusion bites specifically when two truly identical particles are
candidates for the *same* single-particle state with no label available
to tell them apart, which is a much narrower situation than "two qubits."

## Worked Example

**Confirm directly that `antisymmetrize(e0, e0)` throws, using a
3-dimensional basis, and explain exactly why.**

Calling `antisymmetrize(e0, e0)` computes
`tensorProduct(e0,e0) - tensorProduct(e0,e0)`, which is the zero vector
in every component by construction (subtracting an identical vector from
itself). `normalizeVector` then computes this vector's norm (exactly 0)
and throws `"Cannot normalize a (near-)zero vector, this state does not
exist."` This platform's test suite confirms this exact throw occurs,
matching the physical claim precisely: the state "two identical fermions,
both in orbital $e_0$" does not exist.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking exclusion means two fermions can never have the same energy,
or the same spatial wavefunction.** It specifically forbids the *same
complete single-particle state*: two electrons in hydrogen's 1s orbital
with opposite spins are in different states (different spin part) and
are completely allowed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Applying exclusion to bosons.** As the Mathematical Development section
shows directly, $|\psi_S\rangle$ never vanishes at $a=b$. There is no
boson exclusion principle.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The Pauli exclusion principle is a direct algebraic consequence of
  antisymmetrization: $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle-|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=\vec0$
  identically, for any single-particle state $a$.
- This platform's `antisymmetrize(a,a)` throws exactly because no valid
  normalized state exists, correct behavior, not a limitation.
- "State," for electrons, includes spin. Two electrons can share a
  spatial orbital if their spins differ, since that makes their complete
  single-particle states distinct.
