## Electrostatics is the obvious answer, and it fails

Electrostatics is the obvious explanation for solidity and it does not
work. An atom is neutral overall, so at any appreciable distance its
fields cancel, and pushing two neutral atoms together produces no large
Coulomb penalty until the nuclei come close: a separation far smaller
than the one at which matter actually starts to resist.

What resists is that electrons run out of available states. Compressing
two atoms together means their electrons must share a region, and the low
states are already occupied, so the electrons are forced into
higher-momentum states whose kinetic energy is the price of compression.
That price is what your hand feels.

The rule behind it is the <Term id="pauli-exclusion-principle">Pauli exclusion principle</Term>, and the previous
lesson derived it without stopping to say so. It observed in passing that
$|\psi_A\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle-|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=0$
whenever both fermions are assigned the same single-particle state. That
line *is* the exclusion principle. Nothing gets added to it here; this
lesson only takes it seriously.

<HistoricalMoment date="1925">
  Pauli reached this rule while trying to explain the anomalous Zeeman
  effect and the fixed numbers at which electron shells close, submitting
  the paper stating it on January 16, 1925. Lacking any physical mechanism
  for the necessary extra property, he described it only as "a particular
  two-valuedness of the quantum theoretic properties of the electron,
  which cannot be described from a classical point of view," a placeholder
  filled in physically later that same year by Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit's
  proposal of electron spin.
</HistoricalMoment>

## Swap two things that are already the same

Swapping two identical fermions flips the sign of the <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term>. That is
antisymmetry, established last lesson.

Now put both fermions in the same state and swap them again. Swapping two
things that are already identical changes nothing, so the wavefunction
must equal itself. Antisymmetry says it must equal its own negative. Both
at once leaves exactly one possibility, and it is not a small one: the
only number equal to its own negative is zero.

Notice the character of the conclusion. Nothing here is approximate or
suppressed by a small factor. The formalism does not make the
configuration unlikely; it declines to represent it.

## Exactly zero, for any state whatsoever

### The vanishing is exact, not approximate

$$
|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle - |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle = \vec 0
$$

This is the zero vector, algebraically, for any $|a\rangle$ whatsoever.
No <Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> constant converts a zero vector into a unit-norm
state: the required $\mathcal N$ would be $1/0$.

The platform's `normalizeVector` checks the norm before dividing and
throws rather than returning something. That throw is the implementation
agreeing with the physics: there is no state to hand back, so returning
any vector at all would be a lie.

### Statement of the principle

**No two identical fermions can occupy the same single-particle quantum
state.** For electrons specifically, "state" means a full specification
including spin. Two electrons *can* share the same spatial orbital
(like hydrogen's 1s) provided their spins differ (one spin-up, one
spin-down), since a spin-up electron and a spin-down electron are, in
the full state description, distinct single-particle states even with
identical spatial wavefunctions.

### No such restriction for bosons

The symmetric combination $|\psi_S\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle+|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=2|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
is manifestly nonzero and normalizes just fine. Bosons face no
analogous restriction, and in fact tend to *prefer* clustering into the
same state at low temperature (Bose-Einstein condensation, well beyond
this course's scope but a direct large-N consequence of this same
symmetric-combination mathematics).

## Why atoms have shells and the table has rows

Exclusion is why atoms have structure. It is the reason a multi-electron
atom fills successive shells (the next lesson) instead of stacking every
electron into the 1s orbital, and it is why the periodic table has rows
rather than being one long list of increasingly heavy versions of
hydrogen. Scale it up and it is why white dwarfs hold together, and why
they stop holding together above about $1.4$ solar masses. None of this
is extra physics. It all comes from demanding one sign flip under
exchange.

That $1.4$ is worth pushing on, because a core just past it does not
simply vanish.

It pays to be exact about when exclusion applies, because it is easy to
over-apply. The trigger is antisymmetry under exchange of *identical*
particles, not a general property of two-level systems.

Consider two qubits in a quantum computer. Each is addressable: this
transmon, that trapped ion, this electron spin in that quantum dot. The
location distinguishes them even when the hardware is manufactured
identically, so nothing requires their joint state to antisymmetrize, and
both can sit in $|0\rangle$ without difficulty. Exclusion bites only when
two genuinely identical particles are candidates for the same
single-particle state with no label available to separate them, which is
a narrower condition than "two similar systems."

## Why the engine throws instead of returning a state

**Confirm directly that `antisymmetrize(e0, e0)` throws, using a
3-dimensional basis, and explain exactly why.**

Calling `antisymmetrize(e0, e0)` computes
`tensorProduct(e0,e0) - tensorProduct(e0,e0)`, which is the zero vector
in every component by construction (subtracting an identical vector from
itself). `normalizeVector` then finds a norm of $0$ and throws `"Cannot
normalize a (near-)zero vector, this state does not exist."` The
platform's test suite asserts that this throw occurs, which is an unusual
kind of test: it asserts that a physical configuration has no
representation, and it would fail if some future refactor quietly
returned a fallback vector instead.

## The same complete state, spin included

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking exclusion means two fermions can never have the same energy,
or the same spatial wavefunction.** It specifically forbids the *same
complete single-particle state*: two electrons in hydrogen's 1s orbital
with opposite spins are in different states (different spin part) and
are completely allowed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying exclusion to bosons.** The symmetric combination above never
vanishes at $a=b$: it doubles, to $2|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, and
normalizes without complaint. There is no boson exclusion principle.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Derive, in one line of algebra, why $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle-|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=\vec0$
   for any $|a\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because any vector minus itself is the zero vector. Writing
   $|v\rangle=|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, the expression is
   $|v\rangle-|v\rangle=\vec0$, with no property of $|a\rangle$ used and
   nothing left to check.

   The arithmetic being this trivial is the point, and it is worth
   resisting the urge to look for something deeper in it. All the physics
   sits upstream, in the claim that identical fermions must be described
   by the antisymmetric combination. Once that is granted, exclusion is
   not a further law: it is the antisymmetrization formula evaluated at
   $b=a$, and it returns nothing because there was nothing to return.

   The general version is the same fact in a larger determinant. For $N$
   fermions the antisymmetric state is a Slater determinant whose columns
   are the occupied single-particle states, and a determinant with two
   equal columns vanishes, for exactly the reason above: swapping the two
   equal columns must flip the sign and must also change nothing. So
   exclusion for $N$ particles is a standard linear algebra fact, not a
   new physical input at every particle count.

   </details>

2. Two electrons in hydrogen's 1s orbital, one spin-up and one
   spin-down: does this violate exclusion? Why or why not, using the
   "complete single-particle state" definition?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No violation. The complete single-particle state includes spin, so
   $|1s\rangle\otimes|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and
   $|1s\rangle\otimes|{\downarrow}\rangle$ are two different states,
   orthogonal to each other. Exclusion forbids two electrons in the *same*
   complete state, and these are not the same.

   Write the two-electron state out and the antisymmetry is visible where
   it has to be:

   $$
   |\Psi\rangle = \underbrace{\psi_{1s}(\vec r_1)\,\psi_{1s}(\vec r_2)}_{\text{symmetric in space}} \times \underbrace{\frac{|{\uparrow}{\downarrow}\rangle - |{\downarrow}{\uparrow}\rangle}{\sqrt2}}_{\text{antisymmetric in spin}}
   $$

   Swapping the two electrons swaps both factors at once: the spatial
   factor is unchanged, the spin factor flips sign, and the product flips
   sign overall. Antisymmetry is satisfied, and nothing vanishes.

   Notice what that spin factor is. It is the singlet $|0,0\rangle$ from
   Addition of Angular Momentum, and it is not chosen for convenience. If
   both electrons share one spatial orbital, the spatial factor has no
   choice but to be symmetric, so the spin factor is forced to be the
   antisymmetric one. The helium ground state has total spin zero, and
   that is a prediction of exclusion rather than an observation about
   helium.

   The rule this makes precise: one spatial orbital holds at most two
   electrons, since there are only two orthogonal spin states to
   distinguish them. A third electron in 1s would have to duplicate one of
   the two, and the state would vanish. That "at most two" is where every
   shell-filling count in the next lesson comes from, and it is why the
   $n^2$ orbital degeneracy of hydrogen becomes $2n^2$ electrons.

   </details>

3. Why does this platform's engine treat `normalizeVector`'s throw on a
   zero vector as a feature (a correct physical statement) rather than
   fixing it to return some fallback vector?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because there is no correct vector to return. The configuration has no
   representation, and any fallback would assert that it does.

   Consider what a fallback would cost. Return the zero vector and every
   downstream probability comes out zero, so a caller summing outcome
   probabilities gets $0$ instead of $1$ and has to notice on their own.
   Return some arbitrary unit vector and the engine is reporting
   measurement statistics for a configuration that cannot exist. Both are
   worse than stopping, because both are silent, and a wrong number that
   arrives quietly is harder to find than an exception with a message.

   The throw carries the physics accurately: asking for two identical
   fermions in one state is not a computation with an unusual answer, it
   is a question with no answer. The test asserting that the throw happens
   is doing something slightly unusual as tests go, since it pins down the
   *absence* of a state, and it exists so a later refactor cannot quietly
   introduce a fallback in the name of robustness.

   One boundary worth marking, since "throw on zero" is not a universal
   rule. The check is on a *near*-zero norm, and that threshold is a
   judgment call. A genuine state with a very small norm, arising from
   accumulated floating-point error somewhere upstream, would trip the
   same guard and be reported as nonexistent. Encoding physics into an
   error is right when the impossibility is exact, as it is here; it needs
   more care when the quantity being tested is only approximately zero.

   </details>

4. Explain why Bose-Einstein condensation (bosons clustering into the
   same state) is not just "allowed" but actively suggested by the
   symmetric-combination math, in a way exclusion actively forbids for
   fermions.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because symmetrization does not merely fail to forbid $a=b$; it
   increases the amplitude for the two particles to be found in the same
   state, relative to what distinguishable particles would give.

   The norms from the previous lesson show it in one line. For two
   single-particle states with overlap $s=\langle a|b\rangle$:

   $$
   \big\||ab\rangle \pm |ba\rangle\big\|^2 = 2\left(1 \pm |s|^2\right)
   $$

   The symmetric norm squared *grows* as the states approach each other,
   reaching $4$ at $s=1$; the antisymmetric one shrinks to $0$. Once these are
   normalized, the same asymmetry appears as a bias in the statistics:
   bosons are more likely than distinguishable particles to be found close
   together, and fermions are less likely. Those effects have names and
   have been measured, as photon bunching and its fermionic counterpart,
   antibunching, in Hanbury Brown and Twiss style experiments.

   The dynamical version is the one with an industry attached. The rate
   for a boson to enter a mode that already contains $n$ bosons carries a
   factor of $n+1$, so occupancy encourages further occupancy: the more
   crowded the state, the more attractive it becomes. That factor is
   stimulated emission, and it is why a laser produces a single mode
   rather than a spread. Fermions have the reverse factor, $1-n$, which
   hits zero at $n=1$ and shuts the transition off entirely. Exclusion in
   dynamical clothing.

   Where to stop, in the interest of not overclaiming. The two-particle
   algebra establishes the *tendency*, not the phase transition. Actual
   Bose-Einstein condensation, a macroscopic fraction of the gas in one
   state below a sharp critical temperature, needs statistical mechanics
   and a thermodynamic limit that this course does not build. What the
   symmetric combination tells you is which way the bias points, and that
   the bias is there at $N=2$ before any large-$N$ argument begins.

   </details>

## Summary

- The Pauli exclusion principle is a direct algebraic consequence of
  antisymmetrization: $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle-|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle=\vec0$
  identically, for any single-particle state $a$.
- This platform's `antisymmetrize(a,a)` throws exactly because no valid
  normalized state exists, correct behavior, not a limitation.
- "State," for electrons, includes spin. Two electrons can share a
  spatial orbital if their spins differ, since that makes their complete
  single-particle states distinct.
