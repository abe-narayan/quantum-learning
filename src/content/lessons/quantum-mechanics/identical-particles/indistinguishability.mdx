## Motivation

Two classical billiard balls of the same mass and color are identical in
every measurable property, yet you could in principle paint a tiny dot on
one and track it. Classically, indistinguishability is a statement
about *information you don't bother collecting*, not a fact about
reality. Two electrons cannot be told apart even in principle: there is
no dot to paint, no trajectory to follow between measurements. This
lesson makes that difference mathematically precise.

## Mathematical Development

### The exchange operator

For a two-particle state $\psi(1,2)$ (particle 1 in some situation,
particle 2 in another), define the **exchange operator**
$P_{12}\psi(1,2)=\psi(2,1)$: it swaps which particle is "assigned" to
which single-particle state. Since the particles are physically
identical, swapping them must leave every observable prediction
unchanged: $|P_{12}\psi|^2=|\psi|^2$ everywhere.

### Why the eigenvalues are exactly ±1

Applying $P_{12}$ twice returns the original labeling exactly:
$P_{12}^2\psi(1,2)=P_{12}\psi(2,1)=\psi(1,2)$, so $P_{12}^2=I$. If
$\psi$ is an eigenstate, $P_{12}\psi=\lambda\psi$, then
$P_{12}^2\psi=\lambda^2\psi=\psi$, forcing $\lambda^2=1$, so
$\lambda=+1$ or $\lambda=-1$, no other value is possible. Nature turns
out to use both: particles with $\lambda=+1$ (symmetric wavefunctions)
are **bosons**; particles with $\lambda=-1$ (antisymmetric) are
**fermions**. The next lesson names this the spin-statistics
connection.

### A generic product state is not an eigenstate

Given two single-particle states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ (built as
ordinary vectors in some orbital basis), the plain product
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ (particle 1 in $a$, particle 2 in $b$) is
*not*, in general, an eigenstate of $P_{12}$ at all. Exchanging gives
$|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, a genuinely different vector whenever
$a\neq b$. This platform's `exchangeParticles` function performs exactly
this swap on a combined amplitude vector, confirmed directly: applying
it to a plain tensor product does *not* return the same vector (nor its
negative), while applying it to `symmetrize(a,b)` or `antisymmetrize(a,b)`
does, with eigenvalue $+1$ and $-1$ respectively (verified next lesson).

## Physical Interpretation

Because a plain product state isn't a valid exchange eigenstate, "particle
1 in state $a$, particle 2 in state $b$" is not, by itself, a physically
meaningful description of two identical particles. Only the symmetric
or antisymmetric *combination* is. This is the mathematical content of
"you cannot track which particle is which": the state itself refuses to
single one out.

## Worked Example

**Confirm numerically that $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$ is not an
eigenstate of exchange, using a 3-dimensional single-particle basis
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$.**

`tensorProduct(e0, e1)` gives the combined vector with a 1 in the "particle
1 in state 0, particle 2 in state 1" slot; `exchangeParticles` on that
vector gives the "particle 1 in state 1, particle 2 in state 0" vector,
a different vector entirely (not equal to the original, and not equal to
its negative), confirming directly that a bare product state carries
spurious, unphysical "which particle is where" information that a valid
two-identical-particle state cannot have.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking indistinguishability just means "hard to tell apart in
practice."** Classical identical particles are indistinguishable in this
weaker sense too, but can still (in principle) be tracked via continuous
trajectories. Quantum indistinguishability is the stronger claim that no
physically meaningful state can carry "particle 1 vs particle 2"
information at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming any two-particle wavefunction automatically has a definite
exchange symmetry.** As the worked example shows, a bare product state
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ does not. Definite exchange symmetry is a
special property that must be built in explicitly (next lesson).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Quantum indistinguishability means no measurable prediction can depend
  on which identical particle is "labeled" which, a genuinely stronger
  statement than classical indistinguishability.
- The exchange operator $P_{12}$ satisfies $P_{12}^2=I$, forcing its
  eigenvalues to be exactly $+1$ or $-1$.
- A bare product state $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ is generally not a
  $P_{12}$ eigenstate at all, confirmed directly by this platform's
  `exchangeParticles` function, so it isn't, by itself, a valid state
  of two identical particles.
