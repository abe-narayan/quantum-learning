<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Two classical billiard balls of the same mass and color are identical in every measurable property, yet you could in principle paint a tiny dot on one and track it. Two electrons cannot be told apart even in principle: there is no dot to paint, no trajectory to follow between measurements.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

Classical indistinguishability is a statement about information nobody
bothered to collect. Two billiard balls of the same mass and colour have
distinct trajectories whether or not anyone follows them, and painting a
dot on one would settle the matter without disturbing the physics.

Quantum mechanics offers no dot. Between measurements there are no
trajectories to follow, so "which particle went where" is not information
that exists and is hidden; it is information that was never defined. This
lesson turns that sentence into an operator equation, and the equation
turns out to constrain the allowed states of matter severely enough to
explain why atoms have structure at all.

## Mathematical Development

### The exchange operator

For a two-particle state $\psi(1,2)$ (particle 1 in some situation,
particle 2 in another), define the **exchange operator**
$P_{12}\psi(1,2)=\psi(2,1)$: it swaps which particle is "assigned" to
which single-particle state. Since the particles are physically
identical, swapping them must leave every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> prediction unchanged:
$|P_{12}\psi|^2=|\psi|^2$ everywhere. Note what this does *not* say. It
leaves $P_{12}\psi$ free to differ from $\psi$ by a phase, and the next
step is what pins that phase down.

### Why the eigenvalues are exactly ±1

Applying $P_{12}$ twice returns the original labeling exactly:
$P_{12}^2\psi(1,2)=P_{12}\psi(2,1)=\psi(1,2)$, so $P_{12}^2=I$. If
$\psi$ is an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term>, $P_{12}\psi=\lambda\psi$, then
$P_{12}^2\psi=\lambda^2\psi=\psi$, forcing $\lambda^2=1$, so
$\lambda=+1$ or $\lambda=-1$. No other value is available, and in
particular no intermediate phase $e^{i\theta}$ survives, because squaring
it has to return the identity.

Nature uses both. Particles with $\lambda=+1$ (symmetric wavefunctions)
are **bosons**; those with $\lambda=-1$ (antisymmetric) are **fermions**.
The next lesson connects the choice to spin.

<Callout type="note">
One step above is quieter than it looks. The argument shows that *if*
$\psi$ is an eigenstate of $P_{12}$ then $\lambda=\pm1$; that every
physical state of identical particles must be such an eigenstate is an
additional assumption, usually called the symmetrization postulate. It is
not idle bookkeeping: in genuinely two-dimensional systems the exchange
path cannot be undone by a continuous deformation, the $P_{12}^2=I$ step
fails, and particles called anyons acquire arbitrary phases $e^{i\theta}$
instead. Three-dimensional matter has only bosons and fermions.
</Callout>

### A generic product state is not an eigenstate

Given two single-particle states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ (built as
ordinary vectors in some orbital basis), the plain product
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ (particle 1 in $a$, particle 2 in $b$) is
*not*, in general, an eigenstate of $P_{12}$. Exchanging gives
$|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, a different vector whenever $a\neq b$, and
an eigenstate must return as a multiple of itself.

This platform's `exchangeParticles` performs the swap on a combined
<Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> vector. Applied to a plain tensor product it returns neither
the original nor its negative; applied to `symmetrize(a,b)` or
`antisymmetrize(a,b)` it returns exactly $+1$ and $-1$ times the input
(both built in the next lesson).

So the four-dimensional space of two-particle states in a
two-state basis is not all available. Only the symmetric part is open to
bosons and only the antisymmetric part to fermions, and a real pair of
identical particles never occupies the rest of it.

## Physical Interpretation

Because a plain product state is not an exchange eigenstate, "particle 1
in state $a$, particle 2 in state $b$" is not a physically meaningful
description of two identical particles. Only the symmetric or
antisymmetric combination is. The claim "you cannot track which particle
is which" is not a limit on the observer; the state has no such fact in
it to be read.

The consequences are not subtle. Restricting to the symmetric subspace is
what lets a gas of helium atoms condense into a single state, as in the
photograph above; restricting to the antisymmetric subspace is what stops
two electrons from doing the same, which is why atoms have shells and
matter takes up space. Two lessons from here, both follow from the sign
chosen in this section.

## Worked Example

**Confirm numerically that $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$ is not an
eigenstate of exchange, using a 3-dimensional single-particle basis
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$.**

`tensorProduct(e0, e1)` gives the combined vector with a 1 in the "particle
1 in state 0, particle 2 in state 1" slot; `exchangeParticles` on that
vector gives the "particle 1 in state 1, particle 2 in state 0" vector.
That is neither the original nor its negative, so the product state fails
the eigenstate test outright.

What the product state carries, and a valid two-identical-particle state
cannot, is a fact about which particle is where. The grid below shows the
single nonzero entry moving to a different cell, which is that spurious
fact being displaced.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking indistinguishability just means "hard to tell apart in
practice."** Classical identical particles are indistinguishable in this
weaker sense too, but can still (in principle) be tracked via continuous
trajectories. Quantum indistinguishability is the stronger claim that no
physically meaningful state can carry "particle 1 vs particle 2"
information at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any two-particle wavefunction automatically has a definite
exchange symmetry.** As the worked example shows, a bare product state
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ does not. Definite exchange symmetry is a
special property that must be built in explicitly (next lesson).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Quantum indistinguishability means no measurable prediction can depend
  on which identical particle is "labeled" which, a far stronger
  statement than classical indistinguishability.
- The exchange operator $P_{12}$ satisfies $P_{12}^2=I$, forcing its
  eigenvalues to be exactly $+1$ or $-1$.
- A bare product state $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ is generally not a
  $P_{12}$ eigenstate at all, confirmed directly by this platform's
  `exchangeParticles` function, so it isn't, by itself, a valid state
  of two identical particles.
