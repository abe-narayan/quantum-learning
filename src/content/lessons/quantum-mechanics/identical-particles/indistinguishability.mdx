## Information nobody bothered to collect

Classical indistinguishability is a statement about information nobody
bothered to collect. Two billiard balls of the same mass and colour have
distinct trajectories whether or not anyone follows them, and painting a
dot on one would settle the matter without disturbing the physics.

Quantum mechanics offers no dot. Between measurements there are no
trajectories to follow, so "which particle went where" is not information
that exists and is hidden; it is information that was never defined. This
lesson turns that sentence into an operator equation, and the equation
turns out to constrain the allowed states of matter severely enough to
explain why atoms have structure at all.

## The swap operator, and what its square forces

### The exchange operator

For a two-particle state $\psi(1,2)$ (particle 1 in some situation,
particle 2 in another), define the **exchange operator**
$P_{12}\psi(1,2)=\psi(2,1)$: it swaps which particle is "assigned" to
which single-particle state. Since the particles are physically
identical, swapping them must leave every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> prediction unchanged:
$|P_{12}\psi|^2=|\psi|^2$ everywhere. Note what this does *not* say. It
leaves $P_{12}\psi$ free to differ from $\psi$ by a phase, and the next
step is what pins that phase down.

### Why the eigenvalues are exactly ±1

Applying $P_{12}$ twice returns the original labeling exactly:
$P_{12}^2\psi(1,2)=P_{12}\psi(2,1)=\psi(1,2)$, so $P_{12}^2=I$. If
$\psi$ is an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term>, $P_{12}\psi=\lambda\psi$, then
$P_{12}^2\psi=\lambda^2\psi=\psi$, forcing $\lambda^2=1$, so
$\lambda=+1$ or $\lambda=-1$. No other value is available, and in
particular no intermediate phase $e^{i\theta}$ survives, because squaring
it has to return the identity.

Nature uses both. Particles with $\lambda=+1$ (symmetric wavefunctions)
are **bosons**; those with $\lambda=-1$ (antisymmetric) are **fermions**.
The next lesson connects the choice to spin.

<Callout type="note">
One step above is quieter than it looks. The argument shows that *if*
$\psi$ is an eigenstate of $P_{12}$ then $\lambda=\pm1$; that every
physical state of identical particles must be such an eigenstate is an
additional assumption, usually called the symmetrization postulate. It is
not idle bookkeeping: in genuinely two-dimensional systems the exchange
path cannot be undone by a continuous deformation, the $P_{12}^2=I$ step
fails, and particles called anyons acquire arbitrary phases $e^{i\theta}$
instead. Three-dimensional matter has only bosons and fermions.
</Callout>

### A generic product state is not an eigenstate

Given two single-particle states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ (built as
ordinary vectors in some orbital basis), the plain product
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ (particle 1 in $a$, particle 2 in $b$) is
*not*, in general, an eigenstate of $P_{12}$. Exchanging gives
$|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, a different vector whenever $a\neq b$, and
an eigenstate must return as a multiple of itself.

This platform's `exchangeParticles` performs the swap on a combined
<Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> vector. Applied to a plain tensor product it returns neither
the original nor its negative; applied to `symmetrize(a,b)` or
`antisymmetrize(a,b)` it returns exactly $+1$ and $-1$ times the input
(both built in the next lesson).

So the four-dimensional space of two-particle states in a
two-state basis is not all available. Only the symmetric part is open to
bosons and only the antisymmetric part to fermions, and a real pair of
identical particles never occupies the rest of it. It is worth counting
how much of the space that leaves.

## Why a product state describes nothing real

Because a plain product state is not an exchange eigenstate, "particle 1
in state $a$, particle 2 in state $b$" is not a physically meaningful
description of two identical particles. Only the symmetric or
antisymmetric combination is. The claim "you cannot track which particle
is which" is not a limit on the observer; the state has no such fact in
it to be read.

The consequences are not subtle. Restricting to the symmetric subspace is
what lets a gas of helium atoms condense into a single state, as in the
photograph above; restricting to the antisymmetric subspace is what stops
two electrons from doing the same, which is why atoms have shells and
matter takes up space. Two lessons from here, both follow from the sign
chosen in this section.

## Hard to tell apart is the weaker claim

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking indistinguishability just means "hard to tell apart in
practice."** Classical identical particles are indistinguishable in this
weaker sense too, but can still (in principle) be tracked via continuous
trajectories. Quantum indistinguishability is the stronger claim that no
physically meaningful state can carry "particle 1 vs particle 2"
information at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any two-particle wavefunction automatically has a definite
exchange symmetry.** As the worked example shows, a bare product state
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ does not. Definite exchange symmetry is a
special property that must be built in explicitly (next lesson).
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show algebraically that $P_{12}^2=I$ follows directly from the
   definition $P_{12}\psi(1,2)=\psi(2,1)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Name the intermediate function, apply the rule to *it*, and the two
   swaps undo each other.

   Let $\phi=P_{12}\psi$, so that $\phi(1,2)=\psi(2,1)$ by definition.
   Now apply $P_{12}$ a second time, to $\phi$ rather than to $\psi$:

   $$
   P_{12}^2\psi(1,2) = P_{12}\phi(1,2) = \phi(2,1) = \psi(1,2)
   $$

   The last step reads $\phi$'s defining relation with the labels
   interchanged. Since this holds for every $\psi$, the operator identity
   is $P_{12}^2=I$.

   The step people skip is exactly the one named above. Writing
   "$P_{12}\psi(2,1)=\psi(1,2)$" directly looks like the same thing and is
   sloppy in a way that matters: $P_{12}$ acts on a function, not on a
   particular set of arguments, so the rule has to be applied to whatever
   function it is handed. Introducing $\phi$ makes that explicit and the
   argument stops being a shuffle of symbols.

   Two consequences worth banking. $P_{12}$ is its own inverse, and since
   it is also unitary (it permutes an orthonormal basis, preserving inner
   products), $P_{12}^\dagger=P_{12}^{-1}=P_{12}$, so it is Hermitian too.
   Being Hermitian is why it has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal
   eigenbasis, which is what the next question makes use of.

   </details>

2. Why does $P_{12}^2=I$ restrict its eigenvalues to exactly $\{+1,-1\}$,
   and not, say, any value with $|\lambda|=1$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the constraint is $\lambda^2=1$, not $|\lambda|=1$, and those
   are very different conditions: the first has two solutions, the second
   has a whole circle of them.

   From $P_{12}\psi=\lambda\psi$ and $P_{12}^2=I$:

   $$
   \psi = P_{12}^2\psi = P_{12}(\lambda\psi) = \lambda^2\psi \implies \lambda^2 = 1 \implies \lambda = \pm1
   $$

   Test a candidate to see the difference bite. Take
   $\lambda=e^{i\pi/3}$, which certainly has modulus $1$. Then
   $\lambda^2=e^{2i\pi/3}\neq1$, so applying the swap twice would return
   $e^{2i\pi/3}\psi$ rather than $\psi$, and the labels would not be back
   where they started. Only $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi$ survive.

   Tracing where each condition came from is the real content here.
   Physical indistinguishability, $|P_{12}\psi|^2=|\psi|^2$, gives only
   $|\lambda|=1$: it permits any phase, because a phase is unobservable.
   What cuts the circle down to two points is the *operator* identity
   $P_{12}^2=I$, which is a statement about the geometry of exchanging
   labels twice in three-dimensional space, not about what is observable.

   And that is the step that fails in two dimensions, as the
   note above flags. Confined to a plane, exchanging two particles twice
   traces a loop that winds one around the other, and no continuous
   deformation unwinds it, so $P_{12}^2\ne I$. The circle reopens and
   arbitrary phases $e^{i\theta}$ become available. Those are anyons, and
   the fact that they exist in real two-dimensional systems is the best
   evidence that the $\pm1$ here was earned by an argument rather than
   assumed.

   </details>

3. If $|a\rangle=|b\rangle$ (both particles assigned to the *same*
   single-particle state), is the bare product $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
   an eigenstate of $P_{12}$? What eigenvalue?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes, with eigenvalue $+1$. Exchanging the two labels sends
   $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$ to $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, the same
   vector, so it is returned as $+1$ times itself.

   This is the one product state that is legal, and it is legal for a
   reason worth stating: there is no fact about which particle is which to
   displace, because both assignments are the same assignment. The
   explorer above shows it as the entry sitting on the diagonal of the
   coefficient grid, where the swap has nothing to move.

   Two payoffs follow immediately, and they are the whole of the next two
   lessons in miniature.

   Bosons can occupy the same state. $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$ is
   symmetric, hence available, which is what a Bose-Einstein condensate
   does at scale: every atom in one single-particle state, the
   photographed peak at the top of this lesson.

   Fermions cannot. Antisymmetrizing $|a\rangle$ with itself gives
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle - |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle)=0$,
   the zero vector, which is not a state at all. The exclusion principle
   is not an extra rule bolted on later; it is what antisymmetry says when
   two labels coincide.

   </details>

4. Explain, in one sentence, why "particle 1 is in state $a$" is not a
   physically meaningful statement for two identical particles, using
   this lesson's exchange-operator argument.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the only state that could express it, the product
   $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, is not an eigenstate of $P_{12}$ and
   therefore is not an allowed state of identical particles at all, while
   every allowed state gives both particles the same amplitude to be found
   in $a$, leaving no fact in the state for the sentence to be true of.

   The distinction that keeps this from collapsing into "nothing can be
   said" is worth drawing carefully, because it is where the idea usually
   gets over-applied.

   Meaningless: "particle 1 is in $a$." That is an assignment of labels to
   states, and both the symmetric and antisymmetric combinations
   $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\pm|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle)$
   spread their amplitude across both assignments with equal magnitude.

   Perfectly meaningful: "one particle is in $a$ and one is in $b$." That
   is a statement about *occupation*, it is true of both combinations
   above, and it survives exchange untouched. Measuring the two particles'
   energies really does return $E_a$ and $E_b$; what no measurement
   returns is which of the two is the one you would have called particle 1.

   Which is why the natural language for identical particles is occupation
   numbers, "how many particles are in each single-particle state," a
   description in which the labels never appear and so cannot be asked
   about. That reformulation is second quantization, and it exists mainly
   to stop people writing down states like $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ in
   the first place.

   </details>

## Summary

- Quantum indistinguishability means no measurable prediction can depend
  on which identical particle is "labeled" which, a far stronger
  statement than classical indistinguishability.
- The exchange operator $P_{12}$ satisfies $P_{12}^2=I$, forcing its
  eigenvalues to be exactly $+1$ or $-1$.
- A bare product state $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ is generally not a
  $P_{12}$ eigenstate at all, confirmed directly by this platform's
  `exchangeParticles` function, so it isn't, by itself, a valid state
  of two identical particles.
