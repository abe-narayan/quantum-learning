## Motivation

The previous lesson showed a bare product state isn't a valid
two-identical-particle state. This lesson builds the states that
actually are valid: the symmetric combination (bosons) and the
antisymmetric combination (fermions), and checks both directly against
the exchange operator rather than trusting the construction blindly.

## Mathematical Development

### Symmetric combination: bosons

$$
|\psi_S\rangle = \mathcal N\big(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle + |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\big)
$$

with $\mathcal N$ a normalization constant. Applying $P_{12}$ swaps the
two terms, which simply reproduces the same sum: $P_{12}|\psi_S\rangle=+|\psi_S\rangle$,
a genuine $+1$ eigenstate, for *any* choice of $a,b$, including $a=b$.

### Antisymmetric combination: fermions

$$
|\psi_A\rangle = \mathcal N\big(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle - |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\big)
$$

Applying $P_{12}$ swaps the two terms and flips their relative sign back:
$P_{12}|\psi_A\rangle=-|\psi_A\rangle$, a genuine $-1$ eigenstate,
*except* when $a=b$: then the two terms are identical and cancel
exactly, giving the zero vector, not a valid (normalizable) state at
all. This is the Pauli exclusion principle, previewed here and made the
whole subject of the next lesson.

### Verified directly, not just asserted

This platform's `symmetrize`/`antisymmetrize` functions build exactly
these combinations (with `normalizeVector` handling the division), and
`exchangeParticles` independently swaps the combined vector's two
particle-labels. Checking `exchangeParticles(symmetrize(a,b))` against
`symmetrize(a,b)` itself (expecting exact equality) and
`exchangeParticles(antisymmetrize(a,b))` against the *negative* of
`antisymmetrize(a,b)` (expecting exact equality), both confirmed to
floating-point precision, is a genuine numerical proof that the
construction has the claimed eigenvalue, not a restatement of the
algebra that built it.

## Physical Interpretation

**The spin-statistics theorem** (a deep result from relativistic quantum
field theory, well beyond this course's scope, stated here as an
experimentally-confirmed fact) says: particles with integer spin
($0,1,2,\ldots$, photons, the recently-covered spin-1 case from
Angular Momentum & Spin) are bosons; particles with half-integer spin
($\frac12,\frac32,\ldots$, electrons, protons, neutrons) are fermions.
This course takes the connection as given and explores its
consequences, rather than deriving it.

## Worked Example

**Using a 3-dimensional single-particle basis $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$,
confirm `antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` is a genuine $-1$ eigenstate.**

`antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` gives the normalized vector proportional to
$|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle-|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$. Applying
`exchangeParticles` to this result gives exactly the *negative* of the
input, matching to floating-point precision (`maxDiff` between
`antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` and $-$`exchangeParticles(antisymmetrize(e0,e1))`
is exactly 0 in this platform's test suite), a direct numerical
confirmation of the $-1$ eigenvalue.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the normalization step matters.** $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\pm|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
is *not* automatically normalized even when $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$
individually are (its norm depends on whether $a,b$ are orthogonal, and
whether $a=b$). `normalizeVector` handles this correctly in both cases,
rather than assuming a fixed $1/\sqrt2$ factor.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking symmetrization/antisymmetrization is a choice experimenters
make.** It's dictated entirely by the particle species (boson vs.
fermion) via the spin-statistics connection, not a modeling choice
applied differently depending on the situation.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show that $|\psi_S\rangle$ is a valid (normalizable) state for
   *every* choice of $a,b$, including $a=b$, while $|\psi_A\rangle$ is
   not.
2. If $a$ and $b$ are orthogonal and each individually normalized, what
   is $\||a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle+|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\|^2$, before
   normalizing? (This is exactly what `normalizeVector` divides out.)
3. Photons have spin 1; electrons have spin 1/2. Which symmetrization
   rule applies to each, per the spin-statistics connection?
4. Why is checking `exchangeParticles(antisymmetrize(a,b))` against
   $-$`antisymmetrize(a,b)` numerically a stronger confirmation than just
   re-deriving the algebra that built `antisymmetrize` in the first
   place?

## Summary

- $|\psi_S\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle+|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
  is always a valid $+1$ exchange eigenstate (bosons).
- $|\psi_A\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle-|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
  is a valid $-1$ exchange eigenstate only when $a\neq b$ (fermions); at
  $a=b$ it vanishes entirely.
- The spin-statistics theorem (integer spin ↔ boson, half-integer spin ↔
  fermion) is taken as an experimental fact; this platform's engine
  verifies the ±1 eigenvalue claims directly, not just the construction.
