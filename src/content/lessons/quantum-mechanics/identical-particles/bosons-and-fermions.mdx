Every particle in three-dimensional space belongs to one of two families, and the choice is made by a single sign. One sign lets a billion particles pile into the same state, which is what a laser beam and a superfluid are. The other forbids even two, which is why matter is solid.

## What the last lesson left to be built

The previous lesson ruled out the obvious candidate: a bare product state
carries a fact about which particle is where, so it cannot describe two
identical particles. It also showed that whatever does describe them must
be a $\pm1$ eigenstate of exchange.

That is a strong enough constraint to make the construction almost
forced. From two single-particle states there are exactly two ways to
combine them without privileging either particle: add the two orderings,
or subtract them. This lesson builds both, checks their eigenvalues
against the exchange operator rather than against the algebra that
produced them, and finds that the second construction fails in one
specific case. That failure is the Pauli exclusion principle.

## Symmetrize, antisymmetrize, then check

### Symmetric combination: bosons

$$
|\psi_S\rangle = \mathcal N\big(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle + |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\big)
$$

with $\mathcal N$ a <Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> constant, equal to $1/\sqrt2$ when $a$ and
$b$ are orthonormal and distinct. Applying $P_{12}$ swaps the two terms,
and a sum is unchanged by reordering it:
$P_{12}|\psi_S\rangle=+|\psi_S\rangle$. This is a $+1$ <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> for every
choice of $a$ and $b$, $a=b$ included, where the two terms coincide and
the result is just $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$.

### Antisymmetric combination: fermions

$$
|\psi_A\rangle = \mathcal N\big(|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle - |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\big)
$$

Applying $P_{12}$ swaps the two terms, which reverses the sign of the
difference: $P_{12}|\psi_A\rangle=-|\psi_A\rangle$, a $-1$ eigenstate.

With one exception. Set $a=b$ and the two terms become identical, so the
difference is the zero vector. Zero is not a state: it cannot be
normalized, and $\mathcal N$ would have to be infinite. The construction
has not produced a state with some special property; it has failed to
produce a state at all, which is the formalism's way of saying the
configuration does not exist. This is the <Term id="pauli-exclusion-principle">Pauli exclusion principle</Term>,
and the next lesson is about how much of chemistry hangs on it.

### Checking the construction against the operator

This platform's `symmetrize` and `antisymmetrize` build these two
combinations, with `normalizeVector` supplying $\mathcal N$, while
`exchangeParticles` swaps a combined vector's particle labels without
knowing where that vector came from.

Running `symmetrize(a,b)` through `exchangeParticles` returns the input
unchanged; running `antisymmetrize(a,b)` through it returns the input's
negative. Both hold to floating-point precision. Because the swapping
function has no knowledge of how the two combinations were constructed,
this tests the eigenvalue claim rather than retracing the algebra that
motivated it.

## The theorem that assigns the signs

Nothing so far says which particles take which sign. The algebra permits
both and is silent about the assignment.

The **spin-statistics theorem** settles it: particles with integer spin
($0,1,2,\dots$: photons, and the spin-1 case from Angular Momentum &
Spin) are bosons, and particles with half-integer spin
($\tfrac12,\tfrac32,\dots$: electrons, protons, neutrons) are fermions.
The proof belongs to relativistic quantum field theory and is well beyond
this course; it needs special relativity, which is a clue in itself, since
nothing in the exchange argument above mentioned either spin or
relativity.

Taken as given, the rule is worth noticing for how much it fixes. Spin
was derived in an earlier course from a commutator, exchange symmetry in
the last lesson from a relabelling, and the theorem welds two apparently
unrelated results into one.

The rule is stated for particles, and the photograph at the top of this
lesson is of helium, which is not one. A helium-4 nucleus is two protons
and two neutrons bound together, and every one of those is spin-$\tfrac12$.

## Confirming a minus-one eigenstate on the engine

**Using a 3-dimensional single-particle basis $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$,
confirm `antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` is a genuine $-1$ eigenstate.**

`antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` gives the normalized vector proportional to
$|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle-|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$. Applying
`exchangeParticles` to that result gives the negative of the input. The
platform's test suite reports a maximum entrywise difference of $0$
between `antisymmetrize(e0,e1)` and
$-$`exchangeParticles(antisymmetrize(e0,e1))`, so the $-1$ eigenvalue is
confirmed numerically and not merely by construction.

Now repeat it with `antisymmetrize(e0,e0)`. Every entry cancels, and
`normalizeVector` is handed the zero vector and throws rather than
returning anything. The engine does not have a special case for Pauli
exclusion; the exception *is* the exclusion principle, arriving as the
absence of a state rather than as a rule about one. Two fermions in the
same single-particle state have nothing to occupy.

## The normalization step is doing real work

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the normalization step matters.** $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\pm|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
is *not* automatically normalized even when $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$
individually are (its norm depends on whether $a,b$ are orthogonal, and
whether $a=b$). `normalizeVector` handles this correctly in both cases,
rather than assuming a fixed $1/\sqrt2$ factor.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking symmetrization or antisymmetrization is a modelling choice.**
The particle species fixes it through the spin-statistics connection.
There is no regime, temperature, or approximation in which an electron
pair may be treated as symmetric.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show that $|\psi_S\rangle$ is a valid (normalizable) state for
   *every* choice of $a,b$, including $a=b$, while $|\psi_A\rangle$ is
   not.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The whole difference lives in the case $a=b$, where the two terms
   coincide and a sum doubles while a difference cancels:

   $$
   |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle + |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle = 2\,|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle, \qquad |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle - |a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle = 0
   $$

   The symmetric result has norm $2$, so dividing by $\mathcal N^{-1}=2$
   gives the perfectly good normalized state $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$.
   The antisymmetric result is the zero vector, whose norm is $0$, and no
   choice of $\mathcal N$ makes a unit vector out of it. Normalization is
   division by the norm, and there is no dividing by zero.

   Both are normalizable whenever $a\neq b$, so the failure is confined to
   the coincident case. Computing the norms in general shows how the
   failure arrives, which is more illuminating than the special case
   alone. With $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ each normalized and
   $s=\langle a|b\rangle$ their overlap:

   $$
   \big\||a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle \pm |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\big\|^2 = 2\left(1 \pm |s|^2\right)
   $$

   The symmetric norm *squared* runs from $2$ up to $4$ as the two states
   approach each other, never in trouble. The antisymmetric one runs from
   $2$ down to $0$, reaching zero exactly when $|s|=1$, which is when $a$
   and $b$ are the same state up to a phase.

   That continuity is the part worth carrying away. The exclusion
   principle is not a switch thrown when two labels happen to match. The
   antisymmetric state shrinks smoothly toward nothing as the two
   single-particle states are brought into alignment, and two fermions in
   *nearly* the same state are already strongly suppressed relative to two
   bosons. Exclusion at $a=b$ is the endpoint of a gradient, not an
   isolated prohibition.

   </details>

2. If $a$ and $b$ are orthogonal and each individually normalized, what
   is $\||a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle+|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\|^2$, before
   normalizing? (This is exactly what `normalizeVector` divides out.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $2$, so the norm is $\sqrt2$ and $\mathcal N=1/\sqrt2$.

   Expanding the inner product gives four terms:

   $$
   \big\||ab\rangle+|ba\rangle\big\|^2 = \underbrace{\langle ab|ab\rangle}_{1} + \underbrace{\langle ba|ba\rangle}_{1} + \underbrace{\langle ab|ba\rangle}_{|\langle a|b\rangle|^2} + \underbrace{\langle ba|ab\rangle}_{|\langle a|b\rangle|^2} = 2
   $$

   The two cross terms each carry a factor $\langle a|b\rangle\langle
   b|a\rangle=|\langle a|b\rangle|^2$, which orthogonality sends to zero.

   Orthogonality is a hypothesis here, not decoration, and dropping it
   breaks the familiar answer. The general result is $2(1+|s|^2)$ from the
   previous question, so a non-orthogonal pair needs a different
   $\mathcal N$: at $s=1$ the norm squared is $4$ and the constant is
   $1/2$, not $1/\sqrt2$. This is why the platform calls
   `normalizeVector` on the constructed vector rather than multiplying by
   a hard-coded $1/\sqrt2$. The hard-coded factor is right for the
   orthonormal case and quietly wrong everywhere else, and a state that is
   off by a constant factor produces probabilities that do not sum to one
   with no error to announce it.

   </details>

3. Photons have spin 1; electrons have spin 1/2. Which symmetrization
   rule applies to each, per the spin-statistics connection?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Photons have integer spin, so they are bosons and their states are
   symmetric, $P_{12}|\psi\rangle=+|\psi\rangle$. Electrons have
   half-integer spin, so they are fermions and their states are
   antisymmetric, $P_{12}|\psi\rangle=-|\psi\rangle$.

   The consequences are visible without any apparatus. Photons piling into
   one mode is what makes a laser a laser: many photons in the identical
   single-particle state, which the symmetric construction permits at any
   occupation number. Electrons refusing to do the same is why atoms have
   shells, why the periodic table repeats, and why one solid object cannot
   pass through another.

   One caution about applying the rule. It is stated for the *total* spin
   of whatever is being exchanged, so it applies to composites as well,
   and the parity of the constituent count decides. Helium-4, with an even
   number of fermions, is a boson and becomes superfluid at $2.17$ K.
   Helium-3, one neutron lighter and therefore an odd number, is a fermion
   and needs about a thousand times lower temperature and a pairing
   mechanism first. Same element, same chemistry, entirely different
   low-temperature physics, decided by a count of spin-$\tfrac12$ parts.

   </details>

4. Why is checking `exchangeParticles(antisymmetrize(a,b))` against
   $-$`antisymmetrize(a,b)` numerically a stronger confirmation than just
   re-deriving the algebra that built `antisymmetrize` in the first
   place?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because re-deriving the algebra can only tell you the derivation agrees
   with itself. It has no way to catch an error in the derivation, and no
   way at all to catch an error in the code that the rest of the course
   actually runs.

   The two routes are genuinely different pieces of work.
   `antisymmetrize` constructs a vector from a formula. `exchangeParticles`
   takes a flat amplitude vector and permutes its entries, with no
   knowledge of where the vector came from or what it is supposed to
   satisfy. So the test asks whether an object built one way has a
   property verified another way, which is the situation in which
   agreement carries information.

   It rules out a specific and realistic class of mistakes: a sign written
   the wrong way round in the construction, a normalization applied to the
   wrong quantity, and above all an index-ordering slip in flattening a
   two-particle amplitude into one vector, which is the sort of error that
   produces plausible-looking numbers and no exception.

   The honest limit of the argument, since independence is a matter of
   degree rather than a binary. If both functions shared the *same* wrong
   convention for which index runs fastest, they could agree with each
   other and disagree with the physics, and this test would pass. What
   would catch that is a third check against something outside the pair,
   such as computing an observable both ways or comparing against a
   hand-worked small case. A test's value is exactly the set of errors it
   could have detected, and it is worth knowing where that set ends.

   </details>

## Summary

- $|\psi_S\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle+|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
  is always a valid $+1$ exchange eigenstate (bosons).
- $|\psi_A\rangle\propto|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle-|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$
  is a valid $-1$ exchange eigenstate only when $a\neq b$ (fermions); at
  $a=b$ it vanishes entirely.
- The spin-statistics theorem (integer spin ↔ boson, half-integer spin ↔
  fermion) is taken as an experimental fact; this platform's engine
  checks the ±1 eigenvalue claims against `exchangeParticles` directly,
  rather than resting on the construction alone.
