## Real outcomes demand real eigenvalues

A measurement outcome, the energy of an atom, the spin of an electron, is
always a real number; nobody has ever measured $3+2i$ volts. If
physical <Term id="observable">observables</Term> are represented by operators, and eigenvalues are the
possible outcomes (previewed last lesson), those operators had better be
restricted to ones whose <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalues</Term> are guaranteed real. That restriction
has a name, **<Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term>**, and this lesson proves that it does the job,
rather than asserting it.

## The matrix version of equalling your own conjugate

A real number equals its own conjugate. **Hermitian** operators are the
matrix generalization of that idea: an operator that, in a precise sense,
equals its own conjugate transpose. The two theorems below show how much
that buys: the algebraic condition $A=A^\dagger$ is strong enough, on its
own, to force every eigenvalue real and every pair of eigenvectors with
distinct eigenvalues orthogonal.

## One condition, two theorems

### The adjoint

The **adjoint** $A^\dagger$ of an operator $A$ is defined by requiring
$\langle u|Av\rangle = \langle A^\dagger u|v\rangle$ for all $u,v$.
Concretely, for a matrix, $A^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose:
$(A^\dagger)_{ij} = A_{ji}^*$, the same dagger notation introduced for
kets in the Bra-Ket Formalism lesson, now applied to operators.

### Definition

$A$ is **Hermitian** (or self-adjoint) if $A = A^\dagger$.

That is the entire condition, and on its face it says nothing about
eigenvalues at all. A general operator on a complex space has complex
eigenvalues, and some have no real ones at all: the $90°$ rotation matrix
$\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ that stood in for $i$ back in the
Complex Numbers lesson has characteristic equation $\lambda^2+1=0$, whose
roots are $\pm i$. So before reading on, decide what $A=A^\dagger$ buys.

### Theorem: Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues

Let $A|v\rangle=\lambda|v\rangle$, $v\ne0$, $A$ Hermitian. Consider
$\langle v|Av\rangle$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Use the adjoint definition with u=v, then substitute A†=A.">
$$
\langle v|Av\rangle = \langle A^\dagger v|v\rangle = \langle Av|v\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Conjugate symmetry of the inner product relates ⟨Av|v⟩ back to ⟨v|Av⟩.">
$$
\langle Av|v\rangle = \langle v|Av\rangle^*
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Combine the two: a complex number equal to its own conjugate is forced to be real.">
$$
\langle v|Av\rangle = \langle v|Av\rangle^* \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle v|Av\rangle \in \mathbb{R}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Separately, compute ⟨v|Av⟩ directly using the eigenvalue equation.">
$$
\langle v|Av\rangle = \langle v|\lambda v\rangle = \lambda\langle v|v\rangle = \lambda\|v\|^2
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="⟨v|Av⟩ is real (previous step) and ‖v‖² is real and strictly positive (v≠0), so λ itself must be real.">
$$
\lambda = \frac{\langle v|Av\rangle}{\|v\|^2} \in \mathbb{R}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Theorem: eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are orthogonal

Let $A|u\rangle=\lambda_1|u\rangle$, $A|v\rangle=\lambda_2|v\rangle$, $A$
Hermitian, $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$ (both real, by the theorem just
proved). Compute $\langle u|Av\rangle$ two ways. Directly:

$$
\langle u|Av\rangle = \lambda_2\langle u|v\rangle
$$

Via the adjoint (using $A^\dagger=A$ and $\lambda_1$ real, so
$\lambda_1^*=\lambda_1$):

$$
\langle u|Av\rangle = \langle Au|v\rangle = \langle\lambda_1 u|v\rangle = \lambda_1^*\langle u|v\rangle = \lambda_1\langle u|v\rangle
$$

Equating the two: $\lambda_2\langle u|v\rangle = \lambda_1\langle
u|v\rangle$, so $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\langle u|v\rangle=0$. Since
$\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$, $\langle u|v\rangle=0$: orthogonal.

(Eigenvectors that happen to *share* an eigenvalue are not automatically
orthogonal to each other, but they can always be chosen to be, via the
Gram-Schmidt process from the Inner Products lesson. There's no
obstruction, just a choice that needs to be made explicitly.)

### The spectral theorem

Putting the last two theorems together: a Hermitian operator on a
finite-dimensional space has an <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term> entirely of
eigenvectors, and can be written as a weighted completeness relation, the
**spectral decomposition**:

$$
A = \sum_i \lambda_i |e_i\rangle\langle e_i|
$$

(This lesson states the theorem rather than proving the full existence
argument, but verifies it concretely below.) This is the completeness
relation from the Bra-Ket Formalism lesson, now weighted by each basis
vector's eigenvalue: the natural generalization of "a diagonal matrix is
built from its diagonal entries and the standard basis."

### Expectation value

For a <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> state $|\psi\rangle$ and Hermitian observable $A$, the
**expectation value** is defined as $\langle A\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$,
the "sandwich" you'll see everywhere in quantum mechanics. It's
guaranteed real by the same argument as the eigenvalue theorem above
(with $|\psi\rangle$ not necessarily an eigenvector this time). Physically
it represents the average result if the same measurement were repeated
many times on identically-prepared systems, made precise in the
Probability and Quantum States lesson.

## What A = A-dagger buys you physically

Hermiticity is not an arbitrary mathematical restriction. The two theorems
above turn a single algebraic condition, $A=A^\dagger$, into two physical
guarantees at once: every possible measurement outcome is a real number,
and states with different definite outcomes are perfectly distinguishable
(orthogonal, in the sense of the Inner Products lesson). That is why every
observable in quantum mechanics, position, momentum, energy, spin, is
represented by a Hermitian operator, without exception.

The logic runs from the laboratory to the mathematics, not the other way
around. A photon counter, an energy analyzer, and a magnetometer all
report real numbers, so any operator proposed as the representative of
what they measure has to have real eigenvalues, and Hermiticity is the
condition that delivers that. Where a non-Hermitian operator does appear
in physics, as with the effective Hamiltonians used to model a system
leaking particles to its surroundings, the imaginary part is not a
measurement outcome. It is a decay rate, and the model is understood as an
approximation to a larger Hermitian description.

## Pauli-Z decomposed, and one impostor tested

**Verify the spectral decomposition of Pauli-$Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$,
and check whether $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$ is Hermitian.**

$Z$'s eigenvalues are $\pm1$ with eigenvectors $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$
(diagonal already, so this is immediate). The spectral decomposition
claims $Z = (+1)|0\rangle\langle0| + (-1)|1\rangle\langle1|$:

$$
1\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + (-1)\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = Z \checkmark
$$

Now the second matrix, $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$ (the S gate,
if you've seen quantum computing).

## Hermitian does not mean real-entried

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing "Hermitian" with "real."** A Hermitian matrix can have complex
off-diagonal entries (Pauli-$Y$ is a standard example). The requirement
is $A=A^\dagger$, not that every entry is real.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every operator has real eigenvalues.** This is a special
property of Hermitian operators, not a general fact; plenty of
non-Hermitian matrices have complex eigenvalues.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**A Hermitian matrix's diagonal entries must be real.** Check this first.
It's the fastest way to rule out a candidate.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is $\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}$ Hermitian? Justify using the
   adjoint definition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. Apply $(A^\dagger)_{ij} = A_{ji}^*$ in two steps.

   Transpose:

   $$
   A^\mathsf{T} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   Then conjugate every entry:

   $$
   A^\dagger = \left(A^\mathsf{T}\right)^* = \begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix} = A \checkmark
   $$

   Both operations were needed, and neither alone would have sufficed:
   $A^\mathsf{T} \ne A$ and $A^* \ne A$. The off-diagonal pair swapped places
   under the transpose and swapped back under the conjugation, which is exactly
   the structure Hermiticity requires.

   Two features generalize. The diagonal entries of a Hermitian matrix must be
   real, since $A_{ii} = A_{ii}^*$; both are 1 here. And the off-diagonal
   entries must be conjugates of each other across the diagonal,
   $A_{12} = A_{21}^*$; here $i = (-i)^* \checkmark$. Scanning for those two
   conditions is faster than writing the transpose out, and it is the same
   check.

   As predicted by the theorem, the eigenvalues come out real: the trace is 2
   and the determinant is $1 - (i)(-i) = 1 - 1 = 0$, giving
   $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = 0, 2$.

   </details>

2. Is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ Hermitian? (This one is real,
   so don't assume that settles it.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No.

   Conjugation does nothing to a real matrix, so $A^\dagger = A^\mathsf{T}$
   here, and

   $$
   A^\mathsf{T} = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \ne \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} = A.
   $$

   The off-diagonal entries are negatives of each other rather than conjugates
   of each other, so the matrix fails the test at exactly the place question 1
   passed it.

   The parenthetical warning in the question is the substance. "Real" is not
   the criterion; for a real matrix Hermitian means *symmetric*, and this
   matrix is antisymmetric, $A^\mathsf{T} = -A$. Being real removes the
   conjugation from the requirement but leaves the transposition, which is the
   half that fails.

   The consequence is visible in the spectrum, and it is a useful independent
   check. Trace 0 and determinant $0 - (1)(-1) = 1$ give $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$,
   so $\lambda = \pm i$: purely imaginary, so this operator cannot represent an
   observable. It is not a broken operator, though. $A^\dagger = -A$ makes it
   *anti*-Hermitian, and $iA = \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\-i&0\end{pmatrix} = -Y$ is
   Hermitian. Multiplying by $i$ always converts between the two families,
   which is why the Schrödinger equation carries an explicit $i$: it turns the
   Hermitian Hamiltonian into the anti-Hermitian generator that unitary time
   evolution requires.

   </details>

3. For $A=$ Pauli-$Z$ and $|\psi\rangle = (\sqrt{0.6},\sqrt{0.4})$ (both
   entries real and positive), compute $\langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle$, and
   explain why the answer is not one of $Z$'s possible measurement
   outcomes.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle = \left(\sqrt{0.6}\right)^2(+1) + \left(\sqrt{0.4}\right)^2(-1) = 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2.
   $$

   Doing it as a matrix sandwich gives the same thing:
   $Z|\psi\rangle = (\sqrt{0.6}, -\sqrt{0.4})^\mathsf{T}$, and pairing that
   against $\langle\psi|$ gives $0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2$.

   Now the second half. $Z$'s eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$, and a measurement
   of $Z$ returns one of those two numbers and nothing else. Yet
   $\langle Z\rangle = 0.2$, which is neither. There is no contradiction,
   because an expectation value is not a prediction about any single
   measurement; it is the long-run *average* over many measurements on
   identically prepared states:

   $$
   \langle Z\rangle = (+1)P(+1) + (-1)P(-1) = (+1)(0.6) + (-1)(0.4) = 0.2.
   $$

   An average of $\pm1$ outcomes can land anywhere in $[-1, 1]$, and it lands
   *at* an eigenvalue only when the state is an eigenstate, so that one outcome
   is certain. The same thing happens with a fair die, whose average roll of
   3.5 is not a face.

   The reading to resist is "the measurement gives 0.2", or its cousin "the
   qubit is 60% in state $|0\rangle$" as a description of the qubit itself.
   The right statement is that repeated preparation and measurement yields
   $+1$ about 60% of the time and $-1$ about 40% of the time, and 0.2 is the
   mean of that record. This is also why the notation is
   $\langle A \rangle$ rather than $A$: the angle brackets are an average.

   </details>

4. Explain why the theorem "eigenvectors of different eigenvalues are
   orthogonal" required $A$ to be Hermitian. Where exactly would the
   proof break down for a non-Hermitian $A$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The proof computes $\langle u|Av\rangle$ in two ways and equates them. The
   first way is unconditional:

   $$
   \langle u|Av\rangle = \lambda_2\langle u|v\rangle.
   $$

   The second way moves $A$ across the bracket, and *that* is the step
   Hermiticity pays for. By the definition of the adjoint,
   $\langle u|Av\rangle = \langle A^\dagger u|v\rangle$, always. To continue,
   the proof needs $A^\dagger u$ to be something known, and it writes
   $A^\dagger u = Au = \lambda_1 u$, which is legal only because
   $A^\dagger = A$.

   So the breakdown is precise. For a non-Hermitian $A$, $|u\rangle$ is an
   eigenvector of $A$ but there is no reason for it to be an eigenvector of
   $A^\dagger$, so $\langle A^\dagger u|v\rangle$ cannot be simplified at all
   and there is no second expression to equate with the first. The chain stops
   before it reaches $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\langle u|v\rangle = 0$.

   A second, smaller dependence is easy to miss. Even granting the move, the
   proof uses $\lambda_1^* = \lambda_1$ to pull the eigenvalue out of the bra
   without conjugating it, and that realness is itself the *previous* theorem,
   which also required Hermiticity. The two theorems are not independent
   results that happen to share a hypothesis.

   A concrete counterexample settles that this is a real gap and not a defect
   of the argument. Take $A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, which is
   not Hermitian. Its eigenvalues are 1 and 2, distinct, with eigenvectors
   $(1,0)^\mathsf{T}$ and $(1,1)^\mathsf{T}$. Their inner product is 1, not 0:
   distinct eigenvalues, and the eigenvectors are not orthogonal. Hermiticity
   was doing real work.

   </details>

## Summary

- $A^\dagger$ is defined by $\langle u|Av\rangle=\langle A^\dagger
  u|v\rangle$; concretely, conjugate transpose. $A$ is Hermitian if
  $A=A^\dagger$.
- Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues, derived from $\langle
  v|Av\rangle$ being simultaneously real (by Hermiticity) and equal to
  $\lambda\|v\|^2$.
- Eigenvectors of a Hermitian operator with different eigenvalues are
  automatically orthogonal, derived by computing $\langle u|Av\rangle$
  two ways.
- Spectral decomposition: $A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$.
- Expectation value $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ is always
  real for Hermitian $A$.
