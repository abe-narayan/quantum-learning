## Keeping total probability at one

A <Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term>'s norm is tied to total probability. The Probability and
Quantum States lesson makes that precise; the short version is that a
<Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> state must *stay* normalized as it evolves or gets transformed,
or probability would leak away or overshoot $100\%$. The operators
guaranteed to preserve norms are called **<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>**, and every quantum
gate, every basis change, and every step of time evolution in quantum
mechanics is one.

## The matrix analogue of a phase

If Hermitian operators are the matrix analogue of a real number,
**unitary** operators are the matrix analogue of a complex number with
<Term id="modulus">modulus</Term> $1$, a pure phase $e^{i\theta}$, which rotates but never
rescales. Multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ never changes a complex number's
modulus (from the Complex Numbers lesson); a unitary operator is the
higher-dimensional version of that same idea, never changing a vector's
length.

## What U-dagger U = I forces

### Definition

$U$ is **unitary** if $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I$, equivalently,
$U^\dagger = U^{-1}$.

### Theorem: unitary operators preserve inner products

$$
\langle Uu|Uv\rangle = \langle u|U^\dagger U|v\rangle = \langle u|I|v\rangle = \langle u|v\rangle
$$

(the first equality is the adjoint definition, pulling $U^\dagger$ across
from the bra side onto the ket side; the second uses $U^\dagger U=I$
directly). Setting $u=v$ gives the important special case: unitary
operators preserve **norms**,

$$
\|Uv\| = \|v\|
$$

<Callout type="note">
This is why every gate you've met, if you've studied the
Quantum Computing track ($X$, $H$, $S$, $\mathrm{CNOT}$, all of them), is
unitary: a gate must take a normalized state to another normalized state,
and this theorem is the reason that requirement is equivalent to
unitarity.
</Callout>

### Theorem: eigenvalues of a unitary operator have modulus $1$

Let $U|v\rangle=\lambda|v\rangle$, $v\ne0$, $U$ unitary. Norm preservation
gives $\|Uv\|=\|v\|$, i.e. $\|\lambda v\| = \|v\|$, i.e.
$|\lambda|\,\|v\| = \|v\|$. Since $\|v\|\ne0$, this forces $|\lambda|=1$.
Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator is a complex number of modulus
$1$, necessarily of the form $e^{i\theta}$ for some real $\theta$,
directly recalling the $|e^{i\theta}|=1$ fact from the Complex Numbers
lesson.

### Unitary matrices as basis transformations

If $\{|e_i\rangle\}$ and $\{|f_i\rangle\}$ are two <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal bases</Term>,
there is a unitary $U$ with $U|e_i\rangle=|f_i\rangle$ for every $i$:
define $U$ this way on the basis and extend linearly. It preserves inner
products because both bases are orthonormal:
$\langle Ue_i|Ue_j\rangle=\langle f_i|f_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}=\langle
e_i|e_j\rangle$, which is the definition of a unitary map. This is the
operator-level version of the "change of basis" idea from the Vector
Spaces lesson: moving between two orthonormal bases is always a unitary
transformation.

The Hadamard matrix is the smallest concrete case. Feed it the standard
basis and it returns the Hadamard basis:

The right angle between $e_1$ and $e_2$ survives, and so does the length
of each arrow. Both facts are the same theorem: $\langle Ue_1|Ue_2\rangle
= \langle e_1|e_2\rangle = 0$ preserves the angle, and $\|Ue_i\|=\|e_i\|$
preserves the length. What the Hadamard did was rotate and reflect the
frame, not deform it.

### Columns form an orthonormal basis

$U^\dagger U = I$ says something concrete entry-by-entry: the $(i,j)$
entry of $U^\dagger U$ is the inner product of $U$'s $i$-th column with
$U$'s $j$-th column. So $U^\dagger U=I$ is equivalent to: **the
columns of $U$ form an orthonormal set.** This is often the fastest way to
check unitarity by eye.

## Unitarity is probability conservation

Time evolution in quantum mechanics is generated by a unitary operator,
covered in full once the Wave Mechanics course reaches the Schrödinger
equation. That is why a normalized state stays normalized forever as it
evolves: unitarity *is* conservation of total probability, restated in
operator language.

This is also, concretely, what a real device's gate-calibration numbers
are measuring. Randomized benchmarking, run routinely on IBM, Google, and
IonQ hardware, applies long sequences of gates and tracks how the outcome
statistics decay with sequence length, which amounts to a direct measure
of how closely an implemented gate satisfies $U^\dagger U=I$. Leakage out
of the two-level computational subspace and decoherence during the gate
both show up as a measurable departure from unitarity, and that departure
is what a reported gate fidelity puts a number on.

## Hadamard tested, and S contrasted

**Verify the Hadamard matrix $H=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$
is unitary, and contrast it with $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$
from the last lesson (shown there to be non-Hermitian).**

<PredictBeforeReveal
  question="S is not Hermitian (shown last lesson). Does that mean S also fails to be unitary?"
  options={[
    { label: "Yes, non-Hermitian implies non-unitary", value: "non-unitary" },
    { label: "No, S can still be unitary", value: "unitary" },
    { label: "Can't tell without computing S†S", value: "unsure" },
  ]}
  correctValue="unitary"
  explanation="S is unitary despite not being Hermitian. The two properties are independent, and the computation below confirms S†S = I directly. A matrix can fail one condition and satisfy the other; there's no implication either way."
/>

$H$ is real and symmetric, so $H^\dagger=H$. Compute $H^2$:

$$
H^2 = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = I
$$

So $H^\dagger H = H\cdot H = I$: unitary. (Since $H=H^\dagger$ here, $H$
is also Hermitian, a matrix can be both, though that's special, not
generic: it means $H^2=I$.)

For $S$: $S^\dagger S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\cdot i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I$.
**$S$ is unitary**, even though the last lesson showed it is **not
Hermitian**, direct proof that the two properties are independent of
each other.

## Hermitian and unitary are different claims

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming Hermitian and unitary are the same thing, or that one implies
the other.** $S$ above is unitary but not Hermitian; a matrix like
$\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ is Hermitian but not unitary (its
eigenvalues, both $2$, don't have modulus $1$). The Pauli matrices happen
to be both, which is a special, not generic, coincidence.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to conjugate when checking unitarity of a complex matrix.**
The real-matrix analogue of unitary is "orthogonal," $Q^TQ=I$; for
complex matrices this must be $U^\dagger U=I$, conjugate transpose, not
plain transpose.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify that Pauli-$X$ is unitary by computing $X^\dagger X$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $X$ is real and symmetric, so $X^\dagger = X^\mathsf{T} = X$, and

   $$
   X^\dagger X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I \checkmark
   $$

   So $X$ is unitary, and it is also its own inverse: $X^{-1} = X^\dagger = X$,
   which matches the fact that flipping the two basis states twice returns you
   to where you started.

   $X$ is one of the rare operators that is Hermitian *and* unitary at once, and
   the two claims are worth keeping apart because they usually are apart.
   Hermitian is $A^\dagger = A$, which is what makes an operator an observable.
   Unitary is $U^\dagger U = I$, which is what makes it a legal evolution. An
   operator satisfying both must have $A^2 = I$, so its eigenvalues satisfy
   $\lambda^2 = 1$, forcing $\lambda = \pm1$: real (Hermitian) and of modulus 1
   (unitary), simultaneously. So the operators in both families are exactly the
   ones that square to the identity, which includes all three Pauli matrices and
   $I$ itself. Landing in the overlap is a real constraint, though, and
   $S = \mathrm{diag}(1,i)$ shows what falls outside it: unitary, since
   $|1| = |i| = 1$, but not Hermitian.

   </details>

2. Is $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1/2\end{pmatrix}$ unitary? Explain using the
   eigenvalue-modulus theorem, without doing the full matrix
   multiplication.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. The matrix is diagonal, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal entries, 2
   and $\tfrac12$. Their moduli are 2 and $0.5$, neither of which is 1. The
   theorem says every eigenvalue of a unitary operator has modulus exactly 1, so
   a single offending eigenvalue is enough to disqualify it, and here both
   offend.

   Physically the matrix stretches the first basis direction by a factor of 2
   and squashes the second by a factor of 2. A unitary preserves norms, so it
   can stretch nothing, and this one stretches $(1,0)$ to length 2. Total
   probability would not be conserved.

   The instructive part is the check that *fails* to catch it. The determinant
   is $2 \times \tfrac12 = 1$, and a unitary does satisfy $|\det U| = 1$, so a
   determinant test passes this matrix cleanly. That is because the determinant
   only sees the *product* of the eigenvalues, and here the stretch and the
   squash cancel in the product while doing violence individually.
   $|\det U| = 1$ is necessary and nowhere near sufficient, and this matrix is
   the standard counterexample to treating it as a criterion.

   The theorem, by contrast, constrains each eigenvalue separately, which is
   why it is the tool the question asks for.

   </details>

3. Check unitarity of $\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$
   by inspecting whether its columns form an orthonormal set.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Not unitary. The columns are $c_1 = (1,0)^\mathsf{T}$ and
   $c_2 = (0,2)^\mathsf{T}$.

   Orthogonal: $\langle c_1,c_2\rangle = (1)(0)+(0)(2) = 0 \checkmark$.

   Normalized: $\|c_1\| = 1 \checkmark$, but $\|c_2\| = \sqrt{0+4} = 2 \ne 1$,
   which fails.

   Orthonormal means both conditions, and passing one is not partial credit for
   the other. This matrix's columns are perpendicular but the second is twice
   too long, so the matrix maps the unit square to a $1\times2$ rectangle: right
   angles preserved, lengths not.

   The column test is not a separate criterion from $U^\dagger U = I$; it is
   that equation read entrywise. $(U^\dagger U)_{jk} = \sum_i U_{ij}^*U_{ik} = \langle c_j, c_k\rangle$,
   so "$U^\dagger U = I$" says precisely "$\langle c_j,c_k\rangle = \delta_{jk}$",
   which is orthonormality of the columns. Here $(U^\dagger U)_{22} = 4$, the
   squared length of the offending column, which is the same failure in the
   other notation.

   Cross-checking against question 2's theorem: the eigenvalues here are 1 and
   2, and $|2| \ne 1$, so that test rejects it too. Note that the determinant is
   2, so for this matrix even the weak determinant test would have caught it,
   which is exactly what makes $\mathrm{diag}(2,\tfrac12)$ the more instructive
   example of the pair.

   </details>

4. True or false, with justification: every unitary operator is
   invertible.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   True, and the inverse is handed to you by the definition.

   $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I$ says exactly that $U^\dagger$ satisfies the
   defining property of $U^{-1}$, so

   $$
   U^{-1} = U^\dagger.
   $$

   No existence argument is needed. Unlike a general invertible matrix, whose
   inverse has to be computed, a unitary's inverse is obtained by transposing
   and conjugating, which is why undoing a quantum circuit means running its
   gates backwards with each one daggered.

   A second route reaches the same place and explains *why* it could not have
   gone otherwise. A unitary preserves norms,
   $\|U|v\rangle\| = \||v\rangle\|$, so $U|v\rangle = 0$ forces
   $\||v\rangle\| = 0$ and hence $|v\rangle = 0$. Nothing nonzero is sent to
   zero, so no information is destroyed, and the map is invertible by the same
   criterion the Linear Operators lesson used on
   $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$.

   This is the formal content of "quantum evolution is reversible". Every
   isolated quantum process is a unitary, every unitary has an inverse that is
   itself a unitary, so the dynamics can always in principle be run backwards.
   Measurement is the exception, and it is an exception precisely because
   collapse is not a unitary: the projector $|0\rangle\langle0|$ sends
   $|1\rangle$ to zero, and that is irreversible.

   </details>

## Summary

- $U$ is unitary if $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I$.
- Unitary operators preserve inner products, hence norms, derived
  directly from the adjoint definition.
- Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator has modulus $1$.
- A unitary operator's columns form an orthonormal basis; Hermitian and
  unitary are independent properties, not synonyms.
