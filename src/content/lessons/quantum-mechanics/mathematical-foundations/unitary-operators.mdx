## Motivation

A quantum state's norm is tied to total probability — the Probability and
Quantum States lesson makes this precise, but the short version is that a
normalized state must *stay* normalized as it evolves or gets transformed,
or probability would leak away or overshoot $100\%$. The operators
guaranteed to preserve norms are called **unitary**, and every quantum
gate, every basis change, and every step of time evolution in quantum
mechanics is one.

## Intuition

If Hermitian operators are the matrix analogue of a real number,
**unitary** operators are the matrix analogue of a complex number with
modulus $1$ — a pure phase $e^{i\theta}$, which rotates but never
rescales. Multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ never changes a complex number's
modulus (from the Complex Numbers lesson); a unitary operator is the
higher-dimensional version of that same idea, never changing a vector's
length.

## Mathematical Development

### Definition

$U$ is **unitary** if $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I$ — equivalently,
$U^\dagger = U^{-1}$.

### Theorem: unitary operators preserve inner products

$$
\langle Uu|Uv\rangle = \langle u|U^\dagger U|v\rangle = \langle u|I|v\rangle = \langle u|v\rangle
$$

(the first equality is the adjoint definition, pulling $U^\dagger$ across
from the bra side onto the ket side; the second uses $U^\dagger U=I$
directly). Setting $u=v$ gives the important special case: unitary
operators preserve **norms**,

$$
\|Uv\| = \|v\|
$$

<Callout type="note">
This is precisely why every gate you've met, if you've studied the
Quantum Computing track — $X$, $H$, $S$, $\mathrm{CNOT}$, all of them — is
unitary: a gate must take a normalized state to another normalized state,
and this theorem is the reason that requirement is equivalent to
unitarity.
</Callout>

### Theorem: eigenvalues of a unitary operator have modulus $1$

Let $U|v\rangle=\lambda|v\rangle$, $v\ne0$, $U$ unitary. Norm preservation
gives $\|Uv\|=\|v\|$, i.e. $\|\lambda v\| = \|v\|$, i.e.
$|\lambda|\,\|v\| = \|v\|$. Since $\|v\|\ne0$, this forces $|\lambda|=1$.
Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator is a complex number of modulus
$1$ — necessarily of the form $e^{i\theta}$ for some real $\theta$,
directly recalling the $|e^{i\theta}|=1$ fact from the Complex Numbers
lesson.

### Unitary matrices as basis transformations

If $\{|e_i\rangle\}$ and $\{|f_i\rangle\}$ are two orthonormal bases,
there is a unitary $U$ with $U|e_i\rangle=|f_i\rangle$ for every $i$:
define $U$ this way on the basis and extend linearly. It preserves inner
products because both bases are orthonormal —
$\langle Ue_i|Ue_j\rangle=\langle f_i|f_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}=\langle
e_i|e_j\rangle$ — matching the definition of a unitary map exactly. This
is the operator-level version of the "change of basis" idea from the
Vector Spaces lesson: moving between two orthonormal bases is always a
unitary transformation.

### Columns form an orthonormal basis

$U^\dagger U = I$ says something concrete entry-by-entry: the $(i,j)$
entry of $U^\dagger U$ is exactly the inner product of $U$'s $i$-th column
with $U$'s $j$-th column. So $U^\dagger U=I$ is equivalent to: **the
columns of $U$ form an orthonormal set.** This is often the fastest way to
check unitarity by eye.

## Physical Interpretation

Time evolution in quantum mechanics is generated by a unitary operator —
covered in full once the Wave Mechanics course reaches the Schrödinger
equation — which is exactly why a normalized state stays normalized
forever as it evolves: unitarity *is* conservation of total probability,
restated in operator language.

## Worked Example

**Verify the Hadamard matrix $H=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$
is unitary, and contrast it with $S=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$
from the last lesson (shown there to be non-Hermitian).**

$H$ is real and symmetric, so $H^\dagger=H$. Compute $H^2$:

$$
H^2 = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix} = I
$$

So $H^\dagger H = H\cdot H = I$ — unitary. (Since $H=H^\dagger$ here, $H$
is also Hermitian — a matrix can be both, though that's special, not
generic: it means $H^2=I$.)

For $S$: $S^\dagger S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\cdot i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I$.
**$S$ is unitary**, even though the last lesson showed it is **not
Hermitian** — direct proof that the two properties are independent of
each other.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming Hermitian and unitary are the same thing, or that one implies
the other.** $S$ above is unitary but not Hermitian; a matrix like
$\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ is Hermitian but not unitary (its
eigenvalues, both $2$, don't have modulus $1$). The Pauli matrices happen
to be both, which is a special, not generic, coincidence.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting to conjugate when checking unitarity of a complex matrix.**
The real-matrix analogue of unitary is "orthogonal," $Q^TQ=I$ — for
complex matrices this must be $U^\dagger U=I$, conjugate transpose, not
plain transpose.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $U$ is unitary if $U^\dagger U = UU^\dagger = I$.
- Unitary operators preserve inner products, hence norms — derived
  directly from the adjoint definition.
- Every eigenvalue of a unitary operator has modulus $1$.
- A unitary operator's columns form an orthonormal basis; Hermitian and
  unitary are independent properties, not synonyms.
