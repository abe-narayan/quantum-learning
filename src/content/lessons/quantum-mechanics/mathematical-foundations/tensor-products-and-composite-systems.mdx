## Two systems, described together

Two separate physical systems, two electrons, two qubits, a particle and
a measuring device, need to be describable *together*, as one combined
system, without losing the ability to describe superpositions across
them. Simply pairing up states the way a Cartesian product would (treat
"system A in state $i$, system B in state $j$" as one of $nm$ classical
combinations) throws away the one thing that makes the theory quantum:
the ability to superpose across the two systems. The construction that keeps
that structure is the **<Term id="tensor-product">tensor product</Term>**.

Schrödinger named the phenomenon in 1935, calling it *Verschränkung*, and
described it as "the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics" rather
than a wrinkle in it. This lesson shows why that emphasis was warranted:
the failure to factor is not an exotic case; it is what a dimension count
predicts for almost every state.

## When nm stops being a count and becomes a dimension

Classically, two systems with $n$ and $m$ possible states have $nm$
possible joint states, full stop, nothing more to say. Quantum
mechanically, that count of $nm$ becomes the *dimension of a vector
space*, not the number of allowed outcomes, and a <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term> of
dimension $nm$ contains vastly more than just $nm$ <Term id="basis">basis</Term> combinations. It
contains every superposition of them too. That extra room is where
entanglement lives.

## Construction, bilinearity, and the dimension count

### Construction and dimension

Given vector spaces $V$ (dimension $n$, basis $\{|e_i\rangle\}$) and $W$
(dimension $m$, basis $\{|f_j\rangle\}$), their **tensor product**
$V\otimes W$ is the vector space with basis $\{|e_i\rangle\otimes
|f_j\rangle\}$, one basis vector for every pair $(i,j)$, so
$\dim(V\otimes W) = nm$. Two vector spaces of dimension $3$ and $4$
combine into one of dimension $12$; nothing here is specific to qubits or
to equal dimensions.

### Bilinearity

The tensor product of two general vectors $|u\rangle=\sum_i u_i|e_i\rangle$
and $|v\rangle=\sum_j v_j|f_j\rangle$ is defined by requiring
**bilinearity**, linearity in each argument separately:

$$
(a|u_1\rangle+b|u_2\rangle)\otimes|v\rangle = a(|u_1\rangle\otimes|v\rangle) + b(|u_2\rangle\otimes|v\rangle)
$$

and similarly in the second slot. Applying this to the basis expansions
gives the concrete formula:

$$
|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle = \sum_{i,j} u_iv_j\, |e_i\rangle\otimes|f_j\rangle
$$

For coordinate vectors, this is the **Kronecker product**: list every
product $u_iv_j$, in the order that runs through all of $j$ for each fixed
$i$ before moving to the next $i$. Concretely, for $u=(1,2)\in\mathbb{C}^2$
and $v=(0,1,-1)\in\mathbb{C}^3$:

$$
u\otimes v = (1\cdot0,\ 1\cdot1,\ 1\cdot(-1),\ 2\cdot0,\ 2\cdot1,\ 2\cdot(-1)) = (0,1,-1,0,2,-2) \in \mathbb{C}^6
$$

matching $\dim(\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^3)=2\cdot3=6$. This
construction doesn't care that $u$ and $v$ have different dimensions, or
that neither is $2$. The qubit case ($\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2$)
is the smallest, most familiar example, not the general rule.

### The inner product on a tensor product space

Inner products combine multiplicatively on simple tensors:

$$
\langle u\otimes v,\ u'\otimes v'\rangle = \langle u,u'\rangle\langle v,v'\rangle
$$

In particular, if both $\{e_i\}$ and $\{f_j\}$ are <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal</Term>, then
$\{e_i\otimes f_j\}$ is orthonormal too:
$\langle e_i\otimes f_j,\ e_k\otimes f_l\rangle = \langle
e_i,e_k\rangle\langle f_j,f_l\rangle = \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}$, nonzero,
and equal to $1$, only when both indices match.

### Operators on one subsystem

An operator acting on subsystem $A$ alone, leaving subsystem $B$
untouched, is represented as $A\otimes I$, defined on simple tensors by
$(A\otimes I)(|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle) = (A|u\rangle)\otimes|v\rangle$
and extended linearly to the rest of the space. This is what "apply a gate
to qubit 0 only, leaving qubit 1 alone" means, if you've seen quantum
circuits: $A\otimes I$ acting on the whole two-qubit space.

### Why not every vector is a simple product: a dimension count

Everything above sets up a single counting argument. A simple
tensor $|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle$ is specified by choosing a direction
for $|u\rangle$ (up to scale, $n-1$ free complex parameters) and a
direction for $|v\rangle$ ($m-1$ free parameters) and one overall scale,
a family with roughly $n+m-1$ free parameters. But $V\otimes W$ has
dimension $nm$. For $n,m\ge2$, $nm$ grows far faster than $n+m-1$: for
two qubits, $nm=4$ against $n+m-1=3$; for two 3-level systems, $nm=9$
against $n+m-1=5$. So **the simple tensors form only a small slice of
the whole space**, and a generic vector in $V\otimes W$ *cannot* be
written as $|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle$ for any single choice of $u,v$.
That failure to factor is exactly what **entanglement** is: not a strange
exception, but the mathematically generic situation, with product states
as the special, measure-zero case.

<InsightBlock>
  This counting is also the reason simulating quantum systems on a
  classical computer is hard. If every state factored, you could store
  $N$ subsystems ($N$ counts subsystems here, not dimensions) by storing
  each one separately, at a cost growing linearly in $N$. Because a
  generic state does not factor, you have to store all $nm\cdots$
  coefficients of the full product space, which for $N$ qubits is $2^N$
  numbers: the memory required doubles with each qubit added.
</InsightBlock>

If you've seen the Bell state
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ proven non-factorizable by
direct contradiction in the Quantum Computing track, this dimension count
is the structural reason that kind of proof was always going to succeed:
there is not enough room in the space of simple products to reach every
vector.

## A postulate, confirmed by every experiment

Composite quantum systems are described by the tensor product of their
parts' state spaces. This is itself a postulate of quantum mechanics,
confirmed by every experiment ever run on multi-particle systems, rather
than a consequence of anything more basic. Take that postulate seriously
and the dimension count from earlier tells you what to expect: a typical
vector in a large composite space is entangled, with a product state as
the rare exception, not the delicate, hard-to-prepare object it's often
made out to be.

## Is the Bell state a simple tensor?

**Is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ a simple tensor
$|u\rangle\otimes|v\rangle$ for some $u,v\in\mathbb{C}^2$?**

  question="Before working it out algebraically: is (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 expressible as u⊗v for some single-qubit u, v?"
  options={[
    { label: "Yes, some u, v work", value: "yes" },
    { label: "No, no u, v work", value: "no" },
    { label: "Only if u, v are allowed complex entries", value: "complex-only" },
    { label: "Can't tell without trying every u, v", value: "unsure" },
  ]}
  correctValue="no"
  explanation="No u, v work, for any complex entries. This is the Bell state, the standard example of entanglement. The dimension-counting argument above already told you to expect this: simple tensors are a thin slice of a 4-dimensional space, and this particular vector misses that slice. The elimination below shows where the attempt breaks down."
/>

Suppose it were, with $u=(u_0,u_1)$, $v=(v_0,v_1)$. Then $u\otimes v =
(u_0v_0,\ u_0v_1,\ u_1v_0,\ u_1v_1)$, and matching against
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,0,1)$ requires $u_0v_0=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$,
$u_0v_1=0$, $u_1v_0=0$, $u_1v_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. From $u_0v_1=0$: either
$u_0=0$ (then $u_0v_0=0\ne\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, contradiction) or $v_1=0$
(then $u_1v_1=0\ne\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction). No $u,v$ work,
confirming, by direct computation this time rather than dimension
counting alone, that this state is entangled.

### A faster test for two qubits

That elimination generalizes into a check you can run by eye. Write a
two-qubit state's four coefficients $c_{00},c_{01},c_{10},c_{11}$ as a
$2\times2$ array, with the first qubit's index labelling the row:

<MatrixGrid
  matrix={productCoeffMatrix}
  label="|0⟩⊗|+⟩ coefficients"
  compareTo={bellCoeffMatrix}
  compareLabel="Bell state coefficients"
  ariaLabel="Two two-by-two coefficient arrays side by side. The left one, for the product state, has 0.71 in both entries of its top row and zeros in its bottom row. The right one, for the Bell state, has 0.71 on the two diagonal entries and zeros off the diagonal."
/>

If the state factors as $u\otimes v$, then $c_{ij}=u_iv_j$, so the second
row of the array is $u_1/u_0$ times the first: the two rows are
proportional, and the determinant $c_{00}c_{11}-c_{01}c_{10}$ vanishes.
The left array passes that test, $(0.71)(0)-(0.71)(0)=0$, and indeed
$|0\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ factors by construction. The right array gives
$(0.71)(0.71)-0=0.5\ne0$, so no $u,v$ can reproduce it, which is the
elimination above compressed into a single number.

That number has a name once you meet it again: $2|c_{00}c_{11} -
c_{01}c_{10}|$ is the **concurrence** of a two-qubit *pure* state, a
standard measure of how entangled it is (mixed states, which this course
has not met yet, get a longer definition that reduces to this one). It
runs from $0$ for every product state to $1$ for the Bell state above.

## Kronecker ordering, and the product assumption

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming every vector in a tensor product space is automatically a
simple product.** As just shown, most aren't; that's the entire content
of entanglement.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Getting the Kronecker ordering backwards.** This course (matching the
Quantum Computing track's convention) always lists the first system's
index as the more significant one: hold the first index at $0$ and run
the second index through all of its values, then move the first index to
$1$ and run the second through again, and so on. For $u\otimes v$ with
$u\in\mathbb{C}^2$ that reads $(u_0v_0,\ u_0v_1,\ \ldots,\ u_1v_0,\
u_1v_1,\ \ldots)$, never the other way round.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. What is $\dim(V\otimes W)$ if $V$ has dimension $5$ and $W$ has
   dimension $2$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \dim(V\otimes W) = 5 \times 2 = 10.
   $$

   Dimensions multiply, because a basis for the product space is built by
   pairing every basis vector of $V$ with every basis vector of $W$:
   $\{|v_i\rangle\otimes|w_j\rangle\}$ has $5\times2 = 10$ members, and they are
   independent and spanning.

   The answer to reject is 7. Adding dimensions is what happens for a **direct
   sum** $V\oplus W$, which is the space of pairs "either a $V$ vector or a $W$
   vector, side by side". The tensor product is the space of *joint* states, and
   the difference between the two is the difference between a system that is in
   one subsystem or the other and a system that has both at once.

   This one multiplication is the origin of the whole difficulty of simulating
   quantum systems. Each additional qubit multiplies the dimension by 2 rather
   than adding 1, so $n$ qubits need $2^n$ complex amplitudes, and a classical
   machine storing them runs out of memory somewhere around 50 qubits. Nothing
   subtler than "dimensions multiply" is required to see it.

   </details>

2. Compute $u\otimes v$ for $u=(1,0,-1)\in\mathbb{C}^3$ and
   $v=(2,1)\in\mathbb{C}^2$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   u\otimes v = (2,\ 1,\ 0,\ 0,\ -2,\ -1) \in \mathbb{C}^6.
   $$

   Each component of $u$ scales a whole copy of $v$, and the copies are stacked
   in $u$'s order:

   $$
   u\otimes v = \left(u_1 v_1,\ u_1 v_2,\ u_2 v_1,\ u_2 v_2,\ u_3 v_1,\ u_3 v_2\right) = (1\cdot2,\ 1\cdot1,\ 0\cdot2,\ 0\cdot1,\ -1\cdot2,\ -1\cdot1).
   $$

   Two checks are available without redoing the products. The dimension must be
   $3\times2 = 6 \checkmark$. And norms multiply on simple tensors:
   $\|u\| = \sqrt2$, $\|v\| = \sqrt5$, and
   $\|u\otimes v\| = \sqrt{4+1+0+0+4+1} = \sqrt{10} = \sqrt2\cdot\sqrt5 \checkmark$.

   The mistakes worth naming are both about ordering. Producing
   $(2,1,0,0,-2,-1)$ requires $u$'s index to run *slowly* and $v$'s to run
   *fast*; letting them run the other way gives $v\otimes u$, a different
   vector. And componentwise multiplication, which would need the two vectors to
   have the same length and is not defined here at all, is a different operation
   entirely. The tensor product produces every pairwise product, which is why
   the output is longer than either input rather than the same length.

   </details>

3. Is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|01\rangle)$ a simple tensor? If so,
   find $u,v$; if not, explain why.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. Factor the first qubit out:

   $$
   \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|00\rangle-|01\rangle\right) = |0\rangle\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle-|1\rangle\right) = |0\rangle\otimes|-\rangle,
   $$

   so $u = |0\rangle = (1,0)$ and $v = |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

   The factoring is available because both terms share the same first qubit.
   Every basis term has $|0\rangle$ in the first slot, so the first qubit is in
   a definite state, and only the second is doing anything. This is a product
   state: measuring qubit 1 always gives 0 and tells you nothing about qubit 2,
   and measuring qubit 2 gives $0$ or $1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each,
   independently.

   The systematic test, for cases where the factoring is not obvious: write the
   amplitudes as a $2\times2$ grid indexed by (first qubit, second qubit),

   $$
   \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
   $$

   and check its determinant. A simple tensor has amplitude
   $c_{ij} = u_i v_j$, which makes the grid a rank-1 outer product, so its
   determinant vanishes. Here $\tfrac12\left[(1)(0)-(-1)(0)\right] = 0$: simple.
   Run the same test on the Bell state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$
   and the grid is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ with
   determinant $\tfrac12 \ne 0$: entangled.

   The trap in this question is the minus sign, which makes the state resemble
   the Bell-like $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|11\rangle)$ at a glance. That
   one *is* entangled. What matters is not the sign but which slots vary: here
   only the second index changes between the two terms, there both change
   together.

   </details>

4. Explain in your own words why a composite system of two 10-dimensional
   subsystems has "more room" for entangled states than a composite of
   two 2-dimensional ones.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the total dimension grows like a product while the family of product
   states grows like a sum, so the gap between them widens.

   Put the two cases side by side using the counts from this lesson,
   $\dim(V\otimes W) = nm$ against roughly $n+m-1$ parameters for a simple
   tensor:

   For two qubits, $n=m=2$: the space has dimension $4$, and product states form
   a family with about $3$ parameters. The shortfall is $1$.

   For two 10-level systems, $n=m=10$: the space has dimension $100$, and
   product states form a family with about $19$ parameters. The shortfall is
   $81$.

   So in the two-qubit case product states are a 3-parameter slice of a
   4-dimensional space, a thin slice but not a negligible one. In the
   ten-level case they are a 19-parameter slice of a 100-dimensional space, and
   almost every direction you could move in leads out of the product family into
   an entangled state.

   The asymmetry has one source. Specifying a product state means specifying
   each subsystem separately, and separate descriptions add up. Specifying a
   general joint state means giving an amplitude for every *combination* of
   subsystem states, and combinations multiply. Addition never keeps pace with
   multiplication, and the gap between them is precisely the room entanglement
   occupies.

   One caution about "more room". Both families are infinite sets, so this is
   not a claim that one contains more states than the other in any
   counting sense; it is a statement about *dimension*, the number of
   independent knobs. The right mental image is a surface inside a solid, not a
   small pile next to a large one, and as the dimensions grow the surface
   becomes vanishingly thin relative to the solid it sits in.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\dim(V\otimes W) = \dim(V)\cdot\dim(W)$, for spaces of any dimension,
  not just equal ones, and not just qubits.
- The tensor product is bilinear; on coordinates it's the Kronecker
  product, and inner products multiply on simple tensors.
- $A\otimes I$ represents an operator acting on one subsystem only.
- Dimension counting shows simple tensors are a small slice of $V\otimes
  W$ in general: entanglement is the generic case, not a special one.
