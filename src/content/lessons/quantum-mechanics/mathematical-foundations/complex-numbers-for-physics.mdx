## Motivation

Real numbers can't solve $x^2 = -1$. For most of the history of mathematics
that was fine: nothing in ordinary experience needed such a solution. Then
physics ran into equations whose *real* solutions didn't tell the whole
story. A damped oscillator's equation of motion, an AC circuit's voltage,
a wave's oscillation, all have natural descriptions in terms of a number
system where $\sqrt{-1}$ exists. In classical physics, complex numbers are
almost always a convenience: you do the calculation with them and take
the real part at the end, and you could have done the whole thing with
sines and cosines instead, just more painfully.

Quantum mechanics is different. The complex numbers aren't a shortcut you
could avoid with more work. The theory's central objects (amplitudes) are
inherently complex, and a version of quantum mechanics built only from real
numbers genuinely predicts different, wrong physics. We won't prove that
claim in this lesson (it takes the whole framework this course builds to
even state precisely), but it's why this course starts here rather than
with vectors or physics.

## Intuition

A complex number $z = a + bi$ can be pictured as a point $(a, b)$ in the
**complex plane**, rather than a point on a line. Addition
works exactly like vector addition. Multiplication is the interesting
part: multiplying by $i$ doesn't move you along either axis, it **rotates**
you $90°$ counterclockwise. Check it directly: $i \cdot (a + bi) = ai +
bi^2 = -b + ai$, which is the point $(a,b)$ rotated a quarter turn. Once
you see multiplication as a geometric operation (rotate and rescale) rather
than just an algebra rule, polar form and Euler's formula stop looking
mysterious. They're just the natural language for describing rotation and
scaling.

## Mathematical Development

### Rectangular form and arithmetic

A complex number is $z = a + bi$, with $a = \operatorname{Re}(z)$ and
$b = \operatorname{Im}(z)$ ordinary real numbers, and $i$ defined by
$i^2 = -1$. Addition is component-wise:

$$
(a+bi) + (c+di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i
$$

Multiplication follows from distributing and using $i^2 = -1$:

$$
(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad+bc)i
$$

### Conjugation and modulus

The **complex conjugate** $z^* = a - bi$ flips the sign of the imaginary
part. Multiplying $z$ by its own conjugate always gives a non-negative real
number:

$$
zz^* = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - abi + abi - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2
$$

That non-negative real quantity defines the **modulus**:

$$
|z| = \sqrt{zz^*} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}
$$

This single fact, that $zz^*$ is always real and non-negative, is the reason
complex numbers can be turned into probabilities at all: whatever a
"probability amplitude" turns out to be, multiplying it by its own
conjugate always produces something you can legally call a probability.

### Polar form

Rectangular coordinates $(a,b)$ and polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$
describe the same point two ways, related by $a = r\cos\theta$,
$b = r\sin\theta$:

$$
z = a+bi = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta), \qquad r = |z|
$$

The next section derives a famously tidy relationship between this polar
form and the exponential function. Before seeing the derivation, predict
what kind of relationship it is.

### Deriving Euler's formula

The expression $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ turns out to equal a single
exponential, $e^{i\theta}$: this is **Euler's formula**. It's worth deriving,
not just accepting, given the prediction above. The Taylor series for $e^x$ converges for complex
inputs too, so substitute $x = i\theta$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = 1 + i\theta + \frac{(i\theta)^2}{2!} + \frac{(i\theta)^3}{3!} + \frac{(i\theta)^4}{4!} + \frac{(i\theta)^5}{5!} + \cdots
$$

Using $i^2=-1,\ i^3=-i,\ i^4=1,\ i^5=i,\ \ldots$ (the pattern repeats every
four powers) and separating real terms (even powers of $i\theta$) from
imaginary terms (odd powers):

$$
e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots\right)}_{\text{this is }\cos\theta} \;+\; i\underbrace{\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)}_{\text{this is }\sin\theta}
$$

The two bracketed series are exactly the Maclaurin series for $\cos\theta$
and $\sin\theta$, a fact you can look up independently and confirm
matches term by term. So:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad z = re^{i\theta}
$$

A useful immediate consequence: $|e^{i\theta}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta} = 1$
for every real $\theta$. Multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ never changes a
number's modulus. It only rotates it. Keep this fact; it resurfaces
directly in the Unitary Operators lesson. It's easier to feel this as a
fact about rotation, not just an algebraic identity, by watching it happen
rather than only reading it:

### Multiplication in polar form: phases add

Multiply $z_1 = r_1e^{i\theta_1}$ and $z_2 = r_2e^{i\theta_2}$ using
ordinary exponent rules:

$$
z_1 z_2 = r_1r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}
$$

**Magnitudes multiply; phases add.** Conjugation in polar form is simple
too: $z^* = re^{-i\theta}$, same modulus, negated phase (check:
$\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta) = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$, matching
$a - bi$ directly).

### De Moivre's theorem and roots of unity

Raising $e^{i\theta}$ to an integer power is immediate from the exponential
form: $(e^{i\theta})^n = e^{in\theta}$, i.e.

$$
(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta)
$$

This is **De Moivre's theorem**, a one-line consequence of Euler's formula
rather than a separate fact to memorize. It answers a question that looks
hard in rectangular form: solve $z^n = 1$. Writing $z = e^{i\theta}$,
the equation becomes $e^{in\theta}=1=e^{i\cdot 0}$, so $n\theta$ must be an
integer multiple of $2\pi$:

$$
z_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1
$$

These $n$ **roots of unity** sit evenly spaced around the unit circle.
They'll reappear if you go on to study the quantum Fourier transform,
where this exact spacing is the whole mechanism.

### A forward look: complex numbers as $2\times2$ real matrices

Here is a fact that previews the rest of this course: the number $i$ can be
represented by the real matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, a
$90°$ rotation matching the geometric picture from the Intuition section
exactly, and a general complex number $a+bi$ by
$\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$. Multiply two such matrices and
check that the result matches complex multiplication:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c&-d\\d&c\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}ac-bd & -(ad+bc)\\ad+bc & ac-bd\end{pmatrix}
$$

which is exactly the matrix for $(ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i$, the rectangular-form
product derived above. Complex numbers aren't *needed* in some absolute
sense (they can be simulated by real $2\times2$ matrices), but they're
dramatically more convenient. Once this course reaches Linear Operators,
you'll have the language to see this as: complex numbers are one
particular, especially simple, family of linear operators.

## Physical Interpretation

In quantum mechanics, a state is described by complex numbers called
**probability amplitudes**. An amplitude's modulus-squared will turn out to
be a probability (the Probability and Quantum States lesson makes this
precise); its **phase** carries information a plain probability can't:
two amplitudes with the same modulus but different phases behave
differently when added together, which is the origin of quantum
interference. None of that can be built from real numbers alone in a way
that reproduces the actual experimental results. Real amplitudes can
still cancel (interfere) when they have opposite sign, but real numbers
give you only two "directions" (positive and negative) to interfere with,
while a complex phase gives you a full continuous circle of them. That
richer structure is what the rest of quantum mechanics needs.

## Worked Example

**Compute $e^{i\pi}$, and find the three cube roots of unity.**

Euler's formula with $\theta=\pi$: $e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1 + 0
= -1$. This is **Euler's identity**, $e^{i\pi}+1=0$, often called the most
beautiful equation in mathematics for tying together five fundamental
constants, but it's nothing more than Euler's formula evaluated at one
particular angle.

For the cube roots of unity ($n=3$ in the roots-of-unity formula above):

$$
z_k = e^{2\pi i k/3}, \quad k=0,1,2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_0 = 1,\quad z_1 = e^{2\pi i/3} = -\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i,\quad z_2 = e^{4\pi i/3} = -\tfrac12-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i
$$

You can verify $z_1^3=1$ directly: $z_1^3 = e^{2\pi i} = \cos(2\pi)+i\sin(2\pi)=1$. ✓

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating $i$ as an unknown to "solve for" instead of a fixed object with
$i^2=-1$.** Once you accept the definition, every subsequent step is
ordinary algebra: the only special rule you ever need is $i^2=-1$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing a complex number with its modulus.** $z$ carries both magnitude
*and* phase; $|z|$ throws the phase away entirely. A quantum amplitude and
its modulus are not interchangeable: the modulus alone can't reproduce
interference.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**$|z_1+z_2| \neq |z_1|+|z_2|$ in general.** Moduli only add when the two
numbers point in the same direction (same phase). This is precisely what
makes interference possible: two amplitudes of equal modulus but opposite
phase can sum to a modulus of zero.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A complex number $z=a+bi$ lives in the complex plane; $i$ acts as a
  $90°$ rotation.
- Conjugation and modulus: $z^*=a-bi$, $|z|=\sqrt{zz^*}=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$,
  always real and non-negative.
- Euler's formula, derived from the Taylor series:
  $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$; consequently $|e^{i\theta}|=1$.
- In polar form, multiplication multiplies magnitudes and **adds phases**:
  $z_1z_2=r_1r_2e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$.
- De Moivre's theorem and the $n$th roots of unity follow directly from
  Euler's formula.
- A complex number can be represented as a real $2\times2$ matrix, a
  preview of Linear Operators.
