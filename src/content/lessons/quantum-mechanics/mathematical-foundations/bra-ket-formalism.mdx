## Motivation

Every result from the last two lessons, inner products, norms,
orthogonality, can be written in ordinary vector and matrix notation. But
quantum mechanics almost never is written that way, because Paul Dirac
invented a notation compact enough to make the extra structure (operators,
in particular) far easier to write and reason about. Learning it properly
once here saves reconstructing it piecemeal every time a later lesson
introduces another fragment of it.

## Intuition

A **ket** $|v\rangle$ is just a name for a vector, nothing new yet, only
notation. The payoff comes from its partner, the **bra** $\langle v|$,
because a bra and a ket combine into $\langle u|v\rangle$. Read
left-to-right it looks like "bra-c-ket," which is where the names come
from, and that combination is exactly the <Term id="inner-product">inner product</Term> from the last
lesson.

## Mathematical Development

### Ket, bra, and the inner product

$|v\rangle$ denotes a vector, concretely, for $\mathbb{C}^n$, a column
vector. The bra $\langle v|$ is its **conjugate transpose**: lay the
column on its side and conjugate every entry.

$$
|v\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}v_1\\\vdots\\v_n\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle v| = \begin{pmatrix}v_1^* & \cdots & v_n^*\end{pmatrix}
$$

A bra times a ket is ordinary matrix multiplication of a row by a column,
a single number, and exactly the standard inner product:

$$
\langle u|v\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}u_1^*&\cdots&u_n^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\\vdots\\v_n\end{pmatrix} = \sum_i u_i^*v_i
$$

The conjugate-symmetry and linearity axioms from the last lesson carry
over unchanged: $\langle u|v\rangle=\langle v|u\rangle^*$, and
$\langle u|v\rangle$ is linear in $|v\rangle$, conjugate-linear in
$\langle u|$.

### More abstractly: bras as linear functionals

More generally (beyond concrete column vectors), a bra is a **linear
functional**, a linear map from $V$ to $\mathbb{C}$, defined by how it
acts on kets: $\langle v|$ acts on $|w\rangle$ to produce the number
$\langle v,w\rangle$. Every inner product space has exactly one such
functional for each vector $|v\rangle$; the concrete conjugate-transpose
recipe above is what that functional looks like once you write vectors as
columns. This lesson mostly works with the concrete picture, but the more
abstract statement is what lets <Term id="dirac-notation">bra-ket notation</Term> extend cleanly to
infinite-dimensional spaces (like wavefunctions) later in the Quantum
Mechanics pillar, where there's no column of numbers to transpose.

### The outer product

Reversing the order, $|u\rangle\langle v|$, a ket times a bra, is a
column times a row, which produces a **matrix**, not a number:

$$
|u\rangle\langle v| = \begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1^*&v_2^*\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}u_1v_1^* & u_1v_2^*\\u_2v_1^*&u_2v_2^*\end{pmatrix}
$$

This **outer product** is an operator: applied to a ket $|w\rangle$, it
gives $|u\rangle\langle v|w\rangle = \langle v|w\rangle\,|u\rangle$, the
number $\langle v|w\rangle$ times the vector $|u\rangle$. A concrete and
important example: $|0\rangle\langle0|$ for the standard <Term id="basis">basis</Term> of
$\mathbb{C}^2$ is

$$
|0\rangle\langle0| = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

which sends $|0\rangle\mapsto|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle\mapsto0$: it
**projects** onto $|0\rangle$. Projectors built from outer products are
central to how measurement is described mathematically, developed in the
Probability and Quantum States lesson.

Geometrically, $|0\rangle\langle0|$ drops any vector straight onto the
$|0\rangle$ axis: $v=(0.6,0.8)$ lands at $|0\rangle\langle0|v=(0.6,0)$,
discarding whatever component $v$ had along $|1\rangle$ and keeping only
the part already lying along $|0\rangle$ — the "shadow" $v$ casts on that
axis.

### The completeness relation

For an <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term> $\{|e_i\rangle\}$, the sum of every basis
vector's projector equals the **identity operator**:

$$
\sum_i |e_i\rangle\langle e_i| = I
$$

Every remaining lesson in this course leans on this identity, so it's
worth seeing where it actually comes from rather than filing it away as a
rule.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Apply the left side to an arbitrary |ψ⟩, expanded in this basis as |ψ⟩ = Σⱼ cⱼ|eⱼ⟩, and just move the sum's bra-ket around.">
$$
\left(\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\right)|\psi\rangle = \sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = \sum_i \langle e_i|\psi\rangle\,|e_i\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Since {eᵢ} is orthonormal, ⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩ = ⟨eᵢ|Σⱼ cⱼeⱼ⟩ = Σⱼ cⱼ⟨eᵢ|eⱼ⟩ = cᵢ — every term but j=i vanishes.">
$$
\sum_i \langle e_i|\psi\rangle\,|e_i\rangle = \sum_i c_i|e_i\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="This is exactly |ψ⟩ by how it was expanded in the first place — the completeness relation acts as the identity on every vector, so it equals the identity operator.">
$$
\sum_i c_i|e_i\rangle = |\psi\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Operator matrix elements

For an operator $A$, the number $\langle i|A|j\rangle$ (a bra, then $A$,
then a ket, read as $\langle i|$ acting on the vector $A|j\rangle$) is
called a **matrix element**, and it's literally the $(i,j)$ entry of $A$'s
matrix in the basis $\{|i\rangle\}$. The Linear Operators lesson develops
this fully; for a preview, take $A$ to be the Pauli-$X$ matrix
$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (the quantum NOT gate, if you've
seen quantum computing): $X|1\rangle = |0\rangle$, so

$$
\langle0|X|1\rangle = \langle0|0\rangle = 1
$$

matching the top-right entry of $X$'s matrix directly.

### The adjoint (dagger) notation

The bra $\langle v|$ is called the **adjoint** or **dagger** of the ket
$|v\rangle$, written $|v\rangle^\dagger=\langle v|$. The same dagger
notation extends to matrices: for any matrix $M$, $M^\dagger$ is its
conjugate transpose, $(M^\dagger)_{ij}=M_{ji}^*$. This single symbol will
carry the entire Hermitian Operators lesson.

## Physical Interpretation

The completeness relation is the formal version of "any state can be
decomposed into basis states, and the decomposition is unique," the same
fact from the Vector Spaces lesson about coordinates, now written as an
operator identity rather than a sentence. Projectors like
$|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ are the mathematical objects that represent
"asking whether the system is in state $|e_i\rangle$."

## Worked Example

**Verify the completeness relation for $\mathbb{C}^2$'s standard basis by
computing $|0\rangle\langle0|+|1\rangle\langle1|$ directly.**

$$
|0\rangle\langle0| + |1\rangle\langle1| = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I
$$

Matches the identity matrix exactly, confirming the general derivation
above for this specific case.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing $|u\rangle\langle v|$ with $\langle u|v\rangle$.** The first is
an operator (a matrix); the second is a number. Getting the order of bra
and ket backwards changes what kind of object you even have.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the bra's conjugation when computing a matrix element.**
$\langle i|A|j\rangle$ conjugates the entries of $\langle i|$, not $A$
itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $|v\rangle$ is a ket (column vector); $\langle v|=|v\rangle^\dagger$ is
  its bra (conjugate-transpose row vector).
- $\langle u|v\rangle$ is a number (the inner product); $|u\rangle\langle
  v|$ is an operator (the outer product).
- Completeness relation: $\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|=I$ for any
  orthonormal basis, derived by checking it acts as the identity on an
  arbitrary vector.
- $\langle i|A|j\rangle$ is $A$'s $(i,j)$ matrix element in the basis
  $\{|i\rangle\}$.
