## Nine lessons of mathematics, now aimed at something

Nine lessons of linear algebra have built unit vectors, inner products,
Hermitian and unitary operators, eigenvalues, and tensor products. None of
that, by itself, is physics. It's mathematics that happens to be useful
for physics. This lesson supplies the missing piece: the specific
postulates that connect the mathematics to actual, checkable predictions
about what an experiment will show.

## Where every earlier lesson was heading

Every earlier lesson in this course quietly built toward one destination:
a state is a unit vector; an <Term id="observable">observable</Term> is a Hermitian operator; a
measurement outcome is one of that operator's eigenvalues; and the
*probability* of getting a particular outcome comes from how much the
state "overlaps" with the corresponding eigenvector. Every piece of that
sentence is a lesson you've already done the work for.

## The four postulates, stated and connected

### Postulate 1: states are unit vectors

A quantum system's state is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ with
$\big\||\psi\rangle\big\|=1$, in a complex <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term> equipped with an
inner product (a **Hilbert space**, the name for the structure built
across this course).
Two states are the same if they differ only by an overall phase:
$|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ represent the identical
physical situation, for any real $\alpha$, a fact this lesson will
justify below rather than just assert.

### Postulate 2: observables are Hermitian operators

A measurable physical quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator $A$.
Its possible outcomes are its eigenvalues and nothing else, guaranteed
real by the Hermitian Operators lesson, which is the physical requirement
that measurement outcomes be real numbers.

### Postulate 3 (the Born rule): outcome probabilities

Write $A$'s spectral decomposition $A = \sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle
e_i|$ (Hermitian Operators lesson). If the system is in state
$|\psi\rangle$, the probability of measuring outcome $\lambda_i$ is:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "P", symbol: "P(λᵢ)", gloss: "The probability of measuring outcome λᵢ: a number between 0 and 1." },
    { id: "e", symbol: "⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩", gloss: "The overlap between the state ψ and the eigenvector eᵢ for outcome λᵢ, measuring how much of ψ 'points along' that outcome." },
    { id: "mod", symbol: "| · |²", gloss: "Squared modulus, the step that turns a complex amplitude into a real, non-negative probability." },
  ]}
>
$$
P(\lambda_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2
$$
</EquationReveal>

This is a genuine postulate: nothing in this course, or anywhere else in
physics, derives it from more basic principles. But it isn't an arbitrary
choice either, it's the *unique* rule consistent with everything already
built. Expand $|\psi\rangle=\sum_i c_i|e_i\rangle$
where $c_i=\langle e_i|\psi\rangle$ (the completeness relation, Bra-Ket
Formalism lesson). Total probability had better sum to $1$; check it does.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Write out the sum of Born-rule probabilities and unfold |cᵢ|² as ⟨ψ|eᵢ⟩⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩.">
$$
\sum_i P(\lambda_i) = \sum_i |c_i|^2 = \sum_i \langle\psi|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Factor ⟨ψ| and |ψ⟩ out of the sum, leaving the completeness relation standing alone in the middle.">
$$
\sum_i \langle\psi|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\Big(\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\Big)|\psi\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The completeness relation (Bra-Ket Formalism lesson) says this sum equals the identity operator I.">
$$
\langle\psi|\Big(\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\Big)|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Postulate 1 requires ‖ψ‖=1, i.e. ⟨ψ|ψ⟩=1, so the probabilities sum to 1 with nothing left over.">
$$
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Notice how much had to be in place for that to work: normalization from
Postulate 1, an orthonormal eigenbasis from the Hermitian Operators
lesson, and the completeness relation from Bra-Ket Formalism. Had any one
of them failed, the Born rule would assign probabilities that do not sum
to $1$, and the postulate would be dead on arrival.

### Expectation value, and why it matches $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$

The **expectation value**, the average outcome over many repeated
measurements, is $\langle A\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i P(\lambda_i)$ by
definition of "average." Substitute the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> and simplify using the
spectral decomposition:

$$
\langle A\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = \sum_i\lambda_i\langle\psi|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\Big(\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\Big)|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle
$$

That last expression is the "sandwich" formula stated without proof back
in the Hermitian Operators lesson, now standing as a consequence of the
Born rule rather than as a definition of its own.

### Why global phase is unobservable

Replace $|\psi\rangle$ with $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ and recompute every
outcome probability: $\langle e_i|e^{i\alpha}\psi\rangle =
e^{i\alpha}\langle e_i|\psi\rangle$, so
$P(\lambda_i) = |e^{i\alpha}\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 =
|e^{i\alpha}|^2\,|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle
e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, completely unchanged, because $|e^{i\alpha}|=1$
(Complex Numbers lesson). Every prediction this framework makes depends
only on $|\psi\rangle$ up to an overall phase, which is Postulate 1's
claim, now justified rather than stated.

### Measurement and collapse

After a measurement yields outcome $\lambda_i$, the state becomes
$|e_i\rangle$, the corresponding eigenvector. A repeated, immediate
re-measurement of the same observable then gives $\lambda_i$ again with
certainty ($P(\lambda_i)=|\langle e_i|e_i\rangle|^2=1$), matching the
operational meaning of "the system now definitely has that value."

One assumption is buried in that sentence, and it is worth naming now so
it does not have to be unlearned later: it presumes each eigenvalue
$\lambda_i$ has exactly one eigenvector, so that naming the outcome names
the post-measurement state uniquely. When two or more independent
eigenvectors share an eigenvalue (a **degenerate** eigenvalue), the
outcome no longer picks out a single vector, and the collapse rule has to
be restated in terms of projectors onto whole eigenspaces. Everything in
this lesson is the non-degenerate case; the Operators, Observables &
Measurement course does the general one.

### Composite systems

For two systems combined via the <Term id="tensor-product">tensor product</Term> (last lesson), a joint
state lives in $H_1\otimes H_2$; product states describe independent
systems, and non-factorizable (entangled) states describe systems whose
measurement outcomes are correlated in a way no product state can
reproduce.

## Each postulate traced back to its lesson

Collect the four postulates together, each now tied to a specific earlier
lesson: **state** equals unit vector (Vector Spaces, Inner Products);
**observable** equals Hermitian operator (Hermitian Operators);
**outcome** equals eigenvalue (Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors);
**probability** equals Born rule via the spectral decomposition (this
lesson, built on the Bra-Ket Formalism's completeness relation). Unitary
operators (not directly a postulate here, but essential) describe how a
state evolves *between* measurements. This short list is the mathematical
skeleton of every calculation the rest of quantum mechanics will ask you
to do.

## Probabilities and an expectation from two overlaps

**A Hermitian observable has eigenvalues $+1,-1$ with eigenvectors
$|e_+\rangle,|e_-\rangle$. A system is in state $|\psi\rangle$ with
$\langle e_+|\psi\rangle=0.6$ and $\langle e_-|\psi\rangle=0.8$ (both
real). Find $P(+1)$, $P(-1)$, and $\langle A\rangle$, and check the two
ways of computing $\langle A\rangle$ agree.**

$$
P(+1) = |0.6|^2 = 0.36, \qquad P(-1)=|0.8|^2=0.64
$$

$$
\langle A\rangle = (+1)(0.36) + (-1)(0.64) = -0.28
$$

Via the sandwich formula, with $A=\lambda_+|e_+\rangle\langle e_+| +
\lambda_-|e_-\rangle\langle e_-|$ and $|\psi\rangle = 0.6|e_+\rangle +
0.8|e_-\rangle$ (real coefficients, so the expansion coefficients are the
overlaps themselves: $c_+=\langle e_+|\psi\rangle=0.6$, $c_-=0.8$):

$$
\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle = (0.6)^2(+1) + (0.8)^2(-1) = 0.36-0.64=-0.28
$$

Both routes agree, as the general derivation above guarantees they must.

## Amplitude against squared modulus, one last time

<Callout type="warning">
**Using the amplitude directly instead of its squared modulus.**
$P(\lambda_i)=|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, not $\langle
e_i|\psi\rangle$ itself. This has been the single most common error
throughout the course, and this is the lesson where it starts producing
wrong numbers rather than just wrong notation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing <Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term> is physically meaningful.** $|\psi\rangle$ and
$e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ predict identically for *every* observable, as
derived above; only the *relative* phase between terms in a
superposition, not an overall phase on the whole state, is observable.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the worked example's setup, verify $P(+1)+P(-1)=1$ directly
   (already shown above, restate the calculation in your own words).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The worked example has overlaps $\langle e_+|\psi\rangle = 0.6$ and
   $\langle e_-|\psi\rangle = 0.8$, so the Born rule gives

   $$
   P(+1) = |0.6|^2 = 0.36, \qquad P(-1) = |0.8|^2 = 0.64, \qquad 0.36+0.64 = 1 \checkmark
   $$

   Why it had to come out that way, rather than by arithmetic luck: the
   eigenvectors form an orthonormal basis, so expanding
   $|\psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i|e_i\rangle$ and computing
   $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ kills every cross term and leaves
   $\sum_i |c_i|^2$. Since $|\psi\rangle$ is normalized, that sum is 1, and
   the $|c_i|^2$ are exactly the Born probabilities. Normalization of the state
   *is* normalization of the probability distribution; they are one equation
   read two ways.

   Two structural points that the arithmetic hides. The check works basis by
   basis, so a *different* observable on the same state gives a different set of
   probabilities that also sums to 1, using the same normalization once again.
   And $0.6+0.8 = 1.4$, not 1, which is the reminder that it is the squares and
   never the amplitudes that are required to sum to one.

   </details>

2. A state has $\langle e_1|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}$ and two other
   eigenvectors with equal overlap. If probabilities must sum to $1$ and
   are all equal, what is $|\langle e_2|\psi\rangle|^2$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   |\langle e_2|\psi\rangle|^2 = \frac13.
   $$

   Two routes reach it, and they agree, which is the check.

   From the given overlap: $|\langle e_1|\psi\rangle|^2 = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 = \tfrac13$.
   Since all three probabilities are equal, every one of them is $\tfrac13$.

   From the sum rule: three equal probabilities summing to 1 must each be
   $\tfrac13$, without needing the first overlap at all.

   The question is deliberately over-determined, and noticing that is part of
   the answer. Being told the first overlap *and* that all three are equal *and*
   that they sum to 1 is one fact more than the problem needs, and the redundancy
   is what lets you verify rather than merely compute. If the stated overlap had
   been, say, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, the three conditions would have been mutually
   inconsistent and the state described would not exist.

   The error to avoid is answering $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$, which is the
   *amplitude*, not the probability. The question asks for
   $|\langle e_2|\psi\rangle|^2$, and the square is already written into the
   symbol.

   </details>

3. Explain, using the derivation above, why multiplying $|\psi\rangle$ by
   $-1$ (a special case of a global phase, $\alpha=\pi$) changes no
   prediction at all, for probabilities and for expectation values alike.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because every prediction is quadratic in the state, so the phase appears once
   with a conjugate and once without, and the two cancel.

   For probabilities, with $|\psi'\rangle = -|\psi\rangle$:

   $$
   P'(\lambda_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi'\rangle|^2 = |-1|^2\,|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = P(\lambda_i).
   $$

   Every outcome probability, in every basis, is untouched. Since the
   probabilities are all the observable content of the state, nothing further
   could survive.

   For expectation values the cancellation is even more explicit, because the
   state appears on both sides of the operator:

   $$
   \langle\psi'|A|\psi'\rangle = (-1)^*(-1)\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle.
   $$

   The general case runs identically with $e^{i\alpha}$ in place of $-1$:
   $|e^{i\alpha}|^2 = 1$ for the probabilities, and
   $(e^{i\alpha})^*(e^{i\alpha}) = 1$ for the expectation values. The specific
   value $\alpha = \pi$ has no special status.

   The essential qualifier, and the one that stops this from being a licence to
   discard every phase: the factor must multiply the *entire* state. A phase on
   one term of a superposition, $\alpha|e_1\rangle - \beta|e_2\rangle$ against
   $\alpha|e_1\rangle + \beta|e_2\rangle$, does not factor out and does not
   cancel. It changes the probabilities in any basis that is not $\{|e_i\rangle\}$,
   and interference is exactly that difference. Global phase is unphysical;
   relative phase is the physics.

   </details>

4. For the observable $A=$ Pauli-$Z$ and state $|\psi\rangle=|+\rangle$
   (eigenvector of Pauli-$X$, not $Z$), use $Z$'s eigenvectors
   $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ to find $P(+1)$ and $P(-1)$ for a $Z$
   measurement, and $\langle Z\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $Z$'s eigenvectors are $|0\rangle$ with eigenvalue $+1$ and $|1\rangle$ with
   eigenvalue $-1$. Overlap $|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$
   with each:

   $$
   \langle0|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \langle1|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},
   $$

   so

   $$
   P(+1) = P(-1) = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12, \qquad \langle Z\rangle = (+1)\tfrac12 + (-1)\tfrac12 = 0.
   $$

   Confirming by the sandwich:
   $Z|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle) = |-\rangle$, and
   $\langle+|-\rangle = 0$, so $\langle+|Z|+\rangle = 0$ $\checkmark$. Two
   different calculations, one answer, which is the spectral decomposition and
   the matrix sandwich agreeing as they must.

   The parenthetical in the question is the trap being set. $|+\rangle$ *is* an
   eigenvector, of $X$, with eigenvalue $+1$, and it is tempting to carry that
   over and answer $\langle Z\rangle = 1$ with certainty. Being an eigenstate is
   a relationship between a state and one particular observable, not a property
   of the state alone. With respect to $Z$, $|+\rangle$ is maximally
   non-committal.

   $\langle Z\rangle = 0$ also illustrates the point from the Hermitian
   Operators lesson: 0 is not one of $Z$'s eigenvalues, and no individual
   measurement ever returns it. It is the average of a stream of $+1$s and
   $-1$s arriving in equal proportion.

   </details>

## Summary

- State equals unit vector; observable equals Hermitian operator; outcome
  equals eigenvalue; probability equals Born rule, $P(\lambda_i)=|\langle
  e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, a postulate, verified consistent with
  normalization via the completeness relation.
- $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ is derived from the Born
  rule and the spectral decomposition, not assumed independently.
- Global phase is provably unobservable: every probability depends only
  on $|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, which an overall phase factor never
  changes.
- Composite systems combine via the tensor product; entangled states
  describe correlations no product state can reproduce.
