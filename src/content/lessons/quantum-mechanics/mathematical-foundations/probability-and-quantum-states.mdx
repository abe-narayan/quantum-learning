## Motivation

Nine lessons of linear algebra have built unit vectors, inner products,
Hermitian and unitary operators, eigenvalues, and tensor products. None of
that, by itself, is physics. It's mathematics that happens to be useful
for physics. This lesson supplies the missing piece: the specific
postulates that connect the mathematics to actual, checkable predictions
about what an experiment will show.

## Intuition

Every earlier lesson in this course quietly built toward one destination:
a state is a unit vector; an observable is a Hermitian operator; a
measurement outcome is one of that operator's eigenvalues; and the
*probability* of getting a particular outcome comes from how much the
state "overlaps" with the corresponding eigenvector. Every piece of that
sentence is a lesson you've already done the work for.

## Mathematical Development

### Postulate 1: states are unit vectors

A quantum system's state is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ with $\|\psi\rangle\|=1$,
in a complex vector space equipped with an inner product (a **Hilbert
space**, the name for exactly the structure built across this course).
Two states are the same if they differ only by an overall phase:
$|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ represent the identical
physical situation, for any real $\alpha$, a fact this lesson will
justify below rather than just assert.

### Postulate 2: observables are Hermitian operators

A measurable physical quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator $A$.
Its possible outcomes are exactly its eigenvalues, guaranteed real by the
Hermitian Operators lesson, exactly matching the physical requirement that
measurement outcomes are real numbers.

### Postulate 3 (the Born rule): outcome probabilities

Write $A$'s spectral decomposition $A = \sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle
e_i|$ (Hermitian Operators lesson). If the system is in state
$|\psi\rangle$, the probability of measuring outcome $\lambda_i$ is:

$$
P(\lambda_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2
$$

This is a genuine postulate of quantum mechanics, not something derived
from more basic principles in this course, or anywhere else in physics,
but it isn't arbitrary either: it's the *unique* rule consistent with
everything already built. Expand $|\psi\rangle=\sum_i c_i|e_i\rangle$
where $c_i=\langle e_i|\psi\rangle$ (the completeness relation, Bra-Ket
Formalism lesson). Total probability had better sum to $1$; check it does:

$$
\sum_i P(\lambda_i) = \sum_i |c_i|^2 = \sum_i \langle\psi|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\Big(\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\Big)|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1
$$

using the completeness relation again in the second-to-last step, and
$\|\psi\|=1$ (Postulate 1) in the last. Normalization, orthonormal
eigenbases, and the completeness relation all conspire to make this
come out exactly right: a real consistency check, not a coincidence.

### Expectation value, and why it matches $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$

The **expectation value**, the average outcome over many repeated
measurements, is $\langle A\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i P(\lambda_i)$ by
definition of "average." Substitute the Born rule and simplify using the
spectral decomposition:

$$
\langle A\rangle = \sum_i \lambda_i|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = \sum_i\lambda_i\langle\psi|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\Big(\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\Big)|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle
$$

This recovers exactly the "sandwich" formula stated without proof back in
the Hermitian Operators lesson, now derived as a consequence of the Born
rule, closing the loop between that lesson and this one.

### Why global phase is unobservable

Replace $|\psi\rangle$ with $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ and recompute every
outcome probability: $\langle e_i|e^{i\alpha}\psi\rangle =
e^{i\alpha}\langle e_i|\psi\rangle$, so
$P(\lambda_i) = |e^{i\alpha}\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 =
|e^{i\alpha}|^2\,|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle
e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, completely unchanged, because $|e^{i\alpha}|=1$
(Complex Numbers lesson). Every prediction this framework makes depends
only on $|\psi\rangle$ up to an overall phase, which is exactly Postulate
1's claim, now justified rather than just stated.

### Measurement and collapse

After a measurement yields outcome $\lambda_i$, the state becomes
$|e_i\rangle$, the corresponding eigenvector. A repeated, immediate
re-measurement of the same observable then gives $\lambda_i$ again with
certainty ($P(\lambda_i)=|\langle e_i|e_i\rangle|^2=1$), matching the
operational meaning of "the system now definitely has that value."

### Composite systems

For two systems combined via the tensor product (last lesson), a joint
state lives in $H_1\otimes H_2$; product states describe independent
systems, and non-factorizable (entangled) states describe systems whose
measurement outcomes are correlated in a way no product state can
reproduce.

## Physical Interpretation

Collect the four postulates together, each now tied to a specific earlier
lesson: **state** equals unit vector (Vector Spaces, Inner Products);
**observable** equals Hermitian operator (Hermitian Operators);
**outcome** equals eigenvalue (Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors);
**probability** equals Born rule via the spectral decomposition (this
lesson, built on the Bra-Ket Formalism's completeness relation). Unitary
operators (not directly a postulate here, but essential) describe how a
state evolves *between* measurements. This short list is the mathematical
skeleton of every calculation the rest of quantum mechanics will ask you
to do.

## Worked Example

**A Hermitian observable has eigenvalues $+1,-1$ with eigenvectors
$|e_+\rangle,|e_-\rangle$. A system is in state $|\psi\rangle$ with
$\langle e_+|\psi\rangle=0.6$ and $\langle e_-|\psi\rangle=0.8$ (both
real). Find $P(+1)$, $P(-1)$, and $\langle A\rangle$, and check the two
ways of computing $\langle A\rangle$ agree.**

$$
P(+1) = |0.6|^2 = 0.36, \qquad P(-1)=|0.8|^2=0.64
$$

(Check: $0.36+0.64=1$ ✓, consistent with normalization as required.) The
Born rule turns an overlap into a bar you can see, not just a number:

<BarChart
  bars={[
    { label: "P(+1)", value: workedExamplePPlus },
    { label: "P(-1)", value: workedExamplePMinus },
  ]}
  ariaLabel="Bar chart of P(+1) and P(-1) for the worked example"
/>

Two bars, summing to exactly 1, visibly, not just algebraically.

$$
\langle A\rangle = (+1)(0.36) + (-1)(0.64) = -0.28
$$

Via the sandwich formula, with $A=\lambda_+|e_+\rangle\langle e_+| +
\lambda_-|e_-\rangle\langle e_-|$ and $|\psi\rangle = 0.6|e_+\rangle +
0.8|e_-\rangle$ (real coefficients, so this expansion is literally
$c_+=\langle e_+|\psi\rangle=0.6$, $c_-=0.8$):

$$
\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle = (0.6)^2(+1) + (0.8)^2(-1) = 0.36-0.64=-0.28
$$

Both routes agree, as the general derivation above guarantees they must.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Using the amplitude directly instead of its squared modulus.**
$P(\lambda_i)=|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, not $\langle
e_i|\psi\rangle$ itself, the single most common error in this entire
course, worth flagging one last time at the point where it matters most.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing global phase is physically meaningful.** $|\psi\rangle$ and
$e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ predict identically for *every* observable, as
derived above; only the *relative* phase between terms in a
superposition, not an overall phase on the whole state, is observable.
</Callout>

## Summary

- State equals unit vector; observable equals Hermitian operator; outcome
  equals eigenvalue; probability equals Born rule, $P(\lambda_i)=|\langle
  e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, a postulate, verified consistent with
  normalization via the completeness relation.
- $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ is derived from the Born
  rule and the spectral decomposition, not assumed independently.
- Global phase is provably unobservable: every probability depends only
  on $|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, which an overall phase factor never
  changes.
- Composite systems combine via the tensor product; entangled states
  describe correlations no product state can reproduce.
