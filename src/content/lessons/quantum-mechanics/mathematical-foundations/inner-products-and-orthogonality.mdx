## Motivation

A <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term>, as built in the last lesson, has no notion of length or
angle: you can add and scale vectors, but nothing yet says how "big" one
is or whether two of them are perpendicular. Physics needs both. The
length of a <Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term> vector will turn out to be tied to total
probability, and "perpendicular" states will turn out to be the ones a
single measurement can perfectly tell apart. The extra structure that
supplies length and angle is called an **<Term id="inner-product">inner product</Term>**.

## Intuition

For ordinary real vectors, the dot product $u\cdot v = \sum u_iv_i$ already
does this job: $u\cdot u$ gives squared length, and $u\cdot v=0$ means
perpendicular. The complex case needs one modification, conjugation, and
the reason for it is concrete: without conjugation, squared lengths can
come out negative. Everything else follows from repairing that.

## Mathematical Development

### Why conjugation is required

Try defining an inner product on $\mathbb{C}^n$ the naive way, without
conjugating: $\langle v,v\rangle \overset{?}{=} \sum v_i^2$. Take
$v=(i,0)$: $\sum v_i^2 = i^2 = -1$, negative. A notion of "squared
length" that can be negative is useless. Conjugating one side fixes this
immediately, because $v_i^*v_i=|v_i|^2\ge0$ always (the $zz^*\ge0$ fact
from the Complex Numbers lesson, applied one coordinate at a time).

### The axioms

An **inner product** on a complex vector space $V$ is a function
$\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:V\times V\to\mathbb{C}$ satisfying, for all
$u,v,w\in V$ and $a\in\mathbb{C}$:

1. **Conjugate symmetry**: $\langle u,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle^*$.
2. **Linearity in the second argument**:
   $\langle u,av+bw\rangle = a\langle u,v\rangle+b\langle u,w\rangle$.
3. **Positive-definiteness**: $\langle v,v\rangle\ge0$, with equality iff
   $v=0$.

(This lesson uses the physics convention: linear in the *second* slot.
Some math texts put linearity in the first slot instead; both are used in
the literature, so always check a text's convention before combining
formulas from different sources.) Conjugate-linearity in the *first*
argument follows from axioms 1 and 2, rather than needing to be assumed
separately:

$$
\langle au+bw, v\rangle = \langle v, au+bw\rangle^* = (a\langle v,u\rangle+b\langle v,w\rangle)^* = a^*\langle v,u\rangle^*+b^*\langle v,w\rangle^* = a^*\langle u,v\rangle+b^*\langle w,v\rangle
$$

The **standard inner product** on $\mathbb{C}^n$ is
$\langle u,v\rangle = \sum_i u_i^*v_i$; you can check directly that it
satisfies all three axioms.

### Norm and normalization

The **norm** is $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$, real and
non-negative by positive-definiteness. A vector is **<Term id="normalization">normalized</Term>** if
$\|v\|=1$; any nonzero $v$ can be normalized by scaling:
$v/\|v\|$ always has norm exactly $1$ (check:
$\|v/\|v\|\|^2 = \langle v/\|v\|, v/\|v\|\rangle = \langle v,v\rangle/\|v\|^2 = 1$).

### Orthogonality

Two vectors are **orthogonal** if $\langle u,v\rangle=0$. A set of
pairwise-orthogonal, individually-normalized vectors is an
**orthonormal set**, the natural generalization of "perpendicular unit
vectors." An orthonormal *basis* is the best-behaved kind of basis to
work with, for a reason the Bra-Ket Formalism lesson makes precise
(coordinates relative to an orthonormal basis are just inner products).

### The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

For any $u,v\in V$:

$$
|\langle u,v\rangle| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|
$$

/>

Here's the derivation, worked from scratch.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Assume v ≠ 0 (the inequality is trivial if v = 0), and define c as the coefficient that makes w = u − cv orthogonal to v.">
$$
c = \frac{\langle v,u\rangle}{\langle v,v\rangle}, \qquad w = u - cv
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Check the claim directly: substituting c's definition makes the two terms cancel.">
$$
\langle v,w\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle - c\langle v,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle - \langle v,u\rangle = 0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Positive-definiteness guarantees ⟨w,w⟩ ≥ 0 for any vector, including this particular w.">
$$
0 \le \langle w,w\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Expand using ⟨v,u⟩=⟨u,v⟩* and that ⟨v,v⟩ is real. All three of the last terms reduce to the same quantity: two subtract, one adds, leaving a single copy.">
$$
0 \le \langle w,w\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle - c\langle u,v\rangle - c^*\langle v,u\rangle + c^*c\langle v,v\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle - \frac{|\langle v,u\rangle|^2}{\langle v,v\rangle}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Rearrange: move the fraction to the other side.">
$$
|\langle v,u\rangle|^2 \le \langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle = \|u\|^2\|v\|^2
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Take square roots. |⟨v,u⟩| = |⟨u,v⟩| since conjugating doesn't change modulus, and what remains is Cauchy-Schwarz.">
$$
|\langle u,v\rangle| \le \|u\|\,\|v\|
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Geometric interpretation and Gram-Schmidt

For real vectors, $u\cdot v = \|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta$, so <Term id="cauchy-schwarz-inequality">Cauchy-Schwarz</Term> is
just $|\cos\theta|\le1$. Complex vectors don't have a literal angle in the
same sense, but the ratio $|\langle u,v\rangle|/(\|u\|\|v\|)\in[0,1]$
still measures how much $u$ and $v$ "overlap," the same word used for
$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ once bra-ket notation is introduced. The vector
$w=u-cv$ built during the Cauchy-Schwarz proof is the first step of
**Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization**: subtracting off the component along
$v$ leaves a piece orthogonal to it, which is how an arbitrary basis gets
converted into an orthonormal one, one vector at a time.

For real vectors $u=(3,1)$ and $v=(1,2)$, the proof's $c=\langle
v,u\rangle/\langle v,v\rangle$ comes out to exactly $1$, so $cv=(1,2)$ and
$w=u-cv=(2,-1)$. Drawing $w$ starting at $cv$'s tip and ending at $u$'s
tip shows the whole construction at once: $cv$ is $u$'s shadow along $v$,
and $w$ is what's left over, sitting at a right angle to $v$.

## Physical Interpretation

Two quantum states with $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=0$ are the states, and
the only states, that a single measurement can tell apart with certainty.
A geometric condition on vectors doubles as a statement about what is
experimentally distinguishable. $\|\psi\|=1$ is the normalization
condition that makes total measurement probability equal $1$, developed
fully in the Probability and Quantum States lesson.

The non-orthogonal case is where the inner product earns its keep, because
the overlap is not merely a yes-or-no verdict: its size sets a quantitative
limit. Take $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle = (|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$,
with overlap $\langle 0|+\rangle = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$. Suppose someone
hands you one of the two at random and you measure in the
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ basis. Outcome $1$ can only have come from
$|+\rangle$, so it settles the question. Outcome $0$ is inconclusive, and
the best response to it is to answer $|0\rangle$ every time rather than
flip a coin, since $|0\rangle$ is the likelier preparation to have
produced it. That policy is right whenever the state really was
$|0\rangle$, and right on half of the $|+\rangle$ preparations (the half
that happened to give outcome $1$), so your success rate is
$\tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(\tfrac12) = 0.75$. A better-chosen measurement
does better still, and the ceiling for any measurement at all is

$$
P_{\text{success}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2}\right) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac12}\right) \approx 0.854
$$

Orthogonal states put $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle| = 0$ into that formula and
recover $P_{\text{success}} = 1$; identical states put in $1$ and give
$0.5$, which is a coin flip. The single number $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|$
interpolates between the two.

## Worked Example

**Are $u=(1,i)$ and $v=(i,1)$ orthogonal? If not, find the angle-like
overlap ratio.**

$$
\langle u,v\rangle = 1^*\cdot i + i^*\cdot 1 = i + (-i)(1) = i - i = 0
$$

They're orthogonal. (Quick sanity check on why this is unsurprising: $v$
is $i$ times the vector $(1,-i)$, and $(1,-i)$ is orthogonal to $(1,i)$
under this inner product. An overall phase factor
like this $i$ never changes orthogonality, since $|\langle u, cv\rangle| =
|c|\,|\langle u,v\rangle|$ for any scalar $c$.)

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to conjugate.** $\langle u,v\rangle=\sum u_i^*v_i$, not $\sum
u_iv_i$. Skipping the conjugate breaks positive-definiteness, as shown
above.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming $\langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle$.** They're complex
conjugates of each other, equal only when both happen to be real.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Different textbooks conjugate different slots.** This lesson conjugates
the first argument (physics convention, matching bra-ket notation). Watch
for the opposite convention when reading other sources.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An inner product is conjugate-symmetric, linear in the second argument,
  and positive-definite; conjugation is what makes $\langle v,v\rangle$
  real and non-negative for complex vectors.
- Norm: $\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}$; normalization scales any
  nonzero vector to norm $1$.
- Orthogonality: $\langle u,v\rangle=0$; an orthonormal set has no
  redundancy and no "overlap" between distinct members.
- Cauchy-Schwarz, $|\langle u,v\rangle|\le\|u\|\|v\|$, follows by
  projecting out the component along one vector and using
  positive-definiteness on what's left.
