## Narrowing an enormous space of functions

Rotate a system, evolve it forward in time, apply a quantum gate: every
one of these turns one <Term id="quantum-state">state vector</Term> into another, so physics needs a
mathematical object that maps vectors to vectors. An enormous space of
possible functions could do that job, but physics restricts itself to a
narrow slice of them, the **linear** ones, and the restriction is not a
convenience. Linearity is what lets a superposition be built from simpler
pieces and then have an operator act on the whole thing by acting on each
piece separately. Lose linearity and superposition stops meaning anything.

## The structure an operator has to respect

A linear operator is a rule for turning vectors into vectors that respects
the vector-space structure: scale the input, and the output scales the
same way; add two inputs, and the output adds the same way. That's a
strong restriction (most functions you could dream up aren't linear), and
it buys something specific: a linear operator is fully determined by what
it does to a <Term id="basis">basis</Term>, rather than needing to be specified vector by
vector.

## Matrix multiplication, derived from completeness

### Definition

A **linear operator** $A:V\to V$ satisfies, for all $u,v\in V$ and
$a,b\in\mathbb{C}$:

$$
A(au+bv) = aA(u) + bA(v)
$$

/>

$v=(1,1)$ and $2v=(2,2)$ sit on the same line through the origin, one
twice as far out as the other. That is what "scale the input by $2$" looks
like geometrically. Feeding both through
$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ (this lesson's own worked-example
matrix, below) lands them at $Av=(2,3)$ and $A(2v)=(4,6)$: still one
shared line through the origin, still a factor of $2$ apart. That
proportionality surviving the map is what $A(2v)=2A(v)$ means as a
picture rather than as symbols.

**Contrast.** A non-linear map need not preserve this. Take
$h(x,y)=(x^2,y)$: it sends $v=(1,1)\mapsto h(v)=(1,1)$ but
$2v=(2,2)\mapsto h(2v)=(4,2)$, while linearity would require
$h(2v)=2h(v)=(2,2)$. Not only is $(4,2)\neq(2,2)$; the two images no
longer sit on a common line through the origin. Squaring a coordinate
destroys the proportionality the diagram above shows $A$ preserving.

### Matrix representation

Fix an <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term> $\{|e_i\rangle\}$. The **matrix elements**
$A_{ij}=\langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle$ from the last lesson determine $A$
completely: any $|v\rangle=\sum_j c_j|e_j\rangle$ maps to

$$
A|v\rangle = \sum_j c_j A|e_j\rangle = \sum_j c_j \sum_i A_{ij}|e_i\rangle
$$

(expanding $A|e_j\rangle$ itself in the basis, where $A_{ij}=\langle
e_i|A|e_j\rangle$ is its $i$-th coordinate). So once every
$A_{ij}$ is known, $A$'s action on *every* vector is known: an operator
on an $n$-dimensional space is completely specified by an $n\times n$
grid of numbers.

### Composition, derived from completeness

Applying two operators in sequence, $A$ then $B$, written $(AB)|v\rangle
:= A(B|v\rangle)$, is itself linear, hence itself has matrix elements
$(AB)_{ik}=\langle e_i|AB|e_k\rangle$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Insert the completeness relation Σⱼ|eⱼ⟩⟨eⱼ|=I (from the last lesson) directly between A and B. This is legal because inserting the identity operator never changes anything.">
$$
\langle e_i|AB|e_k\rangle = \langle e_i|A\Big(\sum_j|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|\Big)B|e_k\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Regroup: each term in the sum splits cleanly into a matrix element of A and a matrix element of B.">
$$
= \sum_j \langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|B|e_k\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Both factors are matrix elements by definition, and what remains is the ordinary matrix-multiplication formula.">
$$
= \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

That final expression came from nothing but completeness: "compose two
operators" and "multiply their matrices" collapse into the same
operation because inserting the identity was legal, not because matrix
multiplication was defined to work out that way from the start.

### Identity, inverse

The **identity operator** $I$ satisfies $I|v\rangle=|v\rangle$ for every
$v$; its matrix (in any orthonormal basis) is the identity matrix. An
operator $A$ has an **inverse** $A^{-1}$ if $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$. Not every
operator has one: $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ sends the
nonzero vector $(1,-1)$ to $(0,0)$, and any operator that sends a nonzero
vector to zero can't be undone (there's no way to recover which input
produced that zero output), so it has no inverse.

## Gates, observables, and evolution, all one type

Every gate in a quantum circuit, every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> in the sense of "the
mathematical object representing a measurable quantity," and the operator
governing how a state evolves in time are all linear operators. The next
three lessons single out two special families, Hermitian and unitary,
that carry specific physical meaning; between them they account for every
observable and every gate. They do not exhaust the linear operators
physics uses, though: the raising and lowering operators the harmonic
oscillator is solved with are linear and indispensable, and are neither
Hermitian nor unitary.

## Composing two operators, two ways

**Verify the composition formula by computing $AB$ two ways, for
$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and
$B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.**

Direct matrix multiplication:

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\cdot0+0\cdot1 & 2\cdot1+0\cdot0\\0\cdot0+3\cdot1&0\cdot1+3\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Via the derived formula, the $(1,2)$ entry (using 1-indexing) is
$\sum_j A_{1j}B_{j2} = A_{11}B_{12}+A_{12}B_{22} = 2\cdot1+0\cdot0=2$,
matching the direct computation.

## Order matters, and it matters physically

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $AB=BA$.** Operator composition generally does not commute:
$AB$ and $BA$ can differ, sometimes drastically. (Whether two particular
operators commute turns out to be physically important. Commuting
observables can be measured simultaneously, a topic the Operators,
Observables & Measurement course develops fully.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every operator is invertible.** A matrix fails to be invertible
exactly when it sends some nonzero vector to zero.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is the map $f(v) = v + (1,0)$ on $\mathbb{C}^2$ linear? Justify your
   answer using the definition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. Linearity requires $f(av + bw) = af(v) + bf(w)$, and this map fails it.

   The fastest disproof is at the origin. Every linear map sends $0$ to $0$,
   because $f(0) = f(0\cdot 0) = 0\cdot f(0) = 0$. Here $f(0) = (1,0) \ne 0$,
   so $f$ cannot be linear, and no further checking is needed.

   Spelled out on the additivity axiom, to see where the offset accumulates:

   $$
   f(v+w) = v + w + (1,0), \qquad f(v)+f(w) = v + w + (2,0).
   $$

   These differ by $(1,0)$. The constant is added once on the left and twice on
   the right, and no choice of $v$ and $w$ repairs it.

   The map is not badly behaved, it is just in a different category. A map of
   the form "linear part plus a constant" is called **affine**, and translations
   are the standard example. Affine maps are perfectly good geometry, but they
   are not operators in this course's sense and they do not have matrices in the
   $2\times2$ sense: you cannot write $f(v) = Mv$ for any $M$, because
   $Mv$ always sends $0$ to $0$.

   That matters physically. Time evolution, measurement projection and every
   gate in the quantum-computing courses are linear, which is exactly what
   guarantees that knowing an operator's action on a basis determines its
   action on every superposition. An affine map would break that, and with it
   the whole matrix-element machinery this lesson builds.

   </details>

2. Compute $BA$ for the $A,B$ in the worked example above, and confirm it
   differs from $AB$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and
   $B = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$:

   $$
   BA = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&3\\2&0\end{pmatrix},
   $$

   against the worked example's

   $$
   AB = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   They differ: the 2 and the 3 have traded places, so $AB \ne BA$ and these two
   operators do not commute.

   The structural reason is visible without arithmetic. $B$ swaps the two
   coordinates and $A$ scales them by different factors, so "scale then swap"
   and "swap then scale" apply the scalings to different coordinates. Had $A$
   been $\mathrm{diag}(2,2)$, a single scalar in disguise, the two orders would
   have agreed; it is the *different* diagonal entries that make the order
   matter.

   Non-commutativity is not a nuisance in this subject, it is the content. The
   commutator $[A,B] = AB - BA$, here
   $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, is nonzero, and it is exactly such
   nonzero commutators that produce the uncertainty relations: two observables
   can have a shared set of eigenvectors, and so be simultaneously measurable,
   only when they commute.

   The reading error to avoid is treating $AB$ as "$A$ first". In
   $(AB)|v\rangle = A(B|v\rangle)$ the rightmost operator acts first, so $AB$
   means $B$ then $A$. Composition notation runs right to left, and this
   question is a duplicate of the worked example only if you read it the other
   way round.

   </details>

3. Is $\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}$ invertible? Justify your
   answer by finding a nonzero vector it sends to zero, or explaining why
   none exists.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Not invertible. The nonzero vector $(2,-1)^\mathsf{T}$ is sent to zero:

   $$
   \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2-2\\4-4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   That settles it by the argument this lesson gives: an operator sending a
   nonzero vector to zero also sends the zero vector to zero, so two different
   inputs share an output, and there is no way for an inverse to decide which
   one to return.

   Finding the vector is not guesswork. The columns are $(1,2)$ and $(2,4)$,
   and the second is twice the first, so the combination
   $2\times(\text{column 1}) - 1\times(\text{column 2})$ vanishes, and those
   coefficients *are* the vector. Whenever the columns are linearly dependent,
   the dependency relation hands you the kernel vector directly.

   The determinant agrees, $\det = (1)(4) - (2)(2) = 0$, and is quicker to
   compute. It is worth knowing both, because the determinant tells you *that*
   the operator is singular while the kernel vector tells you *what* it
   destroys. Geometrically, this matrix flattens all of $\mathbb{C}^2$ onto the
   single line spanned by $(1,2)$, and the information about where along the
   perpendicular direction a vector started is gone.

   That is also why no quantum gate can look like this. Gates are unitary,
   unitaries preserve norms, and a norm-preserving operator can never send a
   nonzero vector to zero.

   </details>

4. Using the derived composition formula, compute the $(2,1)$ entry of
   $AB$ from the worked example, and check it against direct
   multiplication.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $(AB)_{21} = 3$.

   The formula derived from completeness is $(AB)_{ik} = \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}$.
   With $i=2$, $k=1$ and $j$ running over 1 and 2:

   $$
   (AB)_{21} = A_{21}B_{11} + A_{22}B_{21} = (0)(0) + (3)(1) = 3.
   $$

   Direct multiplication gave $AB = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\end{pmatrix}$, whose
   $(2,1)$ entry is 3 $\checkmark$.

   What the exercise is really checking is that the sum over $j$ is not an
   arbitrary rule. It is the completeness relation
   $\sum_j |e_j\rangle\langle e_j| = I$ inserted between $A$ and $B$ inside
   $\langle e_2|AB|e_1\rangle$, which is legal because inserting the identity
   changes nothing. Row-times-column matrix multiplication is the shadow that
   insertion casts, rather than a definition anyone had to choose.

   Two index habits are worth fixing here. The free indices $i$ and $k$ are the
   ones naming the entry you want and they appear once each; the summed index
   $j$ appears twice and disappears from the answer. And the shared index sits
   *adjacent* in $A_{ij}B_{jk}$, second slot of the left factor and first slot
   of the right, which is why $AB$ needs $A$'s column count to match $B$'s row
   count. Writing $A_{ji}B_{jk}$ by mistake computes an entry of
   $A^\mathsf{T}B$, a different operator entirely.

   </details>

## Summary

- A linear operator satisfies $A(au+bv)=aA(u)+bA(v)$, and is fully
  determined by its matrix elements $A_{ij}=\langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle$.
- Matrix multiplication is *derived* from inserting the completeness
  relation between two composed operators, not assumed as a separate rule.
- Not every operator is invertible; $A^{-1}$ exists only when $A$ never
  sends a nonzero vector to zero.
