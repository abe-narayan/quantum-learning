## Motivation

Rotate a system, evolve it forward in time, apply a quantum gate: every
one of these turns one <Term id="quantum-state">state vector</Term> into another, so physics needs a
mathematical object that does exactly that. An enormous space of possible
functions could do the job, but physics restricts itself to a narrow
slice of them, the **linear** ones, and that restriction isn't a
convenience. Linearity is exactly what lets a superposition be built from
simpler pieces and have an operator act on the whole thing by acting on
each piece separately. Lose linearity and superposition stops meaning
anything.

## Intuition

A linear operator is a rule for turning vectors into vectors that respects
the vector-space structure: scale the input, and the output scales the
same way; add two inputs, and the output adds the same way. That's a
strong restriction (most functions you could dream up aren't linear), but
it's exactly the restriction that makes an operator fully determined
by what it does to a <Term id="basis">basis</Term>, rather than needing to be specified vector by
vector.

## Mathematical Development

### Definition

A **linear operator** $A:V\to V$ satisfies, for all $u,v\in V$ and
$a,b\in\mathbb{C}$:

$$
A(au+bv) = aA(u) + bA(v)
$$

/>

$v=(1,1)$ and $2v=(2,2)$ sit on the same line through the origin, one
exactly twice as far out as the other — that's what "scale the input by
$2$" looks like geometrically. Feeding both through
$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ (this lesson's own worked-example
matrix, below) lands them at $Av=(2,3)$ and $A(2v)=(4,6)$: still one
shared line through the origin, still exactly a factor of $2$ apart. That
proportionality surviving the map, exactly, is what $A(2v)=2A(v)$ means as
a picture rather than as symbols.

**Contrast.** A non-linear map need not preserve this. Take
$h(x,y)=(x^2,y)$: it sends $v=(1,1)\mapsto h(v)=(1,1)$ but
$2v=(2,2)\mapsto h(2v)=(4,2)$, while linearity would require
$h(2v)=2h(v)=(2,2)$. Since $(4,2)\neq(2,2)$ — and the two images aren't
even on the same line through the origin — squaring a coordinate breaks
exactly the proportionality the diagram above shows $A$ preserving.

### Matrix representation

Fix an <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term> $\{|e_i\rangle\}$. The **matrix elements**
$A_{ij}=\langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle$ from the last lesson determine $A$
completely: any $|v\rangle=\sum_j c_j|e_j\rangle$ maps to

$$
A|v\rangle = \sum_j c_j A|e_j\rangle = \sum_j c_j \sum_i A_{ij}|e_i\rangle
$$

(expanding $A|e_j\rangle$ itself in the basis, using $A_{ij}=\langle
e_i|A|e_j\rangle$ as exactly its $i$-th coordinate). So once every
$A_{ij}$ is known, $A$'s action on *every* vector is known: an operator
on an $n$-dimensional space is completely specified by an $n\times n$
grid of numbers.

### Composition, derived from completeness

Applying two operators in sequence, $A$ then $B$, written $(AB)|v\rangle
:= A(B|v\rangle)$, is itself linear, hence itself has matrix elements
$(AB)_{ik}=\langle e_i|AB|e_k\rangle$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Insert the completeness relation Σⱼ|eⱼ⟩⟨eⱼ|=I (from the last lesson) directly between A and B — legal because inserting the identity operator never changes anything.">
$$
\langle e_i|AB|e_k\rangle = \langle e_i|A\Big(\sum_j|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|\Big)B|e_k\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Regroup: each term in the sum splits cleanly into a matrix element of A and a matrix element of B.">
$$
= \sum_j \langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle\langle e_j|B|e_k\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Both factors are just matrix elements by definition — this is exactly the ordinary matrix-multiplication formula.">
$$
= \sum_j A_{ij}B_{jk}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

That final expression fell out of nowhere but completeness: "compose two
operators" and "multiply their matrices" collapse into the same
operation because inserting the identity was legal, not because matrix
multiplication was defined to work out that way from the start.

### Identity, inverse

The **identity operator** $I$ satisfies $I|v\rangle=|v\rangle$ for every
$v$; its matrix (in any orthonormal basis) is the identity matrix. An
operator $A$ has an **inverse** $A^{-1}$ if $AA^{-1}=A^{-1}A=I$. Not every
operator has one: $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ sends the
nonzero vector $(1,-1)$ to $(0,0)$, and any operator that sends a nonzero
vector to zero can't be undone (there's no way to recover which input
produced that zero output), so it has no inverse.

## Physical Interpretation

Every gate in a quantum circuit, every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> in the sense of "the
mathematical object representing a measurable quantity," and the operator
governing how a state evolves in time are all linear operators. The next
three lessons single out two special families, Hermitian and unitary,
that carry specific physical meaning; every quantum-mechanical operator
you'll ever meet belongs to at least one of them.

## Worked Example

**Verify the composition formula by computing $AB$ two ways, for
$A=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}$ and
$B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.**

Direct matrix multiplication:

$$
AB = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2\cdot0+0\cdot1 & 2\cdot1+0\cdot0\\0\cdot0+3\cdot1&0\cdot1+3\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\3&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Via the derived formula, the $(1,2)$ entry (using 1-indexing) is
$\sum_j A_{1j}B_{j2} = A_{11}B_{12}+A_{12}B_{22} = 2\cdot1+0\cdot0=2$,
matching the direct computation.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $AB=BA$.** Operator composition generally does not commute:
$AB$ and $BA$ can differ, sometimes drastically. (Whether two particular
operators commute turns out to be physically important. Commuting
observables can be measured simultaneously, a topic the Operators,
Observables & Measurement course develops fully.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every operator is invertible.** A matrix fails to be invertible
exactly when it sends some nonzero vector to zero.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A linear operator satisfies $A(au+bv)=aA(u)+bA(v)$, and is fully
  determined by its matrix elements $A_{ij}=\langle e_i|A|e_j\rangle$.
- Matrix multiplication is *derived* from inserting the completeness
  relation between two composed operators, not assumed as a separate rule.
- Not every operator is invertible; $A^{-1}$ exists only when $A$ never
  sends a nonzero vector to zero.
