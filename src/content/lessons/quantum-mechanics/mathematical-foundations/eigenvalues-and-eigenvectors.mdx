## The few vectors that keep their direction

Most vectors, hit with an operator, come out pointing in some new
direction. A special few come out pointing the *same* direction, only
stretched or shrunk. Those are an operator's **<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvectors</Term>**, and they
carry most of the physical content of the theory: for the operators
representing physical observables, the eigenvectors are the states with a
definite, predictable value of that <Term id="observable">observable</Term>, and nothing else is.

## Axes that only get stretched

Picture an operator as a transformation of the plane. Most directions get
tilted somewhere else, but there are usually a couple of special axes that
only get stretched along themselves. Those axes are the eigenvectors; the
stretch factor along each is its eigenvalue.

Using the matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ from this
lesson's own Worked Example below: the ordinary vector $e_1=(1,0)$ comes
out tilted, landing at $(2,1)$, pointing somewhere else entirely. But
$v=(1,1)$ comes out at $(3,3)$, exactly $3v$, still on the same line
through the origin, just three times as long.

## The characteristic equation, and diagonalization

### Definition

$|v\rangle\ne0$ is an **eigenvector** of $A$ with **eigenvalue** $\lambda$
if:

$$
A|v\rangle = \lambda|v\rangle
$$

The requirement $v\ne0$ matters: $A\cdot0=\lambda\cdot0$ is true for
*every* $\lambda$ and tells you nothing.

### The characteristic equation

Rewrite the eigenvalue equation as $(A-\lambda I)|v\rangle = 0$. A linear
system $Mv=0$ has a *nonzero* solution exactly when $M$ is not invertible
(an invertible $M$ has only the trivial solution $v=M^{-1}0=0$), and a
matrix fails to be invertible exactly when its determinant is zero. So:

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = 0
$$

This is the **characteristic equation**, a polynomial in $\lambda$ whose
roots are exactly $A$'s eigenvalues.

### The $2\times2$ case, worked in general

For $A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$:

$$
\det(A-\lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix}a-\lambda&b\\c&d-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = (a-\lambda)(d-\lambda)-bc = \lambda^2 - (a+d)\lambda + (ad-bc)
$$

Two useful facts fall out immediately: $a+d = \operatorname{tr}(A)$ (the
**trace**, sum of diagonal entries) and $ad-bc=\det(A)$. So for any
$2\times2$ matrix:

$$
\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A)\,\lambda + \det(A) = 0
$$

By Vieta's formulas for a quadratic, this immediately gives two useful
shortcuts without solving for the eigenvalues individually: **the sum of
the eigenvalues equals the trace, and their product equals the
determinant.**

### Worked: Pauli-$X$

For $X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$: $\operatorname{tr}(X)=0$,
$\det(X)=0\cdot0-1\cdot1=-1$, so $\lambda^2-1=0 \Rightarrow
\lambda=\pm1$.

**Eigenvector for $\lambda=1$:** solve $(X-I)v=0$:
$\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{pmatrix}=0
\Rightarrow v_1=v_2$. Normalizing, $v = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, this is
$|+\rangle$ from the Quantum Computing track, if you've seen it.

**Eigenvector for $\lambda=-1$:** solve $(X+I)v=0 \Rightarrow v_1=-v_2$,
giving $v=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1) = |-\rangle$.

You can check both directly: $X|+\rangle =
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(X|0\rangle+X|1\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|0\rangle)
= |+\rangle$ (eigenvalue $+1$, confirmed), and $X|-\rangle =
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle-|0\rangle) = -|-\rangle$ (eigenvalue $-1$,
confirmed).

### Diagonalization

If $A$ has a full <Term id="basis">basis</Term> of eigenvectors $\{|v_i\rangle\}$ with eigenvalues
$\{\lambda_i\}$, then in that basis $A$'s matrix is diagonal: the
off-diagonal entries $\langle v_i|A|v_j\rangle$ for $i\ne j$ vanish,
because $A|v_j\rangle=\lambda_j|v_j\rangle$ is proportional to
$|v_j\rangle$, which is orthogonal to $\langle v_i|$ when the eigenbasis
happens to be <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal</Term>. Not every operator has enough eigenvectors to
diagonalize this way; the Hermitian Operators lesson shows that an
important class always does.

Concretely, for this lesson's $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$,
build $P$ whose columns are the two normalized eigenvectors
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$. Then $P^\dagger AP$
is what $A$ looks like to an observer using the eigenbasis as coordinates:

The off-diagonal $1$s are gone, and the numbers left on the diagonal are
the eigenvalues $3$ and $1$ found in the Worked Example below. Nothing was
done to the operator: only the coordinate system changed. Applying a
diagonal matrix means multiplying each coordinate by its own number and
never mixing coordinates, which is why finding an eigenbasis is usually
the first move when an operator has to be applied repeatedly, exponentiated,
or evolved in time.

## The states where the outcome is certain

For an operator representing a physical observable, eigenvalues are the
only values that observable can ever be measured to have, and its
eigenvectors are the states in which the outcome is certain rather than
probabilistic. The full justification for this (and why eigenvalues of
*physical* observables always come out real) is the Hermitian Operators
lesson; this lesson builds the pure linear-algebra machinery first.

This isn't only textbook language. On an actual superconducting or
trapped-ion quantum computer, the kind IBM, Google, and IonQ operate, a
measurement is physically a readout process that projects the qubit onto
the eigenbasis of whatever observable is being measured, and the signal
that comes back, the bit reported as $0$ or $1$, is a label for which
eigenvalue was found. The eigenvalue equation on this page is the equation
real readout hardware is built to resolve.

## Both eigenvalues and both eigenvectors of a 2x2

**Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.**

$\operatorname{tr}(A)=4$, $\det(A)=4-1=3$, so
$\lambda^2-4\lambda+3=0 \Rightarrow (\lambda-1)(\lambda-3)=0 \Rightarrow
\lambda=1,3$.

For $\lambda=1$: $(A-I)v=0 \Rightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}v=0 \Rightarrow v_1=-v_2$, giving
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

For $\lambda=3$: $(A-3I)v=0 \Rightarrow
\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}v=0 \Rightarrow v_1=v_2$, giving
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$.

Check: eigenvalues sum to $1+3=4=\operatorname{tr}(A)$ ✓, and multiply to
$1\cdot3=3=\det(A)$ ✓.

## Zero is never an eigenvector

<Callout type="warning">
**Accepting $v=0$ as an eigenvector.** The eigenvalue equation is trivially
true for the zero vector regardless of $\lambda$; it carries no
information and is excluded by definition.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Sign errors in the characteristic polynomial.** Double-check with the
trace/determinant shortcut: if your two eigenvalues don't sum to the
trace, you've made an arithmetic error somewhere.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Find the eigenvalues of Pauli-$Y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$
   using the trace/determinant shortcut.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\lambda = +1$ and $\lambda = -1$.

   For a $2\times2$ matrix the characteristic equation is
   $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A = 0$. Here

   $$
   \operatorname{tr}Y = 0 + 0 = 0, \qquad \det Y = (0)(0) - (-i)(i) = 0 - 1 = -1,
   $$

   so the equation is $\lambda^2 - 1 = 0$ and $\lambda = \pm1$.

   The determinant is where this goes wrong. $(-i)(i) = -i^2 = +1$, so
   subtracting it gives $\det Y = -1$, not $+1$. Getting $\det Y = +1$ produces
   $\lambda^2+1=0$ and the eigenvalues $\pm i$, which should be rejected on
   sight: $Y$ is Hermitian ($Y^\dagger = Y$, since transposing swaps $-i$ and
   $i$ and conjugating swaps them back), and a Hermitian operator's eigenvalues
   are real. That is the value of the shortcut, not the arithmetic saving but
   the two independent checks it hands you.

   The result is also physics rather than algebra. $Y$ represents a spin
   measurement along the $y$-axis, and $\pm1$ are the only two outcomes such a
   measurement can produce. All three Pauli matrices share this spectrum,
   which is the formal statement of "two spots, whichever axis you choose".

   </details>

2. Find an eigenvector of $Y$ for its eigenvalue $+1$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|{+i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}$, or any nonzero multiple of it.

   Solve $Yv = v$ with $v = (a,b)^\mathsf{T}$:

   $$
   \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-ib\\ia\end{pmatrix} \overset{!}{=} \begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   The first row gives $a = -ib$. The second row gives $b = ia$, which is the
   same equation rearranged (multiply the first by $i$), not new information.
   That redundancy is expected and is a sign the calculation is right: the
   whole point of $\lambda$ being an eigenvalue is that $Y - \lambda I$ is
   singular, so its two rows must be proportional. If both rows had given
   genuinely independent conditions, the only solution would be $v=0$ and
   $\lambda$ would not have been an eigenvalue.

   Choosing $a = 1$ gives $b = i$, so $v = (1, i)^\mathsf{T}$, normalized to
   $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,i)^\mathsf{T}$. Check:
   $Y(1,i)^\mathsf{T} = (-i\cdot i,\ i\cdot 1)^\mathsf{T} = (1, i)^\mathsf{T} \checkmark$.

   An eigenvector is only ever determined up to a scalar, so
   $(-i, 1)^\mathsf{T}$ is the same answer wearing a factor of $-i$; both
   describe one line through the origin, and after normalization both describe
   one physical state. Marking one "wrong" because it differs by a phase is a
   misreading of what the equation asks for.

   </details>

3. Find the eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&5\end{pmatrix}$ without
   any calculation. Explain why a diagonal matrix's eigenvalues are
   immediate.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\lambda = 3$ and $\lambda = 5$: the diagonal entries.

   The basis vectors are already eigenvectors. $A e_1 = (3,0)^\mathsf{T} = 3e_1$
   and $A e_2 = (0,5)^\mathsf{T} = 5e_2$, so each one is rescaled and not
   rotated, which is the definition. A diagonal matrix acts on each coordinate
   axis independently, stretching axis $i$ by the factor $A_{ii}$, and an
   eigenvalue problem asks precisely for the directions that get stretched.

   The characteristic polynomial agrees, of course:
   $\det(A-\lambda I) = (3-\lambda)(5-\lambda) = 0$, already factored, which is
   the algebraic shadow of the same fact.

   This is worth stating as a general principle because it runs in both
   directions. Reading eigenvalues off a diagonal matrix is trivial, and the
   spectral theorem's promise is that a Hermitian operator can *always* be
   brought to this form by choosing the right orthonormal basis. Diagonalizing
   is not a computational trick; it is the search for the basis in which an
   operator is as simple as this one already is.

   One boundary worth marking: it holds for triangular matrices too, where the
   eigenvalues are still the diagonal entries even though the basis vectors are
   no longer all eigenvectors. It does *not* hold for a general matrix, and
   reading the diagonal of, say, $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ would
   give $0, 0$ rather than the correct $\pm1$.

   </details>

4. A $2\times2$ matrix has trace $6$ and one eigenvalue is $2$. What is
   the other?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $4$, since $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = \operatorname{tr}A$ gives $2 + \lambda_2 = 6$.

   The sum rule falls straight out of the characteristic equation. Expanding
   $(\lambda-\lambda_1)(\lambda-\lambda_2) = \lambda^2 - (\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\lambda + \lambda_1\lambda_2$
   and matching it against
   $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\lambda + \det A$ identifies the trace with
   the sum of the eigenvalues and the determinant with their product.

   Notice how little the question had to tell you. The matrix's entries are
   never given, and they are not needed: trace and determinant are basis
   independent, so they constrain the spectrum no matter what basis the matrix
   happened to be written in. As a by-product, $\det A = 2 \times 4 = 8$, also
   determined without seeing a single entry.

   The mistake to avoid is reaching for the determinant relation instead and
   answering $3$, from $2\lambda_2 = 6$. Trace goes with the *sum*, determinant
   with the *product*. A quick way to keep them straight: $\operatorname{tr}I = 2$
   for a $2\times2$ identity whose eigenvalues are $1$ and $1$, and
   $1+1 = 2 \checkmark$ while $1\times1 = 1 = \det I \checkmark$.

   </details>

## Summary

- $A|v\rangle=\lambda|v\rangle$, $v\ne0$; eigenvalues are the roots of the
  characteristic equation $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$.
- For a $2\times2$ matrix, $\lambda^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)\lambda+\det(A)=0$:
  eigenvalues sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant.
- Pauli-$X$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ with eigenvectors $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$.
- A full eigenbasis diagonalizes an operator's matrix.
