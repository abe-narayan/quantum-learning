## The payoff of building it one piece at a time

A mixed problem reading as one problem is the
real payoff of building complex numbers, vector spaces, inner products,
<Term id="dirac-notation">bra-ket notation</Term>, operators, eigenvalues, Hermiticity, unitarity, and
tensor products one at a time, then connecting all of it to physical
prediction through the postulates. This lesson adds no new mathematics.
It only asks you to notice, on problems that don't announce which single
lesson they come from, that the toolkit already fits together.

**1. A real, symmetric $2\times2$ matrix $M$ satisfies $M^2=I$. Is $M$
necessarily unitary?**

    { label: "No, counterexamples exist", value: "no" },
    { label: "Only if M is also diagonal", value: "diagonal-only" },
    { label: "Can't tell without a specific M", value: "unsure" },

  correctValue="yes"
  explanation="Yes, necessarily. Real and symmetric means $M^\dagger = M^T = M$ (conjugation does nothing
to a real matrix), so $M$ is Hermitian. Check $M^\dagger M$: since
$M^\dagger=M$, $M^\dagger M = M^2 = I$ directly from the given condition.
So $M^\dagger M=I$, which is the unitarity condition. **Yes**,
necessarily. (Pauli-$X$ is the standard example: real, symmetric, and
$X^2=I$.) This combines the Hermitian Operators lesson's adjoint
definition with the Unitary Operators lesson's defining condition;
neither lesson alone states this fact.

**2. A $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix has trace $5$ and determinant $6$. What
are its eigenvalues, and are its eigenvectors necessarily orthogonal?**

From the Eigenvalues lesson's $2\times2$ shortcut,
$\lambda^2-5\lambda+6=0 \Rightarrow (\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)=0 \Rightarrow
\lambda=2,3$, two *different* eigenvalues. From the Hermitian Operators
lesson's second theorem, eigenvectors of a Hermitian operator with
different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, so **yes**, without
any further calculation.

**3. Two qubits are each individually <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term>. Is their combined
two-qubit state via the <Term id="tensor-product">tensor product</Term> automatically normalized too?**

<PredictBeforeReveal

  options={[

  correctValue="no"

Let $|u\rangle,|v\rangle$ each have norm $1$. Using the tensor-product
inner-product rule from the Tensor Products lesson,
$\langle u\otimes v, u\otimes v\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle\langle

renormalization step needed. This combines the Inner Products lesson's
norm definition with the Tensor Products lesson's multiplicative rule for
inner products on simple tensors.

## The error that spans the whole course

<Callout type="warning">
**The single most common error across this entire course: using an
amplitude directly instead of its squared modulus.** It shows up as early
as Complex Numbers and as late as the Born rule; always the same fix,
square the modulus first.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating Hermitian and unitary as synonyms, or assuming one implies the
other.** They're independent conditions; some operators (the Paulis) are
special enough to be both, but that's not the general case.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting *which* slot an <Term id="inner-product">inner product</Term> convention conjugates.** This
course conjugated the first argument throughout, matching bra-ket
notation; always check a source's convention before mixing formulas.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A $2\times2$ unitary matrix has real entries. Show that it must be a
   rotation matrix $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$
   or a reflection (same form with an overall sign flip on one column),
   using the fact that its columns must form an orthonormal set.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Write the columns as $c_1 = (a,c)^\mathsf{T}$ and $c_2 = (b,d)^\mathsf{T}$
   and impose orthonormality one condition at a time.

   $\|c_1\| = 1$ gives $a^2+c^2 = 1$. Every real solution of that equation is a
   point on the unit circle, so there is an angle $\theta$ with
   $a = \cos\theta$ and $c = \sin\theta$. That is a parametrization, not an
   assumption: no generality has been lost.

   $c_2 \perp c_1$ gives $ab+cd = 0$, so $c_2$ is perpendicular to $c_1$ in
   $\mathbb{R}^2$. The perpendicular line to a given direction is
   one-dimensional, so $c_2$ must be a multiple of $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$.

   $\|c_2\| = 1$ fixes that multiple to $\pm1$, and only those two, since the
   perpendicular direction is already a unit vector.

   The two signs give the two families. With $+$:

   $$
   U = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det U = \cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta = +1,
   $$

   a rotation. With $-$:

   $$
   U = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&-\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det U = -\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta = -1,
   $$

   a reflection, in the line at angle $\theta/2$ through the origin.

   The determinant is what separates them and it is the honest invariant here.
   Rotations preserve orientation, reflections reverse it, and no continuous
   family connects the two: a real $2\times2$ unitary group has two disconnected
   components. Notice also that conjugation never entered the argument, because
   the entries were assumed real, so "unitary" collapsed to "orthogonal" from
   the first line.

   The step most often skipped is the last one. Having found $c_2$ perpendicular
   to $c_1$, it is tempting to write down $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ and stop,
   which quietly throws away the reflections that the question explicitly asks
   for. Orthogonality fixes the line; only normalization fixes the length, and
   it leaves a sign behind.

   </details>

2. A Hermitian operator has eigenvalues $1, 1, 4$ (one repeated). Is a
   generic pair of eigenvectors from the repeated eigenvalue $1$
   guaranteed orthogonal to each other? Contrast with the eigenvector for
   eigenvalue $4$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No for the repeated pair, yes for the eigenvector of 4.

   The orthogonality theorem is stated for eigenvectors belonging to
   *different* eigenvalues, and its proof leans on that hypothesis at the final
   step: it derives $(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\langle u|v\rangle = 0$ and can only
   conclude $\langle u|v\rangle = 0$ because $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$ makes the
   first factor nonzero. For two eigenvectors sharing $\lambda = 1$, the
   equation reads $0 \cdot \langle u|v\rangle = 0$, which is true for every
   value of the overlap and therefore says nothing.

   Concretely, take $A = \mathrm{diag}(1,1,4)$. Both $(1,0,0)$ and
   $(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$ are eigenvectors with eigenvalue 1, and their overlap is
   $1/\sqrt2 \ne 0$. Nothing is broken; they simply are not orthogonal.

   What *is* guaranteed is weaker and still enough. The eigenvectors for
   $\lambda = 1$ form a two-dimensional subspace, the eigenspace, and any
   orthonormal basis of that subspace consists of eigenvectors for the same
   eigenvalue. So an orthogonal pair can always be *chosen*, by running
   Gram-Schmidt inside the eigenspace, and the spectral theorem's promise of an
   orthonormal eigenbasis survives. The choice just has to be made explicitly
   rather than falling out of the theorem.

   The eigenvector for $\lambda = 4$ is in a different position. Its eigenvalue
   differs from 1, so the theorem applies with no caveat, and it is orthogonal
   to *every* vector in the $\lambda=1$ eigenspace, whichever basis you picked
   there.

   The practical consequence, for a physicist rather than an algebraist: a
   degenerate eigenvalue means the measurement outcome 1 does not determine the
   post-measurement state. The state collapses into the eigenspace, not onto a
   line, and pinning it down further requires a second observable that
   distinguishes within that subspace. Degeneracy is the formal reason atoms
   need more than one quantum number.

   </details>

3. Two systems have dimensions $2$ and $3$. What is the dimension of their
   combined state space, and how many free parameters does a simple tensor
   $u\otimes v$ carry (the $n+m-1$ count from the Tensor Products lesson)?
   Compare the two numbers, and say what the gap between them means for
   how common entanglement is.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \dim(V\otimes W) = 2\times3 = 6, \qquad n+m-1 = 2+3-1 = 4.
   $$

   The gap is 2.

   The $-1$ in the parameter count is worth being able to justify. A simple
   tensor is specified by a direction for $u$ and a direction for $v$, but the
   pair $(u,v)$ and the pair $(2u,\ v/2)$ give the same product, so one scale
   parameter is shared between the two factors and must not be counted twice.
   That leaves $n + m - 1$.

   So the product states form a 4-parameter family sitting inside a
   6-dimensional space. They do not fill it, and the two dimensions they miss
   are where entanglement lives. In the strict sense, the simple tensors are a
   measure-zero subset: pick a vector in $V\otimes W$ at random and the
   probability that it factors is zero. Entanglement is not an exotic special
   case that has to be engineered, it is the generic situation, and product
   states are the rarity.

   The gap also grows with the dimensions, which is the real headline. At
   $n=m=2$ it is $4-3=1$; at $n=m=3$, $9-5=4$; at $n=m=10$, $100-19=81$. A
   product grows faster than a sum, so the more room two systems have
   individually, the more overwhelmingly their joint states tend to be
   entangled.

   One caution about the word "parameters": the counts above are complex
   parameters before normalization and global phase are removed, and both
   removals shave a small constant off each side. They do not change the
   comparison, which is what the question turns on, but a count that disagrees
   with these by one or two is probably measuring the physical state space
   rather than the vector space.

   </details>

4. A state $|\psi\rangle$ is measured with a Hermitian observable whose
   spectral decomposition is $A = 2|e_1\rangle\langle e_1| +
   5|e_2\rangle\langle e_2|$. If $\langle A\rangle = 3.5$ and there are
   only two possible outcomes, find $P(2)$ and $P(5)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   P(2) = P(5) = 0.5.
   $$

   Two equations, two unknowns. Normalization gives $P(2)+P(5) = 1$, and the
   expectation value gives

   $$
   2P(2) + 5P(5) = 3.5.
   $$

   Substituting $P(2) = 1 - P(5)$:
   $2 - 2P(5) + 5P(5) = 3.5$, so $3P(5) = 1.5$ and $P(5) = 0.5$, hence
   $P(2) = 0.5$.

   There is a faster reading. $3.5$ sits exactly halfway between the two
   eigenvalues 2 and 5, and an expectation value is the probability-weighted
   average of the outcomes, so the midpoint can only be reached by weighting
   them equally. More generally, $\langle A\rangle$ divides the interval
   $[\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}]$ in the ratio of the two probabilities,
   which is a useful sanity check on any two-outcome calculation.

   Note what the question did *not* give you: $|\psi\rangle$ itself. It is not
   needed, and it is not even determined by the data. Any state with
   $|\langle e_1|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle e_2|\psi\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$ works,
   and there is a whole circle of them differing by the relative phase between
   the two components, all sharing this $\langle A\rangle$. An expectation value
   is a single number extracted from a state; it cannot run backwards to recover
   one.

   The answer to reject is anything outside $[2,5]$, or a negative probability.
   An expectation value always lies between the smallest and largest
   eigenvalues, so if the algebra produces $P(5) = 1.2$ the arithmetic went
   wrong before the physics did.

   </details>

5. Explain why the characteristic equation, completeness relation, and
   Born rule are each, in their own way, consequences of requiring
   *consistency* (probabilities summing to $1$, matrix elements
   determining an operator fully) rather than being independent
   assumptions bolted onto the theory.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The common thread is that each one is forced once you insist a
   previously-made commitment be honoured everywhere, rather than being an extra
   input.

   **The characteristic equation** is forced by the demand that an eigenvector
   be nonzero. $A|v\rangle = \lambda|v\rangle$ rearranges to
   $(A-\lambda I)|v\rangle = 0$, and asking for a nonzero solution is asking for
   $A - \lambda I$ to be non-invertible, which is exactly
   $\det(A-\lambda I) = 0$. Nobody chose that polynomial; it is the
   invertibility criterion applied to a demand already made.

   **The completeness relation** is forced by the demand that expanding a vector
   in a basis and reassembling it return the same vector.
   $\sum_i |e_i\rangle\langle e_i|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle$ for every
   $|\psi\rangle$ is precisely the statement that the coefficients
   $\langle e_i|\psi\rangle$ lose nothing, which is what "basis" was supposed to
   mean. Written as an operator identity that reads
   $\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i| = I$, and everything downstream, matrix
   elements determining an operator, the derivation of matrix multiplication,
   spectral decomposition, is that one identity inserted where it is convenient.

   **The Born rule** is the one place where the argument is different in kind,
   and it is worth being honest about that. Given that probabilities must be
   non-negative, sum to 1 across any orthonormal basis, and be unchanged by
   global phase, $|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$ is a natural candidate and is
   forced by normalization to have the right total, since
   $\sum_i|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$. Gleason's
   theorem strengthens this to near-inevitability: in dimension three or higher,
   the squared overlap is the *only* probability assignment consistent with
   those requirements. But the link from mathematical object to observed
   frequency is still a postulate; consistency narrows the field to one
   candidate rather than deriving the candidate from nothing.

   So the honest summary is that two of the three are theorems dressed as
   definitions, and the third is a postulate that consistency leaves almost no
   freedom in. The reason to see it this way is that it changes what you have to
   remember: a formalism whose pieces are consequences of each other can be
   reconstructed from a couple of commitments, whereas a list of independent
   axioms can only be memorized.

   </details>

6. Looking back across all eleven lessons: which single result would you
   consider the most important prerequisite for everything that comes
   after it in this course? Defend your choice in two or three sentences.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>A defensible answer</summary>

   This one has no single correct answer, and it is not marked on which result
   you name. It is marked on whether the defence actually traces dependencies,
   so read the argument below as one worked example of the kind of case to make,
   not as the answer.

   **The spectral theorem** is the strongest candidate. It is what lets a
   Hermitian operator be written $A = \sum_i \lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$,
   and once that is available the measurement postulate has something to attach
   to: the $\lambda_i$ are the possible outcomes, the $|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$
   are the projectors collapse acts by, and the Born rule has an orthonormal
   basis to compute overlaps against. Without it there is no guarantee that a
   general observable even *has* enough eigenvectors to span the space, and
   expectation values, degeneracy, compatible observables and the whole
   measurement chapter have nothing to stand on.

   Two other choices are at least as defensible if the case is made properly.
   The **inner product with its conjugation** is more foundational in the strict
   sense, since the spectral theorem's statement uses orthogonality, which does
   not exist until an inner product does, and no probability is real or
   non-negative without it. And **completeness**, $\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i| = I$,
   is the most heavily used, appearing in the derivation of matrix
   multiplication, in every basis change, and inside the spectral theorem
   itself.

   What would *not* be a good answer is naming a result and then praising it in
   the abstract. The question asks for a prerequisite, so the defence has to
   name at least one specific later result that fails without it.

   </details>
