## Stock prices, chords, and quantum states

An arrow in 3D space is a vector. So is a list of stock prices, a
polynomial, a musical chord, and, the case this course cares about, a
<Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term>. These don't look alike, but they share the two
operations that matter: you can add two of them, and you can scale one by
a number. **<Term id="vector-space">Vector space</Term>** is the name for any set equipped with those two
operations, obeying a short list of sensible rules. Once you accept that
definition, every theorem proved about "vectors" in the abstract applies
equally to arrows, polynomials, and quantum states: you never have to
re-derive linear algebra separately for each new kind of object physics
throws at you.

## Forget arrows; keep the two operations

Forget arrows for a moment. The only things you ever *do* with a
vector are: add two of them to get a third, and stretch/shrink one by a
number. If a collection of objects supports those two operations and they
behave the way you'd expect (addition doesn't care about order, scaling by
$1$ does nothing, and so on), it's a vector space, full stop, regardless
of whether "vector" conjures an arrow in your head or not.

## Eight axioms, then span, basis, and dimension

### The axioms

A **vector space** over a **field** of scalars $F$ is a set $V$ with an
addition $+$ and a scalar multiplication, satisfying, for all
$u,v,w\in V$ and $a,b\in F$, the eight axioms below. "Field" here is a
piece of algebraic vocabulary that this course never needs in its general
form: it means a number system in which you can add, subtract, multiply
and divide by anything except zero, with the usual rules. For every
purpose in this curriculum $F$ is either $\mathbb{R}$ (the real numbers)
or $\mathbb{C}$ (the complex numbers), and reading "$F$" as "$\mathbb{R}$
or $\mathbb{C}$, whichever the context calls for" loses nothing at all.
The axioms:

1. **Closure**: $u+v\in V$ and $av \in V$.
2. **Commutativity**: $u+v=v+u$.
3. **Associativity of addition**: $(u+v)+w=u+(v+w)$.
4. **Zero vector**: there is a $0\in V$ with $v+0=v$ for all $v$.
5. **Additive inverse**: for every $v$ there is $-v$ with $v+(-v)=0$.
6. **Scalar identity**: $1v=v$.
7. **Distributivity**: $a(u+v)=au+av$ and $(a+b)v=av+bv$.
8. **Associativity of scaling**: $a(bv)=(ab)v$.

This is a checklist, not a proof burden: to show something *is* a vector
space, verify the operations satisfy all eight; to show it *isn't*, find
one axiom that fails. $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{C}^n$ (tuples of $n$
real or complex numbers, added and scaled entrywise) are the working
examples this course uses, but the definition doesn't care that the
"vectors" are tuples: the set of all polynomials of degree $\le 3$, or the
set of all functions $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, are vector spaces too,
under ordinary addition and scaling.

### Linear combinations and span

A **linear combination** of vectors $v_1,\ldots,v_k$ is
$a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k$ for scalars $a_i$. The **<Term id="span">span</Term>** of a set of vectors
is the set of *all* linear combinations of them: everything reachable by
adding and scaling just those vectors.

### Linear independence

A set $\{v_1,\ldots,v_k\}$ is **<Term id="linear-independence">linearly independent</Term>** if the only
combination equal to the zero vector is the trivial one:

$$
a_1v_1+\cdots+a_kv_k = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_1=\cdots=a_k=0
$$

Equivalently: no vector in the set is a combination of the others; each
one adds a direction the others cannot reach.
Independence depends on *which scalars you're allowed to use*. Over
$\mathbb{C}$, the vectors $(1,0)$ and $(i,0)$ are **dependent**,
since $(i,0)=i\cdot(1,0)$, even though they look like "different" vectors:
a complex scalar is all it takes to turn one into the other.

### Basis and dimension

A **<Term id="basis">basis</Term>** is a linearly independent set that spans $V$. Two facts (both
real theorems, stated here rather than proved in full): every vector space
with a finite spanning set has a basis (discard redundant vectors from the
spanning set until none is left), and every basis of a given $V$ has the
same number of vectors. That number is the **dimension**, $\dim V$.

### Coordinates

Fix a basis $\{e_1,\ldots,e_n\}$. Because it spans $V$, every $v\in V$
can be written $v=c_1e_1+\cdots+c_ne_n$; because the basis is
independent, that expression is **unique**. If $v$ also equalled
$c_1'e_1+\cdots+c_n'e_n$, subtracting gives
$(c_1-c_1')e_1+\cdots+(c_n-c_n')e_n=0$, which by independence forces every
$c_i=c_i'$. The numbers $(c_1,\ldots,c_n)$ are $v$'s **coordinates**
relative to this basis. This is what a column vector like
$\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$ already was in every earlier
piece of quantum notation you may have seen: coordinates relative to the
standard basis $\{(1,0),(0,1)\}$, nothing more.

### Change of basis

Coordinates depend on which basis you chose. Consider $\mathbb{R}^2$ with
the standard basis $e_1=(1,0), e_2=(0,1)$, and a second basis rotated by
angle $\theta$: $e_1'=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$,
$e_2'=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$. A vector with standard coordinates
$(x,y)$ has new coordinates $(x',y')$ found by solving
$x e_1+y e_2 = x'e_1'+y'e_2'$ for $x',y'$, which works out to:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "prime", symbol: "(x′, y′)", gloss: "The same vector's coordinates in the rotated basis {e1′, e2′}: a description, not a new vector." },
    { id: "matrix", symbol: "rotation matrix", gloss: "Built entirely from θ, the angle between the two bases. The whole change of description comes from that one number." },
    { id: "xy", symbol: "(x, y)", gloss: "The vector's coordinates in the original standard basis {e1, e2}." },
  ]}
>
$$
\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta\\-\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}
$$
</EquationReveal>

The vector itself hasn't moved. Only its *description* changed, because
you changed the ruler you're measuring it against. This distinction,
physical object versus its coordinates in a chosen basis, matters a great
deal once operators enter the picture: a matrix representation always
depends on a choice of basis, even though the underlying operator doesn't.

### Complex vector spaces, and a subtlety about dimension

Everything above works verbatim with $F=\mathbb{C}$: $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a
vector space over $\mathbb{C}$, of complex dimension $n$ (the standard
basis $\{(1,0,\ldots),(0,1,0,\ldots),\ldots\}$ has $n$ vectors). Here is
the subtlety: you can also view $\mathbb{C}^n$ as a vector space over
$\mathbb{R}$, restricting scalars to real numbers only, and by that
measure its dimension **doubles**, because each complex coordinate needs
two independent real numbers (its real and imaginary parts) to pin down.
So $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^n) = n$ but
$\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^n) = 2n$. This is not pedantry. It is why a
single qubit's state space $\mathbb{C}^2$ is called
"2-dimensional" in quantum computing (complex dimension) while its
geometric picture, the Bloch sphere, an ordinary 2-dimensional *surface*
sitting in 3 real dimensions, comes from a different counting entirely
(4 real dimensions in $\mathbb{C}^2$, minus 1 for the normalization
constraint, minus 1 more because overall phase carries no physical
information, leaves 2). If you've seen the Bloch sphere in the Quantum
Computing track, this is the dimension-counting behind why it's a
2-dimensional surface rather than some higher-dimensional object.

## One algebra, many things to call a vector

Physics gets to choose, for each system, what plays the role of "vector."
For a qubit it's a pair of complex amplitudes; for a particle's spatial
wavefunction it's an entire complex-valued function. The payoff of the
abstract axioms is that *the same linear algebra*, span, independence,
basis, and everything built on top of it later in this course, applies
to both without modification.

## Do these three vectors form a basis?

**Is $\{(1,1,0), (0,1,1), (1,0,1)\}$ a basis for $\mathbb{C}^3$?**

Three vectors in a 3-dimensional space form a basis exactly when they're
linearly independent (independence plus the right count automatically
gives spanning). Set $a(1,1,0)+b(0,1,1)+c(1,0,1)=(0,0,0)$:

$$
a+c=0, \qquad a+b=0, \qquad b+c=0
$$

From the first two, $c=-a$ and $b=-a$. Substituting into the third:
$-a+(-a)=0 \Rightarrow -2a=0 \Rightarrow a=0$, forcing $b=c=0$ too. Only
the trivial combination gives zero, so the set is independent, and with
exactly $3=\dim\mathbb{C}^3$ vectors, it's a basis.

## Spanning, and the field you check over

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing "spans the space" with "is a basis."** A spanning set can have
*extra*, redundant vectors; a basis is the special case where there's no
redundancy left to remove.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Checking independence over the wrong field.** $(1,0)$ and $(i,0)$ are
dependent over $\mathbb{C}$ but would be treated as unrelated if you
carelessly reasoned about them the way you would two real vectors that
merely "look different."
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Complex dimension" and "real dimension" are not the same number.**
$\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^n)=2n$, not $n$. Always know which one a
problem is asking for.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is $\{(1,2),(2,4)\}$ linearly independent in $\mathbb{C}^2$? Justify
   your answer.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. The second vector is twice the first, $(2,4) = 2(1,2)$, so there is a
   combination with coefficients not both zero that gives the zero vector:

   $$
   2\,(1,2) + (-1)\,(2,4) = (0,0).
   $$

   That is exactly what linear dependence means, and exhibiting one such
   combination is a complete proof. The set spans only the one-dimensional
   subspace $\{c(1,2) : c \in \mathbb{C}\}$, a line through the origin, not the
   whole plane, so it cannot be a basis for $\mathbb{C}^2$.

   The check worth internalizing: two vectors in any space are dependent
   precisely when one is a scalar multiple of the other, so scan the ratios
   componentwise. Here $2/1 = 2$ and $4/2 = 2$ agree, which is the dependence.
   Had they disagreed, the pair would have been independent.

   For $2\times2$ matrices the determinant says the same thing:
   $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\2&4\end{pmatrix} = 4-4 = 0$, and a vanishing
   determinant means dependent columns. That shortcut is convenient but does
   not generalize to non-square collections, whereas producing an explicit
   nontrivial combination always does.

   </details>

2. Find the coordinates of $v=(3,5)$ relative to the basis
   $e_1'=(1,1), e_2'=(1,-1)$ of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $v = 4e_1' - e_2'$, so the coordinates are $(4,-1)$.

   Write $v = a e_1' + b e_2'$ and read off both components:

   $$
   a + b = 3, \qquad a - b = 5.
   $$

   Adding gives $2a = 8$, so $a = 4$; subtracting gives $2b = -2$, so $b = -1$.
   Check: $4(1,1) - (1,-1) = (4-1,\ 4+1) = (3,5) \checkmark$.

   The point of the exercise is that "the coordinates of $v$" is not a property
   of $v$ alone. The same vector is $(3,5)$ in the standard basis and $(4,-1)$
   in this one; the arrow has not moved, only the two rulers being used to
   describe it. Every later appearance of a change of basis, and every claim
   that some quantum result is "basis independent", is a claim about which of
   these two kinds of object is being talked about.

   The one shortcut available here is worth flagging as a shortcut. Because
   $e_1'$ and $e_2'$ happen to be orthogonal, the coefficients can also be got
   by projection, $a = \langle e_1', v\rangle / \|e_1'\|^2 = 8/2 = 4$. That
   works only for an orthogonal basis; for a general basis, solving the linear
   system is the method.

   </details>

3. What is $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^4)$? What is
   $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^4)$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^4) = 4$ and $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^4) = 8$.

   Over $\mathbb{C}$, the four vectors $e_1,\dots,e_4$ are a basis: every
   element of $\mathbb{C}^4$ is a complex combination of them, and no fewer
   will do.

   Over $\mathbb{R}$, complex scalars are no longer available, so $ie_1$ can no
   longer be reached from $e_1$ by scaling and has to be supplied separately.
   A real basis is $\{e_1, ie_1, e_2, ie_2, e_3, ie_3, e_4, ie_4\}$: eight
   vectors. Equivalently, each complex coordinate carries two independent real
   numbers.

   Dimension is a property of the pair (space, field of scalars), never of the
   set alone. The same $\mathbb{C}^4$ has dimension 4 or 8 depending on what
   you are allowed to multiply by, which is why the subscript on $\dim$ is not
   decoration.

   This is the counting a single qubit already uses. A qubit state lives in
   $\mathbb{C}^2$: two complex amplitudes, so four real parameters, from which
   normalization removes one and global phase another, leaving the two that
   place a point on the Bloch sphere.

   </details>

4. Is the set of all $2\times2$ complex matrices a vector space under
   matrix addition and scalar multiplication? If so, propose a basis and
   state its dimension.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes, and $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} = 4$.

   All eight axioms hold, and each for an unremarkable reason: matrices add
   entrywise, so commutativity and associativity of addition are inherited from
   $\mathbb{C}$ one entry at a time; the zero matrix is the zero vector;
   $-A$ is the additive inverse; and the two distributive laws and
   $1 \cdot A = A$ are again entrywise facts.

   A basis is the four matrix units:

   $$
   E_{11} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{12} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{21} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad E_{22} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   They span, since $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix} = aE_{11}+bE_{12}+cE_{21}+dE_{22}$,
   and they are independent, since that combination is the zero matrix only
   when $a=b=c=d=0$. So the space is $\mathbb{C}^4$ with the entries written in
   a square instead of a column.

   The trap in the question is matrix multiplication. Matrices come with a
   product, and the product is *not* part of what makes this a vector space;
   the definition asks only for addition and scaling. Nothing here needs to be
   checked about $AB$, and in particular the fact that $AB \ne BA$ is
   irrelevant to the answer. (A vector space that also carries a compatible
   product has a longer name, an algebra, and that extra structure is what the
   Linear Operators lesson goes on to use.)

   The Pauli matrices with the identity, $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$, form another basis
   of this same space, which is the fact behind writing any single-qubit
   Hamiltonian as a combination of Paulis.

   </details>

## Summary

- A vector space is any set with addition and scalar multiplication
  obeying eight axioms: "vector" doesn't have to mean "arrow."
- Linear independence, span, basis, and dimension are defined
  independently of what the vectors "are."
- Coordinates are a vector's description relative to a chosen basis;
  changing the basis changes the coordinates, not the vector.
- $\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbb{C}^n)=n$, but
  $\dim_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbb{C}^n)=2n$, a distinction that directly
  explains the Bloch sphere's dimension count.
