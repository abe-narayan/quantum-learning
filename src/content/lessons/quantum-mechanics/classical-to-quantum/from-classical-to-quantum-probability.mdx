## The phenomenon Feynman called impossible

Richard Feynman once called the <Term id="double-slit-experiment">double-slit experiment</Term> "a phenomenon
which is impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical
way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics." That's a bold
claim for an experiment simple enough to set up with a laser pointer and
two closely-spaced slits cut in foil. This lesson works through the
actual numbers behind that claim.

The last lesson pinned down what classical probability is: it always
describes ignorance about one underlying definite fact. The double-slit
pattern is the concrete case where that definition breaks down, not
through any subtlety in how the ignorance gets described, but in the raw
measured numbers themselves.

## One particle at a time, and still a fringe

Fire particles, electrons, photons, even large molecules, one at a
time at a screen with two narrow slits, and record where each one lands.
Common sense says: each particle goes through slit 1 or slit 2 (maybe you
don't know which, but *something* definite happens), and the pattern that
builds up should be the sum of "pattern with only slit 1 open" and
"pattern with only slit 2 open." That's not what happens. The actual
pattern has bright and dark fringes, bands where particles land far more
or less often than either single-slit pattern alone would predict, some
spots going almost completely dark even though *both* slits are open and
either one alone would have put particles there.

## Add the amplitudes first, then square

### The classical prediction

If "going through slit 1" and "going through slit 2" are mutually
exclusive events, whatever your uncertainty about which one happens,
ordinary (Kolmogorov) probability requires:

$$
P(\text{land at } x) = P_1(\text{land at } x) + P_2(\text{land at } x)
$$

where $P_1, P_2$ are the probabilities with only that one slit open. This
is just the classical addition rule from the last lesson, applied to two
alternatives. It doesn't matter *what* hidden fact determines which slit a
given particle used, or whether you know it. As long as some definite
fact exists, probabilities of exclusive alternatives add.

### The actual rule: add amplitudes, then square

Experimentally, the correct prediction instead assigns a complex
**<Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term>** to each path, $\psi_1$ for slit 1, $\psi_2$ for slit 2,
and adds the amplitudes *before* computing a probability:

$$
P(\text{land at } x) = |\psi_1(x) + \psi_2(x)|^2
$$

Because $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are complex, this is **not** the same as
$|\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2$ in general. Expanding the square directly
shows why:

$$
|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 = (\psi_1+\psi_2)^*(\psi_1+\psi_2) = |\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+\underbrace{\psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1}_{\text{cross terms}}
$$

The cross terms are the entire story: the classical addition rule has no
such term at all, while $\lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$ always carries one,
and its size and sign are set by the <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative phase</Term> between the two
amplitudes. Where the cross terms are positive, the pattern brightens
past the classical prediction (**constructive interference**); where
they're negative, it dims, potentially to zero even though both
individual probabilities are positive (**destructive interference**).

### A concrete calculation

Take two equal-magnitude contributions, $\psi_1=0.5$ and $\psi_2 =
0.5e^{i\theta}$, and compare the classical sum to the actual quantum rule
as $\theta$ varies:

| $\theta$ | $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2+\lvert\psi_2\rvert^2$ (classical) | $\lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$ (quantum) |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ (in phase) | $0.5$ | $1.0$ |
| $\pi/2$ | $0.5$ | $0.5$ |
| $\pi$ (opposite phase) | $0.5$ | $0.0$ |

The classical prediction never changes; it only depends on the
individual magnitudes, which are fixed at $0.5$ each. The quantum
prediction swings from **double** the classical value (perfectly
constructive) to **zero** (perfectly destructive), purely as a function of
relative phase. That swing is the fringe pattern. Moving across the
screen changes $x$, which changes the difference in path length from the
two slits, which changes $\theta$; the table's three rows are three
points on that sweep, and the bright and dark bands are where it tops out
and bottoms out.

### Why no hidden definite path explains this

Suppose, to save the classical picture, each particle really does travel
through exactly one slit, definitely, and you're merely ignorant of
which. Then the classical addition rule from the last lesson has to hold.
It holds for *any* probability distribution over any hidden variables
whatsoever, as long as the underlying event ("used slit 1" vs. "used slit
2") is genuinely one-or-the-other. The $\theta=\pi$ row above is the
direct contradiction: the observed probability is exactly zero at a point
where each individual slit, alone, would place particles there with
positive probability. No redistribution of hidden ignorance changes that
row's arithmetic. The amplitude-based rule isn't an alternative
description of the same physics. It's required by the actual numbers.

## Run with molecules, not just electrons

The experiment has been run, not merely imagined, with electrons,
neutrons, whole atoms, and molecules built from hundreds of atoms, fired
one particle at a time. The fringes build up dot by dot, and each dot
lands where the amplitude rule says it should.

Now push on the setup. Nothing in the argument above required you to stay
ignorant of which slit the particle used; the classical rule assumed only
that some definite fact existed, known or not. So put a detector at the
slits and find out which one it was.

So which-path information is not free bookkeeping laid over an unchanged
process. Learning the path changes what the screen shows. That is a claim
about what measurement is, not about how good detectors are, and the
lessons on the postulates return to it.

## Two amplitudes sixty degrees apart

**Two paths contribute amplitudes $\psi_1 = 0.6$ and $\psi_2 =
0.6e^{i\pi/3}$. Find the quantum probability and compare to the classical
prediction.**

Classical: $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2 = 0.36+0.36=0.72$.

Quantum: $\psi_2 = 0.6(\cos 60° + i\sin 60°) = 0.3+0.3\sqrt3\,i \approx
0.3+0.5196i$. Then $\psi_1+\psi_2 \approx 0.9+0.5196i$, and

$$
|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 \approx 0.9^2+0.5196^2 \approx 0.81+0.27=1.08
$$

The quantum prediction ($1.08$) exceeds the classical one ($0.72$),
partial constructive interference, consistent with $\theta=\pi/3$ sitting
between the fully-constructive ($\theta=0$) and neutral ($\theta=\pi/2$)
cases from the table above.

## Exact and reproducible, one particle at a time

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming interference is just "small effects averaging out weirdly."**
It's exact, reproducible, single-particle-at-a-time behavior, not a
statistical artifact, and it can send a probability all the
way to zero at a spot where two positive contributions individually
placed particles.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Believing a not-yet-invented, more clever hidden variable could still
rescue the classical addition rule.** The contradiction shown above uses
only the *actually measured* probabilities and the classical addition
axiom. It doesn't depend on any specific guess about what the hidden
variable might be.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $\psi_1=0.4$ and $\psi_2=0.4e^{i\pi}$, compute the quantum
   probability and compare it to the classical prediction.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Quantum: $0$. Classical: $0.32$.

   Start by turning the phase factor into a number. $e^{i\pi} = -1$
   (Euler's formula at half a turn), so $\psi_2 = -0.4$ exactly, with no
   imaginary part at all.

   Quantum rule, amplitudes first:

   $$
   |\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 = |0.4 + (-0.4)|^2 = |0|^2 = 0
   $$

   Classical rule, probabilities first:

   $$
   |\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2 = 0.16+0.16 = 0.32
   $$

   This is the $\theta=\pi$ row of the lesson's table, run with magnitude
   $0.4$ instead of $0.5$. It is the row that does the arguing. Each path
   alone would land particles here $16\%$ of the time. Open both and the
   count goes to zero. There is no assignment of probabilities to "used
   slit 1" and "used slit 2" that produces $0$ from two positive numbers,
   because $P_1+P_2 = 0$ forces $P_1=P_2=0$, contradicting the
   single-slit measurements.

   The specific wrong turn to watch for is computing
   $|\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2 e^{i\pi}|^2$ and announcing that the phase
   "cancels out" because $|e^{i\pi}|=1$. That is true, and it is exactly
   why the classical answer is stuck at $0.32$ regardless of $\theta$.
   The magnitude of $\psi_2$ never changed. What changed is the direction
   $\psi_2$ points when it is added to $\psi_1$, and addition happens
   before the magnitude is taken, not after.

   </details>

2. For $\psi_1=0.3$ and $\psi_2=0.3e^{i\pi/2}$, compute both predictions.
   Is this constructive, destructive, or neither?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Both predictions are $0.18$. Neither constructive nor destructive.

   $e^{i\pi/2} = i$, so $\psi_2 = 0.3i$ and

   $$
   \psi_1+\psi_2 = 0.3+0.3i, \qquad |\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 = 0.3^2+0.3^2 = 0.18
   $$

   The classical prediction is $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2 = 0.09+0.09 = 0.18$
   as well. They agree.

   Do not read that agreement as "no interference happened here." The
   cross terms are still there in the algebra; they are
   $\psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1 = (0.3)(0.3i) + (-0.3i)(0.3) =
   0.09i - 0.09i = 0$. Two nonzero contributions cancelled each other
   because they are complex conjugates and a purely imaginary number plus
   its conjugate is zero. Interference is present and its net effect is
   nil, which is a different statement from interference being absent.

   Geometrically this is the right angle. Adding two perpendicular
   vectors of length $0.3$ gives a hypotenuse whose squared length is the
   sum of the squared legs, and Pythagoras is precisely the statement that
   the cross term vanishes. Every other angle breaks the right triangle
   and the two predictions separate again.

   This is the $\theta=\pi/2$ row of the table, and it is the row students
   most often use to conclude that the quantum and classical rules "mostly
   agree and differ only in edge cases." The parametric sweep in the next
   lesson shows the opposite: agreement happens at two isolated angles out
   of a full turn, and everywhere else the two curves are apart.

   </details>

3. Explain, in your own words, why $P(\text{land at }x) =
   \lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$ reducing to
   $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2+\lvert\psi_2\rvert^2$ at $\theta=\pi/2$ is
   consistent with the cross-term formula derived above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the cross-term formula does not say the cross terms are always
   nonzero. It says they are proportional to $\cos\theta$, and $\cos\theta$
   has zeros.

   Expanding the square gave

   $$
   |\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 = |\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+\left(\psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1\right)
   $$

   A number plus its own conjugate is twice its real part, so the bracket
   is $2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2)$. With $\psi_1$ real and positive
   and $\psi_2 = |\psi_2|e^{i\theta}$, that is
   $2|\psi_1||\psi_2|\cos\theta$. At $\theta=\pi/2$, $\cos\theta = 0$ and
   the entire bracket vanishes, leaving exactly the classical expression.

   So the quantum rule does not contradict the classical rule at every
   angle. It contains it as the special case where the relative phase
   happens to sit at a quarter turn. The lesson's claim is stronger and
   narrower than "the classical rule is wrong": the classical rule is a
   fixed number, the quantum rule is a curve, and the curve passes through
   that number twice per turn.

   The wrong version of this answer is "the cross terms cancel because
   $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ have equal magnitudes." Equal magnitudes have
   nothing to do with it. Run the same expansion with $\psi_1=0.6$ and
   $\psi_2=0.2e^{i\pi/2}$: the cross term is $2(0.6)(0.2)\cos(\pi/2)=0$
   again, and the predictions agree again, at $0.40$ this time. What
   makes the term vanish is the angle, and only the angle. Equal
   magnitudes matter for a different question, whether destructive
   interference can reach exactly zero, which is what the challenge below
   is about.

   </details>

## Summary

- Classical probability requires $P = P_1+P_2$ for mutually exclusive
  alternatives, always, regardless of what the hidden alternative is.
- The measured double-slit pattern instead follows $P =
  \lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$, which can range from double the classical
  value to exactly zero depending on relative phase.
- This numerically contradicts the classical rule at specific, calculable
  points, ruling out any hidden-definite-path explanation, not merely
  disfavoring one.
- Which-path measurement destroys the interference and restores the
  classical rule, a genuine physical effect, not a limitation of
  detectors.
