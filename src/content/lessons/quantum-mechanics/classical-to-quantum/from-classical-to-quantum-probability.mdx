## Motivation

The last lesson defined classical probability precisely: it always
describes ignorance about one underlying definite fact. This lesson
presents a real, repeatedly confirmed experiment whose numbers cannot be
explained that way, no matter how the ignorance is described: the
double-slit experiment, and interference from it.

## Intuition

Fire particles, electrons, photons, even large molecules, one at a
time at a screen with two narrow slits, and record where each one lands.
Common sense says: each particle goes through slit 1 or slit 2 (maybe you
don't know which, but *something* definite happens), and the pattern that
builds up should be the sum of "pattern with only slit 1 open" and
"pattern with only slit 2 open." That's not what happens. The actual
pattern has bright and dark fringes, bands where particles land far more
or less often than either single-slit pattern alone would predict, some
spots going almost completely dark even though *both* slits are open and
either one alone would have put particles there.

## Mathematical Development

### The classical prediction

If "going through slit 1" and "going through slit 2" are mutually
exclusive events, whatever your uncertainty about which one happens,
ordinary (Kolmogorov) probability requires:

$$
P(\text{land at } x) = P_1(\text{land at } x) + P_2(\text{land at } x)
$$

where $P_1, P_2$ are the probabilities with only that one slit open. This
is just the classical addition rule from the last lesson, applied to two
alternatives. It doesn't matter *what* hidden fact determines which slit a
given particle used, or whether you know it. As long as some definite
fact exists, probabilities of exclusive alternatives add.

### The actual rule: add amplitudes, then square

Experimentally, the correct prediction instead assigns a complex
**amplitude** to each path, $\psi_1$ for slit 1, $\psi_2$ for slit 2,
and adds the amplitudes *before* computing a probability:

$$
P(\text{land at } x) = |\psi_1(x) + \psi_2(x)|^2
$$

Because $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are complex, this is **not** the same as
$|\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2$ in general. Expanding the square directly
shows why:

$$
|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 = (\psi_1+\psi_2)^*(\psi_1+\psi_2) = |\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+\underbrace{\psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1}_{\text{cross terms}}
$$

The cross terms are the entire story: they vanish for real, same-sign
numbers added the "classical" way, but not for complex amplitudes with a
relative phase. Where the cross terms are positive, the pattern brightens
past the classical prediction (**constructive interference**); where
they're negative, it dims, potentially to zero even though both
individual probabilities are positive (**destructive interference**).

### A concrete calculation

Take two equal-magnitude contributions, $\psi_1=0.5$ and $\psi_2 =
0.5e^{i\theta}$, and compare the classical sum to the actual quantum rule
as $\theta$ varies:

| $\theta$ | $\lvert\psi_1\rvert^2+\lvert\psi_2\rvert^2$ (classical) | $\lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$ (quantum) |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ (in phase) | $0.5$ | $1.0$ |
| $\pi/2$ | $0.5$ | $0.5$ |
| $\pi$ (opposite phase) | $0.5$ | $0.0$ |

The classical prediction never changes; it only depends on the
individual magnitudes, which are fixed at $0.5$ each. The quantum
prediction swings from **double** the classical value (perfectly
constructive) to **zero** (perfectly destructive), purely as a function of
relative phase. This is exactly the fringe pattern: as position $x$ on the
screen changes, the relative phase between the two paths changes (their
path lengths to the two slits differ), sweeping through exactly this
range.

### Why no hidden definite path explains this

Suppose, to save the classical picture, each particle really does travel
through exactly one slit, definitely, and you're merely ignorant of
which. Then the classical addition rule from the last lesson has to hold.
It holds for *any* probability distribution over any hidden variables
whatsoever, as long as the underlying event ("used slit 1" vs. "used slit
2") is genuinely one-or-the-other. The $\theta=\pi$ row above is the
direct contradiction: the observed probability is exactly zero at a point
where each individual slit, alone, would place particles there with
positive probability. No redistribution of hidden ignorance changes that
row's arithmetic. The amplitude-based rule isn't an alternative
description of the same physics. It's required by the actual numbers.

## Physical Interpretation

This isn't a thought experiment. It's been performed with electrons,
neutrons, atoms, and molecules of hundreds of atoms, one particle at a
time, and the interference pattern always builds up exactly as the
amplitude rule predicts. A related, equally important fact: if you add a
detector that determines which slit each particle actually used, the
interference pattern disappears, and the classical addition rule is
restored. Extracting which-path information genuinely changes the
physical outcome, a deep fact about measurement this course will return
to, not a limitation of the detector.

## Worked Example

**Two paths contribute amplitudes $\psi_1 = 0.6$ and $\psi_2 =
0.6e^{i\pi/3}$. Find the quantum probability and compare to the classical
prediction.**

Classical: $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2 = 0.36+0.36=0.72$.

Quantum: $\psi_2 = 0.6(\cos 60° + i\sin 60°) = 0.3+0.3\sqrt3\,i \approx
0.3+0.5196i$. Then $\psi_1+\psi_2 \approx 0.9+0.5196i$, and

$$
|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2 \approx 0.9^2+0.5196^2 \approx 0.81+0.27=1.08
$$

The quantum prediction ($1.08$) exceeds the classical one ($0.72$),
partial constructive interference, consistent with $\theta=\pi/3$ sitting
between the fully-constructive ($\theta=0$) and neutral ($\theta=\pi/2$)
cases from the table above.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming interference is just "small effects averaging out weirdly."**
It isn't a statistical artifact. It's the exact, reproducible,
single-particle-at-a-time behavior, and it can send a probability all the
way to zero at a spot where two positive contributions individually
placed particles.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing a not-yet-invented, more clever hidden variable could still
rescue the classical addition rule.** The contradiction shown above uses
only the *actually measured* probabilities and the classical addition
axiom. It doesn't depend on any specific guess about what the hidden
variable might be.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Classical probability requires $P = P_1+P_2$ for mutually exclusive
  alternatives, always, regardless of what the hidden alternative is.
- The measured double-slit pattern instead follows $P =
  \lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$, which can range from double the classical
  value to exactly zero depending on relative phase.
- This numerically contradicts the classical rule at specific, calculable
  points, ruling out any hidden-definite-path explanation, not merely
  disfavoring one.
- Which-path measurement destroys the interference and restores the
  classical rule, a genuine physical effect, not a limitation of
  detectors.
