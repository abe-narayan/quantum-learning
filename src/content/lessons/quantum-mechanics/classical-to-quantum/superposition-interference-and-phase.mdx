## Motivation

Early in this course, interference appeared as a fact about two physical
paths to a screen, and the slits did most of the explanatory work. That
framing has a problem: interference also shows up in systems with no
paths at all, including a single qubit sitting still in a laboratory.

The tools now exist to say what interference is without mentioning
geometry. The short version is that it is a mismatch between how a state
is written and which question you ask of it, and everything else in this
lesson is the consequence of taking that seriously.

## Intuition

A superposition $|\psi\rangle = c_a|a\rangle+c_b|b\rangle$ has a relative
phase between $c_a$ and $c_b$. Measure in the $\{|a\rangle,|b\rangle\}$
basis itself, and that phase is invisible: $P(a)=|c_a|^2$ does not care
what phase $c_a$ carries. Measure in a *different* basis, and the same
phase reshapes the outcome probabilities continuously, from certainty to
impossibility and back. Interference is what happens when you ask a
question (a choice of measurement basis) that does not line up with the
way the state happens to be written.

## Mathematical Development

### The superposition principle

Postulate 1 says any normalized vector in the <Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term> is a valid
physical state. So if $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ are both valid states, any
normalized combination $|\psi\rangle=c_a|a\rangle+c_b|b\rangle$
($|c_a|^2+|c_b|^2=1$) is too. This is the entire content of the
**superposition principle**: nothing extra to postulate beyond the
vector-space structure already built.

### Measuring in the "natural" basis: no interference

If $\{|a\rangle,|b\rangle\}$ is itself an <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal eigenbasis</Term> of the
observable being measured, the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> gives $P(a)=|c_a|^2$,
$P(b)=|c_b|^2$ directly, no cross terms, no dependence on the relative
phase between $c_a$ and $c_b$ at all.

### Measuring in a different basis: interference appears

Take the concrete case $|\psi\rangle =
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\right)$, and
measure in the $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$ basis instead of
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$. Using
$|+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$:

$$
\langle+|\psi\rangle = \frac12\left(\langle0|+\langle1|\right)\left(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle\right) = \frac12\left(1+e^{i\varphi}\right)
$$

$$
P(+) = |\langle+|\psi\rangle|^2 = \frac14\left|1+e^{i\varphi}\right|^2 = \frac14\left(1+e^{i\varphi}\right)\left(1+e^{-i\varphi}\right) = \frac14\left(2+e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}\right) = \frac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}
$$

(using $e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}=2\cos\varphi$, from Euler's formula).
That is an interference pattern in $\varphi$: $P(+)$ runs from $1$ at
$\varphi=0$ (fully constructive) down to $0$ at $\varphi=\pi$ (fully
destructive), which is the double-slit fringe shape, obtained here from a
projective measurement in a rotated basis with no paths, no slits, and no
screen anywhere in the derivation. **The <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative phase</Term> that was invisible
in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ basis is fully visible in this one.**

One caution before moving on: $\cos\varphi$ is an even function, so
$P(+)$ alone cannot tell $\varphi$ from $-\varphi$. Recovering the phase
completely takes a second measurement in a third basis, the eigenbasis of
$Y$, whose probability comes out proportional to $1+\sin\varphi$ instead.
One basis reveals that a phase exists; two are needed to read it off.
That is the first step of state tomography, which the Quantum Computing
pillar builds out in full.

### The same formula, twice

Compare $P(+)=\tfrac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}$ to the Time Evolution lesson's
result, $P(\text{still }|+\rangle\text{ at time }t) = \cos^2(\omega
t/2)$. The half-angle identity $\cos^2(\theta/2) =
\tfrac{1+\cos\theta}{2}$ shows these are the *same* formula, with
$\varphi \leftrightarrow \omega t$. Precession in time and interference
across a change of measurement basis are not two phenomena that resemble
each other; they are one statement, $\tfrac{1+\cos(\text{phase})}{2}$,
reached once by letting a phase accumulate and once by choosing where to
look. The only difference is what supplied the phase.

## Physical Interpretation

This reframes what "which-path information destroys interference" (from
the earlier lesson) really means: detecting which path a particle took
is, mathematically, a measurement in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$-type
"path basis," and that is the one basis in which the relative phase
contributes nothing. Declining to measure the path, and instead measuring
in a basis like $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$ that mixes the two together, is
what lets the phase reach the probabilities. Nothing mysterious is added
by the act of observation: it is the ordinary Born rule, evaluated in two
different bases, and the difference between the answers was already
sitting in the algebra.

## Worked Example

**For $\varphi=2\pi/3$, find $P(+)$ using the derived formula.**

$$
P(+) = \frac{1+\cos(2\pi/3)}{2} = \frac{1+(-\tfrac12)}{2} = \frac{1/2}{2}=\frac14
$$

Check against the direct calculation:
$\langle+|\psi\rangle=\tfrac12(1+e^{i2\pi/3})$, and
$e^{i2\pi/3}=-\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$, so
$1+e^{i2\pi/3}=\tfrac12+\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$, with squared magnitude
$\tfrac14+\tfrac34=1$; then $P(+)=\tfrac14\cdot1=\tfrac14$ ✓, matching.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming relative phase always shows up in probabilities, in every
basis.** It's invisible in the superposition's own basis by construction,
and only appears once you measure in a basis that mixes the original
terms together.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating interference and time evolution as unrelated topics that
happen to share notation.** The half-angle identity turns one formula
into the other with nothing left over. What differs is only where the
phase came from: a choice of basis in one case, elapsed time in the
other.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Superposition is a direct consequence of Postulate 1's vector-space
  structure, nothing extra to add.
- Measuring in a superposition's own basis hides relative phase
  completely; measuring in a different basis reveals it, via
  $P=\tfrac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}$, derived directly from the Born rule.
- The half-angle identity turns that formula into the Time Evolution
  lesson's precession probability: interference across bases and
  precession in time are one piece of mathematics with two sources of
  phase.
- A single cross-basis measurement shows that a phase is present but
  cannot resolve its sign; a second basis is needed to determine it.
