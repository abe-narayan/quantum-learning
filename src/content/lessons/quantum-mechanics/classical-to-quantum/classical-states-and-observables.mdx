## The picture that held for centuries

That confidence, that a system has a definite, complete state at every
instant, and every physical quantity is just some function of that
state, is the classical picture. It held up for centuries because it
matches everyday experience: a thrown ball lands where the numbers say
it will, and when it doesn't, you look for the wind you forgot to
measure rather than doubting that the ball had a trajectory.

Two commitments are hiding in that sentence, and both will fail later.
First, the state is complete: nothing about the ball is left over once
you have listed $x$ and $p$. Second, the state exists whether or not
anyone looks. Measuring is reading off a value that was already sitting
there. Neither commitment survives contact with an electron, so it helps
to see them stated plainly while they still feel obvious.

## Phase space, functions, and where probability enters

### Phase space

A classical particle moving in one dimension has a **state** completely
specified by two real numbers: its position $x$ and its momentum $p$. The
set of all possible $(x,p)$ pairs is called **phase space**. A system with
more degrees of freedom (three-dimensional motion, multiple particles)
just needs more coordinates. Phase space grows, but the idea doesn't
change: a state is a point, full stop.

### Observables are functions of the state

A classical **observable**, anything you could measure, like energy,
momentum, or angular momentum, is an ordinary real-valued function of the
phase-space point:

$$
O = O(x, p)
$$

For a particle of mass $m$ under a potential $V(x)$, momentum is simply
$p$ itself, and the total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential
parts:

$$
E(x,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x)
$$

Given the state $(x,p)$, every observable's value is completely
determined. There's no step where "measuring" $E$ could give an
ambiguous or probabilistic answer. This is the feature quantum mechanics
removes, and the word "observable" survives the transition while its
meaning changes underneath: not a function you plug numbers into, but an
operator acting on a vector, as the Mathematical Foundations course
already built. Keep both readings of the word in view; confusing them is
the source of most early misreadings of the quantum formalism.

### Determinism

Classical mechanics (via Newton's laws, or equivalently Hamilton's
equations, which this course won't need in detail) evolves a state
$(x(t), p(t))$ forward in time in a completely determined way: the same
initial state always produces the same future, and in principle, knowing
the present state exactly lets you predict any future state exactly.

### Where classical probability actually comes from

Classical physics does use probability constantly: statistical
mechanics, thermodynamics, coin flips, dice. But in every classical case,
the probability describes **ignorance**, not indeterminacy. A coin's
outcome looks random only because you don't know (or didn't measure
precisely enough to compute) its exact initial spin, air resistance, and
landing conditions; a classical probability distribution $\rho(x,p)$ over
phase space represents *your* uncertainty about which point the system
occupies, not any uncertainty the system itself has. In principle, even
if not in practice, a classical system always has one exact, definite
state, whether or not you know what it is. This is called **epistemic**
probability, from the Greek for knowledge.

## Accurate, right up until it isn't

The classical picture, definite states, observables as their functions,
probability as ignorance, is an extraordinarily accurate description of
ordinary objects, not a wrong one. This course's job is to locate where
and why it stops being accurate, and the answer is not "classical
mechanics needs a small correction." As the next lesson shows, some
experimentally-measured probabilities cannot be explained by *any*
underlying definite state, however unknown. That rules out the entire
strategy of patching the classical picture with hidden variables: what is
needed is a different kind of probability, not better bookkeeping for the
classical kind.

## The energy of one oscillator at one instant

**A classical harmonic oscillator has mass $m=1$, spring constant $k=4$,
and is in the state $x=1$, $p=2$. Find its total energy.**

$$
E(x,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{2^2}{2(1)} + \frac{1}{2}(4)(1)^2 = 2 + 2 = 4
$$

This value is exact and unambiguous. There is nothing probabilistic
about evaluating a function at a known point.

## Ignorance is not the only kind of probability

<Callout type="note">
**Treating classical probability and quantum probability as the same kind
of thing, just applied to smaller objects.** Classical probability always
traces back to not knowing the exact state; the next lesson shows quantum
probability can't be explained that way, even in principle.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming "observable" means the same thing in both theories.** Classical
observables are functions of a state; quantum observables, built
carefully across the Mathematical Foundations course, are <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term>
operators. These are different *kinds* of mathematical object, not the
same kind viewed at different scales.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A classical particle of mass $2$ has state $x=3, p=4$, moving under
   $V(x) = x^2$. Find its total energy. Then find a *different* state
   $(x', p')$ with that same energy, and describe the set of all states
   sharing it as a curve in phase space.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $E = 13$.

   Evaluate the function at the point, exactly as the lesson's definition
   says to:

   $$
   E(x,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x) = \frac{4^2}{2(2)} + 3^2 = \frac{16}{4} + 9 = 4 + 9 = 13
   $$

   The two commonest slips are both in the kinetic term. Writing $p^2/2m$
   as $(p/2m)^2$ gives $1$ instead of $4$, and forgetting that $m=2$ sits
   in the denominator gives $8$. The potential term is safer here only
   because $V(x)=x^2$ happens to look like a kinetic energy; it is not
   one, and nothing in this problem divides it by anything.

   For a second state with the same energy, pick any $x'$ you like and
   solve for $p'$. Taking $x'=1$ gives $V=1$, so $p'^2/4 = 12$ and
   $p' = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt3 \approx 6.93$. The state
   $(1, 4\sqrt3)$ is a completely different point of phase space with the
   same energy as $(3,4)$.

   The set of all of them is the level curve $E(x,p)=13$:

   $$
   \frac{p^2}{4} + x^2 = 13 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{x^2}{13} + \frac{p^2}{52} = 1
   $$

   an ellipse centered on the origin with semi-axis $\sqrt{13}\approx3.61$
   along $x$ and $\sqrt{52}\approx7.21$ along $p$. That is the same kind of
   object as the brand-colored contour in the phase-space panel above,
   drawn there for a different oscillator.

   The part worth taking away is the direction of the logic. The state
   determines the energy uniquely, but the energy does not determine the
   state: a whole one-dimensional curve of states shares the value $13$.
   Knowing $E$ alone therefore leaves you ignorant of *which* point the
   system occupies, and that ignorance is the only kind of uncertainty
   classical mechanics has. It is a statement about your information, not
   about the particle, which is sitting at one definite point on that
   ellipse the entire time.

   </details>

2. A weather forecaster says "70% chance of rain tomorrow." Is this
   epistemic probability, in the classical sense? Justify your answer.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. It is epistemic through and through.

   Apply the lesson's test: does the number describe ignorance about one
   underlying definite fact, or genuine indeterminacy in the system? The
   atmosphere is an enormous classical system with a definite state, and
   tomorrow either rain falls or it does not. The forecaster cannot
   measure every parcel of air, and the equations amplify small errors
   fast, so the $70\%$ records how much of the possible-initial-conditions
   space leads to rain given what was actually measured. Two forecasters
   with different data will quote different numbers for the same
   tomorrow, which is the signature of a probability that lives in the
   description rather than in the weather.

   The seductive wrong answer is "no, because weather is chaotic, so it is
   genuinely random." Chaos is exactly the wrong reason. A chaotic system
   is still deterministic: the same initial state produces the same future
   every time. What chaos does is make the ignorance grow explosively, so
   a tiny gap in your knowledge becomes a large gap in your prediction.
   That makes the epistemic probability *large*, not non-epistemic.

   A second wrong turn is to object that $70\%$ is really a long-run
   frequency over many similar past days, not anybody's ignorance. That
   is a claim about how the number was *estimated*, not about what it
   describes. Counting past analogues is one method of quantifying
   ignorance about which state today's atmosphere is in, and the
   probability still refers to a single definite outcome that has not
   been observed yet.

   One honest caveat, since the next lesson turns on precisely this. The
   air molecules are quantum systems, so at the bottom the world is not
   epistemic. But nothing in the forecast needs that: no amplitude has to
   be added before squaring anywhere in meteorology, and the classical
   addition rule for "rain" and "no rain" holds. That is the contrast the
   next lesson breaks, and it breaks it with an experiment, not with an
   appeal to complexity.

   </details>

3. Explain why, classically, knowing a system's exact state at one instant
   is (in principle) enough to know its state at every future instant.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the classical equations of motion are first-order in time in
   the phase-space variables, and a state is precisely the data those
   equations need to start.

   Hamilton's equations, the form the lesson mentions without needing in
   detail, say that the rate of change of the state is a function of the
   state:

   $$
   \dot x = \frac{\partial E}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot p = -\frac{\partial E}{\partial x}
   $$

   Read the right-hand sides. They contain $x$ and $p$ and nothing else.
   So the point $(x,p)$ at one instant fixes the direction the point is
   moving at that instant, which fixes where it is a moment later, which
   fixes the direction there, and so on. Nothing outside the state is ever
   consulted, which is exactly what "the state is complete" meant in the
   lesson's first commitment.

   Two conditions are doing real work here, and dropping either one breaks
   the claim.

   The first is that the state is the *full* phase-space point. Position
   alone will not do it: two particles at $x=3$ with momenta $+4$ and
   $-4$ have identical positions and opposite futures. This is why the
   classical state has two numbers per degree of freedom rather than one.

   The second is regularity of the force law. Uniqueness of the solution
   is a theorem about differential equations, and it needs the right-hand
   sides to be well enough behaved (Lipschitz continuity is the usual
   sufficient condition). Contrived potentials exist for which two
   different trajectories leave the same point, and determinism genuinely
   fails for them. The claim is a theorem with a hypothesis, not a slogan.

   Finally, note what "in principle" is protecting. Real measurements have
   finite precision, and in a chaotic system that error grows fast enough
   that long-range prediction is impossible in practice. That is a
   limitation on knowledge, not on the trajectory. The particle still has
   one, and that is the commitment the next lesson attacks: not that
   trajectories are hard to compute, but that experiment rules out any
   theory in which a definite one exists at all.

   </details>

## Summary

- A classical state is a point $(x,p)$ in phase space; observables are
  functions $O(x,p)$ of that point.
- Classical evolution is deterministic: the same state always produces the
  same future.
- Classical probability is epistemic. It reflects ignorance about which
  definite state a system occupies, never genuine indeterminacy in the
  system itself.
