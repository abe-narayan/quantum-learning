## Motivation

Eleven lessons of linear algebra built a powerful mathematical toolkit
without saying much about physics at all. Before applying that toolkit,
it's worth being precise about what it's replacing: what "state" and
"observable" meant *before* quantum mechanics, so the differences that are
coming aren't differences of degree, but differences of kind.

## Intuition

Drop a ball. At any instant, everything there is to know about it, where
it is, how fast it's moving, is two numbers: position and momentum.
Nothing about the ball is hidden, approximate, or waiting to be resolved.
That confidence, that a system has a definite, complete state at every
instant, and every physical quantity is just some function of that
state, is the classical picture, and it held up for centuries because it
matches everyday experience almost perfectly.

## Mathematical Development

### Phase space

A classical particle moving in one dimension has a **state** completely
specified by two real numbers: its position $x$ and its momentum $p$. The
set of all possible $(x,p)$ pairs is called **phase space**. A system with
more degrees of freedom (three-dimensional motion, multiple particles)
just needs more coordinates. Phase space grows, but the idea doesn't
change: a state is a point, full stop.

### Observables are functions of the state

A classical **observable**, anything you could measure, like energy,
momentum, or angular momentum, is an ordinary real-valued function of the
phase-space point:

$$
O = O(x, p)
$$

For a particle of mass $m$ under a potential $V(x)$, momentum is simply
$p$ itself, and the total energy is the sum of kinetic and potential
parts:

$$
E(x,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(x)
$$

Given the state $(x,p)$, every observable's value is completely
determined. There's no step where "measuring" $E$ could give an
ambiguous or probabilistic answer. This is the feature quantum mechanics
is going to remove, and it's worth naming precisely, because "observable"
is going to mean something structurally different very soon: not a
function you plug numbers into, but an operator acting on a vector, as
the Mathematical Foundations course already built.

### Determinism

Classical mechanics (via Newton's laws, or equivalently Hamilton's
equations, which this course won't need in detail) evolves a state
$(x(t), p(t))$ forward in time in a completely determined way: the same
initial state always produces the same future, and in principle, knowing
the present state exactly lets you predict any future state exactly.

### Where classical probability actually comes from

Classical physics does use probability constantly: statistical
mechanics, thermodynamics, coin flips, dice. But in every classical case,
the probability describes **ignorance**, not indeterminacy. A coin's
outcome looks random only because you don't know (or didn't measure
precisely enough to compute) its exact initial spin, air resistance, and
landing conditions; a classical probability distribution $\rho(x,p)$ over
phase space represents *your* uncertainty about which point the system
occupies, not any uncertainty the system itself has. In principle, even
if not in practice, a classical system always has one exact, definite
state, whether or not you know what it is. This is called **epistemic**
probability, from the Greek for knowledge.

## Physical Interpretation

The classical picture, definite states, observables as their functions,
probability as ignorance, isn't wrong. It's an extraordinarily accurate
description of ordinary objects. The entire point of this course is to
locate precisely where and why it stops being accurate, and the answer is
not "classical mechanics needs a small correction." As the next lesson
shows, some experimentally-measured probabilities cannot be explained by
*any* underlying definite state, however unknown. A fundamentally
different kind of probability is required, not just better bookkeeping
for the classical kind.

## Worked Example

**A classical harmonic oscillator has mass $m=1$, spring constant $k=4$,
and is in the state $x=1$, $p=2$. Find its total energy.**

$$
E(x,p) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{2^2}{2(1)} + \frac{1}{2}(4)(1)^2 = 2 + 2 = 4
$$

This value is exact and unambiguous. There is nothing probabilistic
about evaluating a function at a known point.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating classical probability and quantum probability as the same kind
of thing, just applied to smaller objects.** Classical probability always
traces back to not knowing the exact state; the next lesson shows quantum
probability can't be explained that way, even in principle.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming "observable" means the same thing in both theories.** Classical
observables are functions of a state; quantum observables, built
carefully across the Mathematical Foundations course, are Hermitian
operators. These are different *kinds* of mathematical object, not the
same kind viewed at different scales.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A classical state is a point $(x,p)$ in phase space; observables are
  functions $O(x,p)$ of that point.
- Classical evolution is deterministic: the same state always produces the
  same future.
- Classical probability is epistemic. It reflects ignorance about which
  definite state a system occupies, never genuine indeterminacy in the
  system itself.
