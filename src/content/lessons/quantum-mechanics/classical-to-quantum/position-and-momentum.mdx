## Motivation

Narrow the slit and, past a certain width, the beam stops obeying you.
Instead of a tighter line, it fans out into the pattern below, wider than
the slit that made it. Confining the beam's position at the slit widens
its spread in direction of travel, which is to say its momentum.

Classical wave optics predicts this too, so on its own the photograph
proves nothing about quantum mechanics. What quantum mechanics adds is
that the same trade-off holds for particles with mass, holds for pairs of
observables that have nothing to do with waves, and comes with an exact
number attached rather than an order-of-magnitude estimate.

One thing does change. Every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> so far has lived on a
finite-dimensional <Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term>, where a handful of numbers pin down a
state. Position takes a continuum of values, so the space is now
infinite-dimensional. This lesson takes stock of what that breaks, which
turns out to be less than expected: the full continuous machinery belongs
to the Wave Mechanics course, and none of it is needed for the result
below.

## Intuition

None of the four postulates needs amending for a continuous observable.
States are still vectors, position and momentum are still Hermitian
operators, measurement still runs through the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term>. What changes is
bookkeeping: the *label* on the basis states becomes a continuous
variable $x$ rather than a discrete index $i$, so sums over outcomes turn
into integrals and a few normalization statements need care.

Because the postulates survive intact, so does every theorem proved from
them. Heisenberg's <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term> is about to arrive as one line
of substitution rather than as a new physical idea.

## Mathematical Development

### Position and momentum as operators

A particle's state is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in an infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space; position eigenstates $|x\rangle$ exist for every real
number $x$ (with subtleties around normalization that a fully rigorous
treatment handles carefully, deferred to Wave Mechanics). Position and
momentum are both Hermitian operators, $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, and satisfy
the **canonical commutation relation**:

$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar
$$

<Callout type="note">
This relation is the quantum version of a classical structure (the
Poisson bracket $\{x,p\}=1$) turned into a commutator, a standard and
motivated step, but not one this lesson derives from first principles;
doing so rigorously needs the position-space representation ($\hat p$
acting as $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ on wavefunctions) built in the Wave Mechanics
course. Here, $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ is taken as given, and its
*consequences*, which this course already has every tool needed to
derive, are worked out below.
</Callout>

### The Heisenberg uncertainty principle, derived

The Expectation Values and Uncertainty lesson proved, for *any* two
Hermitian observables $A,B$:

$$
\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|
$$

Substitute $A=\hat x$, $B=\hat p$, and $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ directly:

$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \tfrac12|\langle i\hbar\rangle| = \tfrac12\hbar|\langle1\rangle| = \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

(using $\langle i\hbar\rangle = i\hbar\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = i\hbar$
for a normalized state, so $|\langle i\hbar\rangle|=\hbar$). That is the
**Heisenberg uncertainty principle**, $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$.

Notice how little work the last step did. Unlike every other uncertainty
bound this course has computed, the right-hand side here does not depend
on the state at all: $[\hat x,\hat p]$ is a number times the identity, so
$\langle[\hat x,\hat p]\rangle$ comes out the same in every state. Every
other pair, including $X$ and $Y$ from the earlier lesson, has a bound
that weakens or vanishes for particular states. Position and momentum
never let up.

### Connecting to the harmonic oscillator

The harmonic oscillator's position and momentum operators can be written
directly in terms of the ladder operators from the last lesson:

$$
\hat x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,(a+a^\dagger), \qquad \hat p = i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}\,(a^\dagger-a)
$$

This deserves checking rather than taking on faith: compute $[\hat
x,\hat p]$ using only $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ (the harmonic oscillator's
defining relation from the last lesson) and bilinearity of the
commutator.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute the ladder-operator definitions of x̂ and p̂ and expand the commutator bilinearly into its four pieces.">
$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}\,[a+a^\dagger,\ a^\dagger-a] = i\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}\left([a,a^\dagger]-[a,a]+[a^\dagger,a^\dagger]-[a^\dagger,a]\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The middle two commutators vanish (any operator commutes with itself), leaving [a,a†]−[a†,a] = 1−(−1) = 2.">
$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}\cdot2 = i\hbar \checkmark
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

So $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ and $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ are the same statement
written in two variable sets. The ladder algebra of the last lesson was
never an oscillator-specific trick; it was the canonical commutation
relation in a change of coordinates, which is why ladder operators keep
reappearing in systems that are not oscillators at all.

## Physical Interpretation

Momentum has a second job beyond being an observable. Compare the
Time Evolution lesson's $U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$, which shifts a state
forward in time, with

$$
T(a) = e^{-i\hat p a/\hbar}, \qquad T(a)|x\rangle = |x+a\rangle
$$

which shifts it through space. The two have the same form, and in each
case the generator is the observable conjugate to the thing being
shifted: energy for time, momentum for position. That pairing is the
source of the commutation relation rather than a consequence of it, and
Wave Mechanics develops the point properly.

There is a historical footnote worth keeping. $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$
came from Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and the Schrödinger equation came
from wave mechanics; the two were built independently in 1925 and 1926,
looked nothing alike, and were only afterwards shown to be one theory.
This course reached both from the same four postulates, which is why the
equivalence needed no argument here.

## Worked Example

**Using $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, if a particle's position is known
to $\Delta x = 10^{-10}$ m (roughly an atomic radius), what is the
minimum possible $\Delta p$?**

$$
\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x} = \frac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{2\times10^{-10}} \approx 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg·m/s}
$$

Small in everyday units, but for an electron (mass $\approx
9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg) this corresponds to a minimum velocity uncertainty
of roughly $\Delta p/m \approx 5.8\times10^5$ m/s. That is about $0.2\%$
of the speed of light, for an electron merely known to sit inside an
atom.

Run the same numbers for a $1$ gram bead localized to a micron and the
velocity uncertainty lands near $10^{-25}$ m/s, which no experiment will
ever resolve. The bound applies to both; only the mass in the denominator
decides whether anyone notices.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\hat p$ as an ordinary number rather than an operator.** Every
manipulation above used $[\hat x,\hat p]$ as an operator commutator, the
same kind of object as $[X,Y]$ for the Pauli matrices. Only the dimension
of the space changed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Re-deriving the uncertainty bound from scratch instead of reusing the
general relation.** $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ is a single substitution
into the Expectation Values lesson's already-proven inequality, there's
no need for a separate argument specific to position and momentum.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ is the canonical commutation relation; its full
  derivation needs the continuous position representation built in Wave
  Mechanics, but its *consequences* don't.
- Substituting directly into the general uncertainty relation gives
  $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, the famous bound, as a special case of
  a theorem already proven.
- $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ follows directly from $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ once
  $\hat x,\hat p$ are written in terms of the harmonic oscillator's ladder
  operators: one algebra, two familiar faces.
