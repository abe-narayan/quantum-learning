## Past a certain width, the beam stops obeying

Narrow the slit and, past a certain width, the beam stops obeying you.
Instead of a tighter line, it fans out into the pattern below, wider than
the slit that made it. Confining the beam's position at the slit widens
its spread in direction of travel, which is to say its momentum.

Classical wave optics predicts this too, so on its own the photograph
proves nothing about quantum mechanics. What quantum mechanics adds is
that the same trade-off holds for particles with mass, holds for pairs of
observables that have nothing to do with waves, and comes with an exact
number attached rather than an order-of-magnitude estimate.

One thing does change. Every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> so far has lived on a
finite-dimensional <Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term>, where a handful of numbers pin down a
state. Position takes a continuum of values, so the space is now
infinite-dimensional. This lesson takes stock of what that breaks, which
turns out to be less than expected: the full continuous machinery belongs
to the Wave Mechanics course, and none of it is needed for the result
below.

## Four postulates, no amendments needed

None of the four postulates needs amending for a continuous observable.
States are still vectors, position and momentum are still Hermitian
operators, measurement still runs through the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term>. What changes is
bookkeeping: the *label* on the basis states becomes a continuous
variable $x$ rather than a discrete index $i$, so sums over outcomes turn
into integrals and a few normalization statements need care.

Because the postulates survive intact, so does every theorem proved from
them. Heisenberg's <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term> is about to arrive as one line
of substitution rather than as a new physical idea.

## One substitution into a theorem already proved

### Position and momentum as operators

A particle's state is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in an infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space; position eigenstates $|x\rangle$ exist for every real
number $x$ (with subtleties around normalization that a fully rigorous
treatment handles carefully, deferred to Wave Mechanics). Position and
momentum are both Hermitian operators, $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, and satisfy
the **canonical commutation relation**:

$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar
$$

<Callout type="note">
This relation is the quantum version of a classical structure (the
Poisson bracket $\{x,p\}=1$) turned into a commutator, a standard and
motivated step, but not one this lesson derives from first principles;
doing so rigorously needs the position-space representation ($\hat p$
acting as $-i\hbar\,d/dx$ on wavefunctions) built in the Wave Mechanics
course. Here, $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ is taken as given, and its
*consequences*, which this course already has every tool needed to
derive, are worked out below.
</Callout>

### The Heisenberg uncertainty principle, derived

The Expectation Values and Uncertainty lesson proved, for *any* two
Hermitian observables $A,B$:

$$
\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|
$$

Substitute $A=\hat x$, $B=\hat p$, and $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ directly:

$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \tfrac12|\langle i\hbar\rangle| = \tfrac12\hbar|\langle1\rangle| = \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

(using $\langle i\hbar\rangle = i\hbar\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = i\hbar$
for a normalized state, so $|\langle i\hbar\rangle|=\hbar$). That is the
**Heisenberg uncertainty principle**, $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$.

Notice how little work the last step did. Unlike every other uncertainty
bound this course has computed, the right-hand side here does not depend
on the state at all: $[\hat x,\hat p]$ is a number times the identity, so
$\langle[\hat x,\hat p]\rangle$ comes out the same in every state. Every
other pair, including $X$ and $Y$ from the earlier lesson, has a bound
that weakens or vanishes for particular states. Position and momentum
never let up.

### Connecting to the harmonic oscillator

The harmonic oscillator's position and momentum operators can be written
directly in terms of the ladder operators from the last lesson:

$$
\hat x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,(a+a^\dagger), \qquad \hat p = i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}\,(a^\dagger-a)
$$

This deserves checking rather than taking on faith: compute $[\hat
x,\hat p]$ using only $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ (the harmonic oscillator's
defining relation from the last lesson) and bilinearity of the
commutator.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute the ladder-operator definitions of x̂ and p̂ and expand the commutator bilinearly into its four pieces.">
$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\sqrt{\tfrac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}\,[a+a^\dagger,\ a^\dagger-a] = i\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}\left([a,a^\dagger]-[a,a]+[a^\dagger,a^\dagger]-[a^\dagger,a]\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The middle two commutators vanish (any operator commutes with itself), leaving [a,a†]−[a†,a] = 1−(−1) = 2.">
$$
[\hat x,\hat p] = i\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}\cdot2 = i\hbar \checkmark
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

So $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ and $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ are the same statement
written in two variable sets. The ladder algebra of the last lesson was
never an oscillator-specific trick; it was the canonical commutation
relation in a change of coordinates, which is why ladder operators keep
reappearing in systems that are not oscillators at all.

## Momentum's second job: generating translations

Momentum has a second job beyond being an observable. Compare the
Time Evolution lesson's $U(t)=e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$, which shifts a state
forward in time, with

$$
T(a) = e^{-i\hat p a/\hbar}, \qquad T(a)|x\rangle = |x+a\rangle
$$

which shifts it through space. The two have the same form, and in each
case the generator is the observable conjugate to the thing being
shifted: energy for time, momentum for position. That pairing is the
source of the commutation relation rather than a consequence of it, and
Wave Mechanics develops the point properly.

There is a historical footnote worth keeping. $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$
came from Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and the Schrödinger equation came
from wave mechanics; the two were built independently in 1925 and 1926,
looked nothing alike, and were only afterwards shown to be one theory.
This course reached both from the same four postulates, which is why the
equivalence needed no argument here.

## The minimum momentum spread inside an atom

**Using $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, if a particle's position is known
to $\Delta x = 10^{-10}$ m (roughly an atomic radius), what is the
minimum possible $\Delta p$?**

$$
\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2\Delta x} = \frac{1.055\times10^{-34}}{2\times10^{-10}} \approx 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg·m/s}
$$

Small in everyday units, but for an electron (mass $\approx
9.1\times10^{-31}$ kg) this corresponds to a minimum velocity uncertainty
of roughly $\Delta p/m \approx 5.8\times10^5$ m/s. That is about $0.2\%$
of the speed of light, for an electron merely known to sit inside an
atom.

Run the same numbers for a $1$ gram bead localized to a micron and the
velocity uncertainty lands near $5\times10^{-26}$ m/s, which no experiment
will ever resolve. The bound applies to both; only the mass in the denominator
decides whether anyone notices.

## p-hat is an operator, not a number

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\hat p$ as an ordinary number rather than an operator.** Every
manipulation above used $[\hat x,\hat p]$ as an operator commutator, the
same kind of object as $[X,Y]$ for the Pauli matrices. Only the dimension
of the space changed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Re-deriving the uncertainty bound from scratch instead of reusing the
general relation.** $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ is a single substitution
into the Expectation Values lesson's already-proven inequality, there's
no need for a separate argument specific to position and momentum.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. What is $[\hat p,\hat x]$ (note the reversed order), given
   $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$? Then show the uncertainty bound derived above
   is completely unchanged by that reversal, and name the feature of the
   bound's statement that makes it so.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $[\hat p,\hat x] = -i\hbar$, and the bound is untouched: still
   $\hbar/2$.

   The first part is antisymmetry, which is not a rule to remember but a
   one-line consequence of the definition:

   $$
   [\hat p,\hat x] = \hat p\hat x - \hat x\hat p = -\left(\hat x\hat p - \hat p\hat x\right) = -[\hat x,\hat p] = -i\hbar
   $$

   Now substitute the reversed pair into the general relation with
   $A=\hat p$, $B=\hat x$:

   $$
   \Delta p\,\Delta x \ge \tfrac12\left|\langle[\hat p,\hat x]\rangle\right| = \tfrac12\left|-i\hbar\right| = \tfrac12\hbar = \frac{\hbar}{2}
   $$

   Identical to before. Two features of the statement are doing that work
   together. The right-hand side carries an **absolute value**, which
   discards the sign the reversal introduced, since $|-i\hbar| =
   |i\hbar| = \hbar$. And the left-hand side is a **product of two real
   non-negative numbers**, so $\Delta p\,\Delta x$ and $\Delta x\,\Delta
   p$ are the same number. Neither side can tell which operator was named
   first.

   That insensitivity is not a triviality; it is a consistency check the
   derivation had to pass. Nothing physical distinguishes "the uncertainty
   relation between position and momentum" from "the uncertainty relation
   between momentum and position," so a bound that changed under the
   swap would have been evidence of an error upstream. Trace where the
   absolute value came from and the reason appears: the derivation bounded
   $|\langle f|g\rangle|^2$, a squared magnitude, which was already
   sign-blind before the commutator was ever named.

   The slip worth flagging is dropping the minus sign and writing
   $[\hat p,\hat x] = i\hbar$ on the grounds that "the bound comes out the
   same anyway." The bound does, and the commutator does not. The sign is
   physical elsewhere: it fixes the *direction* the Bloch vector turns in
   the two-level analogue, and it is why $e^{-i\hat pa/\hbar}$ translates
   a state by $+a$ rather than $-a$ in the translation formula above.

   </details>

2. Using the general uncertainty relation, what is the minimum value of
   $\Delta x\,\Delta(2p)$, in terms of $\hbar$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\hbar$. Twice the familiar bound.

   Two routes reach it, and agreeing is the point.

   Through the commutator: $2\hat p$ is a Hermitian operator in its own
   right, and the commutator is linear in each slot, so
   $[\hat x, 2\hat p] = 2[\hat x,\hat p] = 2i\hbar$. Substituting into the
   general relation,

   $$
   \Delta x\,\Delta(2p) \ge \tfrac12\left|2i\hbar\right| = \hbar
   $$

   Through the standard deviation: scaling an observable by a constant
   $c$ scales its spread by $|c|$, since
   $\operatorname{Var}(cA) = c^2\operatorname{Var}(A)$. So
   $\Delta(2p) = 2\Delta p$, and

   $$
   \Delta x\,\Delta(2p) = 2\,\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge 2\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2} = \hbar
   $$

   Same answer, and the second route shows why it had to be: both sides of
   the inequality picked up the same factor of $2$, so the inequality
   itself did not move.

   That is the trap in this question. It looks as though rescaling
   momentum has made the trade-off twice as severe, and it has not. What
   changed is the unit in which the momentum spread is reported. Measure
   momentum in half-units and every momentum uncertainty doubles as a
   number while nothing about the state changes, so the bound doubles to
   keep pace. The physically meaningful statement is the one that survives
   rescaling: the *product* is bounded below, and the bound scales exactly
   as the observables do.

   A useful way to see that nothing was gained is to ask whether the
   minimum is still reachable. It is, and by the same states: any state
   saturating $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \hbar/2$ saturates $\Delta
   x\,\Delta(2p)=\hbar$. No state was made better or worse off.

   </details>

3. If a particle's momentum were known exactly ($\Delta p=0$), what does
   the uncertainty relation say about $\Delta x$? Is this physically
   achievable, even in principle?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The relation says $\Delta x$ would have to be unbounded, and no, it is
   not achievable, not even in principle.

   Put $\Delta p = 0$ into $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$ and the left
   side is $0$ for any finite $\Delta x$, so the inequality would read
   $0 \ge \hbar/2$, which is false. The bound is therefore violated by
   *every* finite position spread. Unlike the two-level examples earlier
   in this course, there is no escape by way of the right-hand side
   shrinking: $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ is a number times the identity, so
   $\tfrac12|\langle[\hat x,\hat p]\rangle| = \hbar/2$ in every state,
   with nothing to soften it.

   So no state of the theory has $\Delta p=0$. That is worth stating as a
   fact about the operator rather than about apparatus: a state with
   $\Delta p = 0$ would be an eigenvector of $\hat p$ living in the
   Hilbert space, and $\hat p$ on the line has no such eigenvector. The
   would-be candidate is the plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$, which has
   constant magnitude everywhere and so cannot be normalized, meaning it
   is not a state at all. It remains an indispensable *idealization*,
   used the way a point mass is used, and the Wave Mechanics course builds
   the wave-packet machinery that makes it rigorous.

   The wrong turn to name explicitly is arguing that $\Delta p=0$ is fine
   provided $\Delta x=\infty$, on the grounds that $0\times\infty$ can be
   anything. That is not an argument, it is a refusal to evaluate.
   $\Delta x$ is defined as the square root of
   $\langle x^2\rangle-\langle x\rangle^2$ computed on a normalized
   wavefunction, and $\infty$ is not a value it takes for any such
   wavefunction with $\Delta p = 0$, because there is no such wavefunction
   to begin with. The right conclusion is not that the product is
   indeterminate; it is that the premise has no state satisfying it.

   The physically honest version of the idea does survive. Momentum can be
   made as sharp as you like, at the cost of a position spread growing
   like $\hbar/(2\Delta p)$, without limit and without ever reaching the
   endpoint. Approaching an idealization arbitrarily closely is a
   different thing from occupying it.

   </details>

4. Verify, by substituting the ladder-operator definitions of $\hat x$
   directly, that $\hat x$ is Hermitian (recall $a^\dagger$ is the
   adjoint of $a$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\hat x^\dagger = \hat x$, and the check is three steps.

   Start from the definition given above, and take the adjoint. The
   adjoint is conjugate-linear on scalars and reverses products, and here
   the prefactor $\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}$ is a real positive number, so it
   passes through unchanged:

   $$
   \hat x^\dagger = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(a+a^\dagger\right)^\dagger = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(a^\dagger + a^{\dagger\dagger}\right) = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(a^\dagger + a\right) = \hat x
   $$

   using $(A+B)^\dagger = A^\dagger+B^\dagger$ and $a^{\dagger\dagger}=a$,
   the fact that taking the adjoint twice returns the original operator.
   Addition commutes, so $a^\dagger + a$ is the same operator as
   $a+a^\dagger$, and the check closes.

   The structural point is that $a$ and $a^\dagger$ are individually *not*
   Hermitian, which is exactly why they can raise and lower: a Hermitian
   operator's eigenvalues are real and it cannot shift the spectrum of $N$
   the way these do. Hermiticity is recovered by pairing each with its own
   adjoint. The sum $a+a^\dagger$ is Hermitian, and so is
   $i(a^\dagger-a)$, and those two combinations are the physically
   meaningful ones.

   That second combination is where the instructive error lives. Run the
   same computation on the *difference* without the $i$:

   $$
   \left(a^\dagger - a\right)^\dagger = a - a^\dagger = -\left(a^\dagger - a\right)
   $$

   which is anti-Hermitian, not Hermitian. Multiplying by $i$ fixes it,
   because the adjoint conjugates the scalar and the two sign flips cancel:

   $$
   \left[i\left(a^\dagger-a\right)\right]^\dagger = -i\left(a-a^\dagger\right) = i\left(a^\dagger-a\right)
   $$

   So the $i$ sitting in front of $\hat p$ in the definition above is not
   a convention or a leftover from the algebra. It is what makes momentum
   an observable at all, and dropping it produces an operator whose
   eigenvalues are imaginary and which Postulate 2 refuses.

   </details>

## Summary

- $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ is the canonical commutation relation; its full
  derivation needs the continuous position representation built in Wave
  Mechanics, but its *consequences* don't.
- Substituting directly into the general uncertainty relation gives
  $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$, the famous bound, as a special case of
  a theorem already proven.
- $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ follows directly from $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ once
  $\hat x,\hat p$ are written in terms of the harmonic oscillator's ladder
  operators: one algebra, two familiar faces.
