<LessonHook eyebrow="One note, with nowhere to leak">
A superposition of energy eigenstates precesses: its measurement statistics visibly change over time. A state that starts as a single energy eigenstate never does, and the reason is worth deriving rather than assuming.
</LessonHook>

## The complementary half of the last result

The last lesson's worked example showed a superposition's statistics
precessing in real time. This lesson proves the complementary fact: a
state that begins in a single energy eigenstate keeps identical
measurement statistics forever. Both results come out of the same
formula, $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$, and comparing them is what reveals which
feature of a state actually drives the motion. It turns out not to be
energy, but energy *differences*.

## A single note against a chord

If a state starts out as one exact "note" of the Hamiltonian (a single
energy eigenstate), time evolution can only ever produce that same note,
just shifted in phase, since there's no other eigenstate around for it to
leak into. A chord (superposition of several notes) is different: each
note's phase advances at its own rate, and the *relative* phase between
notes, which is what determines the chord's character, does change.

## One phase, and every probability frozen

### Energy eigenstates evolve by a single phase

Let $|n\rangle$ satisfy $H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$. The evolution
operator is defined by its <Term id="taylor-series">Taylor series</Term>, the same way $e^{i\theta}$ was
built in the Complex Numbers lesson:

$$
U(t)|n\rangle = e^{-iHt/\hbar}|n\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{-iHt}{\hbar}\right)^k|n\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{-iE_nt}{\hbar}\right)^k|n\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle
$$

(each power of $H$ acting on $|n\rangle$ just returns $E_n^k|n\rangle$,
since $|n\rangle$ is an eigenvector of $H$, so the whole series collapses
back into a plain number, $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, times $|n\rangle$). So:

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle
$$

The state at time $t$ is the *same* vector $|n\rangle$, multiplied by a
single overall phase factor.

### Every measurement probability is time-independent

By Postulate 1, an overall phase factor doesn't change the physical
state at all: $|n\rangle$ and $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$ represent the
identical physical situation for every value of $t$. Concretely, for
*any* <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $B$ with eigenbasis $\{|e_i\rangle\}$, the Born-rule
probability is

$$
P(\lambda_i, t) = |\langle e_i|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = |e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|^2\,|\langle e_i|n\rangle|^2 = |\langle e_i|n\rangle|^2
$$

using $|e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|=1$ (Complex Numbers lesson), completely
independent of $t$. This is why $|n\rangle$ is called a **stationary
state**: not because the state *vector* stops changing (it does not; it
accrues phase continuously) but because every measurable prediction built
from it is constant in time.

On the Bloch sphere, "accrues phase but stays put" and "actually
precesses" are a difference you can watch rather than only derive:

### What a superposition does instead

The eigenstate result also settles the general case, because $U(t)$ acts
on each eigenstate independently. Take $|\psi(0)\rangle = c_1|1\rangle +
c_2|2\rangle$ with $H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$:

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = c_1 e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}|1\rangle + c_2 e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}|2\rangle
$$

Pull the first phase out in front of the whole expression:

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\left(c_1|1\rangle + c_2 e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar}|2\rangle\right)
$$

The prefactor is a global phase and drops out of every prediction. What
survives is the *relative* phase between the two components, turning at
angular frequency $\omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar$. So a superposition is
non-stationary if and only if it spans two distinct energies, and the
speed of its oscillation is fixed by the gap between them, never by the
energies themselves. Shifting the zero of energy by adding a constant to
$H$ changes $E_1$ and $E_2$ equally, leaves $\omega_{21}$ alone, and
therefore changes nothing observable, which is the reason a Hamiltonian's
overall offset is always physically irrelevant.

That frequency has a name outside this course: $\hbar\omega_{21} =
E_2-E_1$ is the Bohr condition, and it is why an atom dropping between
two levels emits light at one sharp frequency rather than a smear.

### Energy is conserved, for any state

Stationarity is about all observables in one special state. Here is the
complementary statement: one special observable, $\langle H\rangle$, is
conserved in *every* state. Since
$U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is built purely out of $H$ (as a power series in
$H$), it commutes with $H$: $U(t)H = HU(t)$, so $U^\dagger(t)HU(t)=H$
exactly (using $U^\dagger(t)=U(-t)$, easily checked from the series).
Then:

$$
\langle H\rangle_t = \langle\psi(t)|H|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)HU(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|H|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle H\rangle_0
$$

The expectation value of total energy never changes under Schrödinger
evolution. This is the quantum form of energy conservation, and the
argument used only that $U(t)$ is built from $H$, so it covers every
state rather than the eigenstates alone.

## Why chemists call an orbital stationary

"Stationary state" is standard language throughout chemistry and atomic
physics: an electron in a definite energy eigenstate of an atom (an
"orbital") has a probability cloud that does not move, even though the
underlying state vector never stops accruing phase. This is why textbook
orbital pictures can be drawn as still images at all.

A superposition of two orbitals is a different object. Its charge
distribution sloshes back and forth at $\omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar$, and
an oscillating charge distribution radiates. That is the mechanism behind
atomic transitions and Rabi oscillations alike, and it is why an atom
sitting in a single eigenstate is stable: with nothing oscillating, there
is nothing to radiate.

## Stationarity checked on an explicit Hamiltonian

**Verify stationarity directly for $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$,
$|\psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle$ (an eigenstate, with $H|0\rangle =
\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}|0\rangle$).**

By the formula above, $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i(\hbar\omega/2)t/\hbar}|0\rangle
= e^{-i\omega t/2}|0\rangle$, which is what the rotation formula gave in
the last lesson's practice question 1. The probability of measuring
$|0\rangle$ again is $|e^{-i\omega t/2}|^2\cdot|\langle0|0\rangle|^2 =
1\cdot1 = 1$ for every $t$.

Now contrast $|\psi(0)\rangle=|+\rangle$ under the same $H$. Here
$E_0=+\hbar\omega/2$ and $E_1=-\hbar\omega/2$, so the gap is
$E_1-E_0=-\hbar\omega$ and the relative phase turns at $\omega$. That
reproduces the $\cos^2(\omega t/2)$ revival curve from the previous
lesson without redoing the algebra: one energy gap, one oscillation
frequency.

## The vector does change; the predictions do not

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing "stationary" means the <Term id="quantum-state">state vector</Term> doesn't change at all.**
It changes by an overall phase continuously. "Stationary" refers
specifically to every measurable prediction staying constant, which
follows from that phase being unobservable, not from the phase being
absent.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming energy conservation only holds for eigenstates of $H$.** The
derivation above shows $\langle H\rangle$ is conserved for *any* initial
state. Note also what it does not say. The individual probabilities of
measuring each energy are frozen too, for the same reason; but the
probabilities of measuring *other* observables are free to oscillate, and
in a superposition of two energies generally do. A frozen
$\langle H\rangle$ is not a frozen system.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is a state that starts as a 50/50 superposition of two *different*
   $H$-eigenstates a stationary state? Justify your answer using the
   definition above, not just intuition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No, provided the two eigenstates carry *different energies*. If they
   happen to be degenerate, the answer flips to yes, and that exception is
   worth keeping in view.

   Take the main case first. Write $|\psi(0)\rangle =
   \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$ with $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$
   and $E_1\ne E_2$. The derivation above gives

   $$
   |\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|1\rangle + e^{-i(E_2-E_1)t/\hbar}|2\rangle\right)
   $$

   The definition of stationary is that *every* measurement probability is
   constant in time, so to answer no it is enough to exhibit one
   observable whose probability moves. Choose the observable whose
   eigenbasis mixes the two levels, with eigenvector $|e\rangle =
   \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$. Dropping the global prefactor,
   which does nothing:

   $$
   \langle e|\psi(t)\rangle = \frac12\left(1+e^{-i\omega_{21}t}\right), \qquad P(e,t) = \frac{1+\cos\omega_{21}t}{2}
   $$

   with $\omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar$. That runs from $1$ down to $0$ and
   back, so the state is not stationary. The concrete instance is the
   previous lesson's worked example: $|+\rangle$ under
   $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ is a 50/50 mix of the two $H$-eigenstates
   $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, and $P(+)=\cos^2(\omega t/2)$ visibly
   oscillates.

   The wrong turn is to test the wrong observable and conclude the state
   *is* stationary. Measure the energy itself and you get
   $P(E_1)=P(E_2)=\tfrac12$ forever, because each component's phase has
   magnitude $1$. Measure anything else diagonal in the energy basis and
   the same thing happens. Those probabilities really are frozen, and
   they are frozen for every state, as the callout above notes. Concluding
   from them that nothing moves is testing only the observables that were
   never going to reveal the motion. Stationarity is a claim about all
   observables, so it takes one counterexample to refute and no finite
   number of confirmations to establish.

   Now the exception. If $E_1=E_2$, the two eigenstates are degenerate and
   $\omega_{21}=0$; the relative phase never turns, the superposition is
   itself an eigenstate of $H$, and it is stationary. So "two different
   eigenstates" is not the right hypothesis on its own. What drives the
   motion is two different *eigenvalues*, which is the same lesson the
   Bohr condition teaches: gaps, not labels.

   </details>

2. For $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ and $|\psi(0)\rangle=|1\rangle$, find
   $|\psi(t)\rangle$ and verify $P(1,t)=1$ for all $t$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{+i\omega t/2}|1\rangle$, and $P(1,t)=1$ for every
   $t$.

   First read off the energy. $Z|1\rangle = -|1\rangle$, so

   $$
   H|1\rangle = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z|1\rangle = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}|1\rangle, \qquad E_1 = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}
   $$

   The sign is the whole content of this question, and it is where the
   mistake lives. Then the eigenstate result applies directly:

   $$
   |\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}|1\rangle = e^{-i(-\hbar\omega/2)t/\hbar}|1\rangle = e^{+i\omega t/2}|1\rangle
   $$

   The phase turns the *other way* from the $|0\rangle$ case in the
   previous lesson, which picked up $e^{-i\omega t/2}$. Two counter-
   rotating phases, one per level, differing by $\omega t$ in total: that
   difference is what drove the worked example's oscillation, and it is
   why the gap and not the individual energies sets the frequency.

   The probability check is one line, and it does not need the sign at
   all:

   $$
   P(1,t) = \left|\langle1|\psi(t)\rangle\right|^2 = \left|e^{+i\omega t/2}\right|^2\left|\langle1|1\rangle\right|^2 = 1\cdot1 = 1
   $$

   using $|e^{i\alpha}|=1$ for real $\alpha$. So the state is certain to
   be found in $|1\rangle$ at every instant, and by the same argument
   every other observable's probabilities are frozen too.

   The instructive contrast is with what the *vector* did. It moved
   continuously the whole time, all the way around the unit circle in the
   complex plane once every $t=4\pi/\omega$. Stationary never meant
   motionless. It means the motion is entirely in a direction no
   measurement can see, which is precisely Postulate 1's phase-invariance
   clause doing physical work rather than sitting there as a technicality.

   </details>

3. Explain why $\langle H\rangle$ being exactly conserved for every state
   is a *stronger* statement than "energy eigenstates are stationary,"
   and what extra case that covers.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because stationarity only ever speaks about eigenstates, and almost no
   state is one. The extra case is every superposition of two or more
   energies, which is where all the interesting physics happens.

   Be precise about what each statement quantifies over. Stationarity
   says: *for eigenstates of $H$*, all observables have frozen
   statistics. Conservation of $\langle H\rangle$ says: *for every state
   whatsoever*, one particular number is frozen. So the comparison worth
   making is over which states each result licenses a claim about
   $\langle H\rangle$, and there stationarity covers a vanishingly small
   set while the conservation theorem covers all of them.

   The proof is what makes this visible. It never mentioned eigenstates:

   $$
   \langle H\rangle_t = \langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)HU(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|H|\psi(0)\rangle
   $$

   because $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is a power series in $H$ and therefore
   commutes with $H$. Nothing was assumed about $|\psi(0)\rangle$ beyond
   its being a state, so the argument applies unchanged to the wildest
   superposition.

   The extra case in concrete terms: take $|+\rangle$ under
   $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$. It is not an eigenstate of $H$, its
   $X$-basis probabilities swing between $0$ and $1$, and stationarity has
   nothing to say about it. Yet $\langle H\rangle =
   \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}\langle Z\rangle = 0$ at every instant, because
   the state stays an equal mix of $+\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}$ and
   $-\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}$ forever. Energy is conserved in a system that
   is emphatically not standing still.

   Two guardrails on this answer.

   First, the conservation theorem is not simply a superset of
   stationarity. Stationarity freezes *every* observable in an eigenstate,
   while conservation freezes only $\langle H\rangle$ across all states.
   Neither statement contains the other outright; what is stronger is
   specifically the claim about $\langle H\rangle$, and specifically in
   the range of states it applies to.

   Second, do not slide from "$\langle H\rangle$ is conserved" to "nothing
   changes." The warning callout above makes the point: the energy
   *distribution* is frozen too, for the same reason, but every other
   observable is free to oscillate and in a two-energy superposition
   generally does. A conserved energy is a constraint on the motion, not
   an absence of it.

   </details>

## Summary

- An energy eigenstate evolves as $|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$,
  the same vector, times an overall phase.
- Because <Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term> is unobservable, every measurement probability for
  such a state is constant in time. That is what "stationary" means.
- A superposition of two eigenstates oscillates at
  $\omega_{21}=(E_2-E_1)/\hbar$: energy *gaps* set every quantum
  frequency, and the zero point of energy never matters.
- $\langle H\rangle$ is conserved for *any* state, which follows from
  $U(t)$ commuting with $H$.
