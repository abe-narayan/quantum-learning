<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
A superposition of energy eigenstates precesses — its measurement statistics visibly change over time. A state that starts as a single energy eigenstate never precesses at all, in a precise sense worth deriving rather than assuming.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

The last lesson's worked example showed the first case directly. This
lesson proves the complementary fact.

## Intuition

If a state starts out as one exact "note" of the Hamiltonian (a single
energy eigenstate), time evolution can only ever produce that same note,
just shifted in phase, since there's no other eigenstate around for it to
leak into. A chord (superposition of several notes) is different: each
note's phase advances at its own rate, and the *relative* phase between
notes, which is what determines the chord's character, does change.

## Mathematical Development

### Energy eigenstates evolve by a single phase

Let $|n\rangle$ satisfy $H|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$. The evolution
operator is defined by its <Term id="taylor-series">Taylor series</Term>, exactly like $e^{i\theta}$ was
built from the Taylor series in the Complex Numbers lesson:

$$
U(t)|n\rangle = e^{-iHt/\hbar}|n\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{-iHt}{\hbar}\right)^k|n\rangle = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!}\left(\frac{-iE_nt}{\hbar}\right)^k|n\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle
$$

(each power of $H$ acting on $|n\rangle$ just returns $E_n^k|n\rangle$,
since $|n\rangle$ is an eigenvector of $H$, so the whole series collapses
back into a plain number, $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$, times $|n\rangle$). So:

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle
$$

The state at time $t$ is the *same* vector $|n\rangle$, multiplied by a
single overall phase factor.

### Every measurement probability is time-independent

By Postulate 1, an overall phase factor doesn't change the physical
state at all: $|n\rangle$ and $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$ represent the
identical physical situation for every value of $t$. Concretely, for
*any* <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $B$ with eigenbasis $\{|e_i\rangle\}$, the Born-rule
probability is

$$
P(\lambda_i, t) = |\langle e_i|\psi(t)\rangle|^2 = |e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|^2\,|\langle e_i|n\rangle|^2 = |\langle e_i|n\rangle|^2
$$

using $|e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|=1$ (Complex Numbers lesson), completely
independent of $t$. This is why $|n\rangle$ is called a **stationary
state**: not because the state *vector* is unchanging (it visibly isn't,
it accrues phase continuously) but because every physically measurable
prediction built from it is exactly constant in time.

precesses" is a difference you can watch, not just derive:

### Energy is exactly conserved, for any state

Here's a stronger, more general fact: $\langle H\rangle$ itself is
exactly conserved for *any* state, not only eigenstates of $H$. Since
$U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ is built purely out of $H$ (as a power series in
$H$), it commutes with $H$: $U(t)H = HU(t)$, so $U^\dagger(t)HU(t)=H$
exactly (using $U^\dagger(t)=U(-t)$, easily checked from the series).
Then:

$$
\langle H\rangle_t = \langle\psi(t)|H|\psi(t)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)HU(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle\psi(0)|H|\psi(0)\rangle = \langle H\rangle_0
$$

Total energy's expectation value never changes under Schrödinger
evolution, the quantum version of energy conservation, and it holds
generally, not just for the special case of an eigenstate.

## Physical Interpretation

"Stationary state" is standard language throughout chemistry and atomic
physics: an electron in a definite energy eigenstate of an atom (an
"orbital") has a probability distribution over position that is
truly static in time, even though nothing about the underlying state
vector has literally stopped changing. A superposition of two different
orbitals, by contrast, has a probability distribution that visibly
oscillates, the physical origin of phenomena like atomic transitions and
Rabi oscillations, both built from exactly the precession picture the
last lesson derived.

## Worked Example

**Verify stationarity directly for $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$,
$|\psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle$ (an eigenstate, with $H|0\rangle =
\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}|0\rangle$).**

By the formula above, $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i(\hbar\omega/2)t/\hbar}|0\rangle
= e^{-i\omega t/2}|0\rangle$, matching the rotation-formula computation
from the last lesson's practice question 1 exactly. The probability of
measuring $|0\rangle$ again is $|e^{-i\omega t/2}|^2\cdot|\langle0|0\rangle|^2
= 1\cdot1 = 1$ for every $t$: certain, and constant, confirming
stationarity concretely rather than only abstractly.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing "stationary" means the <Term id="quantum-state">state vector</Term> doesn't change at all.**
It changes by an overall phase continuously. "Stationary" refers
specifically to every measurable prediction staying constant, which
follows from that phase being unobservable, not from the phase being
absent.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming energy conservation only holds for eigenstates of $H$.** The
derivation above shows $\langle H\rangle$ is exactly conserved for *any*
initial state, a strictly more general fact than stationarity itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An energy eigenstate evolves as $|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$,
  the same vector, times an overall phase.
- Because <Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term> is unobservable, every measurement probability for
  such a state is exactly constant in time, the definition of
  "stationary."
- $\langle H\rangle$ is exactly conserved for *any* state, derived from
  $U(t)$ commuting with $H$, a stronger, more general statement than
  stationarity of eigenstates alone.
