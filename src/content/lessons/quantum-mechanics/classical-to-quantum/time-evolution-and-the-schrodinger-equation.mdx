## The gap Postulate 4 appears to leave

Postulate 4 said evolution is unitary, without saying which unitary
operator, or how it depends on time. That looks like a gap in the theory,
and it is a smaller one than it appears. Unitarity is a strong enough
constraint on its own: applied to an infinitesimal time step, it forces
the <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term> structure on the generator, and everything else in this
lesson follows from calculus applied to that one fact.

<Callout type="note">
**Calculus starts here, and is assumed rather than taught.** Every lesson
up to this one was built from algebra, trigonometry and linear algebra.
This one is the first that needs single-variable calculus, and this
curriculum has no calculus lesson to send you to, so it is worth naming
exactly what is used: a first-order Taylor expansion with a big-$O$
remainder, $f(\varepsilon) = f(0) + f'(0)\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)$,
applied to an operator-valued function of the time step; the limit
$\lim_{N\to\infty}(1+x/N)^N = e^x$; and the derivative
$\frac{d}{dt}e^{\lambda t} = \lambda e^{\lambda t}$. Nothing beyond
those three, and all three sit in the first half of any introductory
calculus text. If they are new, the *results* of this lesson still stand
on their own and every later lesson uses only those results, but the
derivations below will read as assertions instead of arguments, which
is worth knowing before you decide they are.
</Callout>

## A tiny nudge, and what unitarity allows

Think of evolving a state for a very short time $dt$ as a tiny nudge,
$U(dt) \approx I + (\text{something}) \cdot dt$. Unitarity constrains what
that "something" is allowed to be, and the constraint pushes it into the
same Hermitian-operator mold every <Term id="observable">observable</Term> already lives in. That
coincidence is what eventually lets the generator be identified with
energy, with no unrelated bookkeeping object standing in for it.

## From a Hermitian generator to the equation

### The generator must be Hermitian

Write the evolution operator for a short time step as
$U(dt) = I - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt + O(dt^2)$ for some operator $H$ to be
determined ($\hbar$ and the factor of $i$ are conventional choices that
make $H$ come out with units of energy, confirmed below). Impose
unitarity, $U^\dagger(dt)U(dt)=I$, and keep only first-order terms in
$dt$:

$$
U^\dagger U = \left(I+\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H^\dagger dt\right)\left(I-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt\right) = I + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}(H^\dagger - H)\,dt + O(dt^2)
$$

For this to equal $I$ at first order, the $dt$ term must vanish:
$H^\dagger = H$, so **the generator of unitary evolution must be
Hermitian.** Nothing was assumed about $H$ beforehand; unitarity of the
time step alone put it in the one class of operators Postulate 2 reserves
for observables. That is what makes it possible, a few lines below, to
identify $H$ with a measurable quantity rather than treating it as an
abstract generator.

### From the generator to the Schrödinger equation

To reach a finite time $t$, chain $N$ of these steps of length $t/N$ and
let $N$ grow:

$$
U(t) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\left(I - \frac{i H t}{N\hbar}\right)^{N} = e^{-iHt/\hbar}
$$

That limit is the operator version of $\lim_{N\to\infty}(1+x/N)^N = e^x$,
and it is legitimate here for two reasons, both worth naming. Every
factor in the product is the same operator, so ordering never becomes an
issue. And the $O(dt^2)$ term dropped from each individual step does not
quietly accumulate: each of the $N$ steps discards something of size
$O(1/N^2)$, so the whole product discards $N\cdot O(1/N^2)=O(1/N)$, which
goes to zero along with everything else. Chopping time finer really does
converge, rather than trading one error for $N$ of them. (If $H$ itself
depended on time, the factors would differ and would generally fail to
commute; the exponential would then have to be replaced by a time-ordered
product. This course keeps $H$ constant, and works throughout in
finite-dimensional spaces, where the exponential's power series converges
unconditionally; for an unbounded $H$ on an infinite-dimensional space
the same result holds but is Stone's theorem, not a power series.)

Differentiate both sides of $|\psi(t)\rangle = U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$ with
respect to $t$, using $\frac{d}{dt}e^{-iHt/\hbar} =
-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ (ordinary calculus applied to the
operator exponential):

$$
\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = -\frac{i}{\hbar}H\,U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle = -\frac{i}{\hbar}H|\psi(t)\rangle
$$

Rearranged, this is the **time-dependent Schrödinger equation**:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "ihbar", symbol: "iħ d/dt", gloss: "The rate of change of the state, scaled by ħ and rotated a quarter-turn by i. This side is pure calculus; no physics has entered it." },
    { id: "psi", symbol: "|ψ(t)⟩", gloss: "The state vector at time t: everything knowable about the system at that instant." },
    { id: "H", symbol: "H", gloss: "The Hamiltonian: the Hermitian generator of evolution, identified physically with the system's total energy." },
  ]}
>
$$
i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}|\psi(t)\rangle = H|\psi(t)\rangle
$$
</EquationReveal>

$H$ is called the **Hamiltonian**, and it's physically identified with
the system's total energy (the same identification that makes the
$E=\hbar\omega$ relation from de Broglie/Planck-era physics consistent
with this framework, a connection made precise once this course's
successor, Wave Mechanics, introduces continuous position). This
identification is itself a further physical input, not something the
unitarity argument alone proves; it's confirmed by every subsequent
prediction matching experiment.

### A concrete two-level example: reusing existing machinery

For a two-level system, any traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix can be
written $H = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)$ for a real
number $\omega$, a unit vector $\hat n$, and the Pauli matrices
$\vec\sigma=(X,Y,Z)$ (traceless is no real restriction: adding a
multiple of the identity only contributes an unobservable <Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term>,
as the Global Phase vs. Relative Phase lesson in the Quantum Computing
track shows directly). Then:

$$
U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar} = e^{-i(\omega t/2)(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)I - i\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)
$$

Read the right-hand side again and compare it to the definition of
`rotationAboutAxis` in this platform's quantum engine: they are the same
formula, with $\theta = \omega t$. Time evolution under a two-level
Hamiltonian *is* rotation of the Bloch sphere at constant angular
velocity $\omega$ about the axis $\hat n$. Anyone who has clicked the
Bloch Sphere Explorer's rotation gates has been computing this evolution
operator already, one discrete step at a time.

## Eigenstates hold still; superpositions precess

A state that starts as an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> of $H$ stays an eigenstate of $H$
for all time (the next lesson, Stationary States, proves this directly
from the formula for $U(t)$ above). A state that starts as a
superposition of two different-energy eigenstates instead **precesses**:
its measurement probabilities for other observables oscillate at a
frequency set by the energy *difference* between those eigenstates. The
difference is what matters here, with the individual energies dropping out, which is the
same observation that made the overall phase unobservable in Postulate 1.

## Starting from the plus state and watching it return

**$H = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ (so $\hat n = \hat z$). The system starts
in $|\psi(0)\rangle=|+\rangle$. Find $|\psi(t)\rangle$ and its
probability of measuring $|+\rangle$ again at time $t$.**

Using the rotation formula with $\hat n=\hat z$:

$$
U(t) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)I - i\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)Z
$$

Applied to $|+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$, and using
$Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$, $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$:

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\left(\cos\tfrac{\omega t}{2}-i\sin\tfrac{\omega t}{2}\right)|0\rangle + \left(\cos\tfrac{\omega t}{2}+i\sin\tfrac{\omega t}{2}\right)|1\rangle\right] = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(e^{-i\omega t/2}|0\rangle+e^{i\omega t/2}|1\rangle\right)
$$

The probability of measuring $|+\rangle$ again is
$|\langle+|\psi(t)\rangle|^2$. Computing $\langle+|\psi(t)\rangle =
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}e^{-i\omega t/2} +
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}e^{i\omega t/2}\right) = \tfrac12(e^{-i\omega
t/2}+e^{i\omega t/2}) = \cos(\omega t/2)$ (Euler's formula, again), the
probability is $\cos^2(\omega t/2)$: the state precesses around the
Bloch sphere's equator at angular frequency $\omega$, returning to
$|+\rangle$ exactly (probability $1$) at $t=0, \tfrac{2\pi}{\omega},
\tfrac{4\pi}{\omega},\ldots$

Plotting that result makes the periodicity concrete. The two curves below
are $|\langle+|\psi(t)\rangle|^2$ and $|\langle-|\psi(t)\rangle|^2$,
computed by evolving $|+\rangle$ with the platform's own `rotationZ` and
taking inner products, not by evaluating $\cos^2$ by hand:

  frames={precessionFrames}

The state is never partly lost along the way: the two curves sum to $1$
at every instant, which is unitarity showing up as a visible property of
the plot. At $\omega t = \pi$ the system is certain to be found in
$|-\rangle$, having started certain to be found in $|+\rangle$, with no
measurement performed anywhere in between.

## The equation and unitarity are one statement

<Callout type="note">
**Treating the Schrödinger equation as something separate from
unitarity, to be additionally postulated.** As derived above, it follows
directly from requiring the generator of unitary evolution to be
Hermitian: the two are the same statement in different forms.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing $H$ (an operator) with $E$ (a number).** $H$'s *eigenvalues*
are energies; $H$ itself acts on the whole Hilbert space, well beyond
one eigenstate.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ and initial state $|\psi(0)\rangle=|0\rangle$
   (an eigenstate of $H$), use the rotation formula to find
   $|\psi(t)\rangle$. (Compare your answer to the next lesson's claim
   about eigenstates of $H$.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-i\omega t/2}|0\rangle$.

   The rotation formula for $\hat n=\hat z$ is the one the worked example
   used:

   $$
   U(t) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)I - i\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)Z
   $$

   Apply it to $|0\rangle$ and use $Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$, so both terms
   return $|0\rangle$ and only their coefficients survive:

   $$
   |\psi(t)\rangle = \left(\cos\frac{\omega t}{2} - i\sin\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)|0\rangle = e^{-i\omega t/2}|0\rangle
   $$

   the last step by Euler's formula read backwards.

   Sanity-check it against the general eigenstate result the next lesson
   proves, $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$. Here
   $H|0\rangle = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}|0\rangle$, so
   $E_0 = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}$ and the predicted phase is
   $e^{-i(\hbar\omega/2)t/\hbar} = e^{-i\omega t/2}$. The two routes agree,
   which they must, since the rotation formula is just $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$
   written out for a two-level system.

   What matters is what did *not* happen. The vector never acquired a
   $|1\rangle$ component, so no amount of waiting turns $|0\rangle$ into
   anything else under this Hamiltonian. And the factor it did acquire has
   magnitude $|e^{-i\omega t/2}|=1$, a global phase, which Postulate 1
   declares physically invisible. Every measurement probability is
   therefore frozen, which is exactly the stationarity claim.

   The slip to watch for is dropping the factor of $\tfrac12$ and writing
   $e^{-i\omega t}$. The half comes from $H$ carrying
   $\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}$ rather than $\hbar\omega$, and it is the same
   half that makes the Bloch vector complete a turn in $\omega t = 2\pi$
   rather than $\pi$. It matters the moment you compare two levels: the
   worked example's $|+\rangle$ picks up $e^{-i\omega t/2}$ on one
   component and $e^{+i\omega t/2}$ on the other, and the observable
   oscillation runs at the *difference*, $\omega$, not at $\omega/2$.

   </details>

2. Using the worked example's result, at what time $t$ (in terms of
   $\omega$) is the system guaranteed to be found in $|-\rangle$ instead
   of $|+\rangle$ with certainty?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At $t = \pi/\omega$, and again at every odd multiple of it:
   $t = (2k+1)\pi/\omega$ for $k=0,1,2,\ldots$

   The worked example gave $P(+) = \cos^2(\omega t/2)$. Since the two
   outcomes exhaust the basis, $P(-) = 1 - \cos^2(\omega t/2) =
   \sin^2(\omega t/2)$. Certainty in $|-\rangle$ means $P(-)=1$, so

   $$
   \sin^2\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\omega t}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega t = \pi + 2k\pi
   $$

   The first such moment is $\omega t = \pi$, that is $t = \pi/\omega$.
   Read it off the plot above: the brand curve touches zero and the accent
   curve touches one at exactly $\omega t = \pi$ and again at $3\pi$.

   Two features of this answer are the physics.

   The state started certain to be found in $|+\rangle$ and became certain
   to be found in $|-\rangle$, two orthogonal states, with no measurement
   performed at any point in between. Nothing collapsed. This is
   deterministic evolution of the vector, and the probabilities moved
   because the vector moved.

   And the answer scales as $1/\omega$, not as $\omega$. A larger energy
   splitting drives the state around faster, so certainty arrives sooner.
   The commonest error is to solve $\cos^2(\omega t/2)=0$ and stop at
   $\omega t/2 = \pi/2$ without dividing, reporting $t=\pi/(2\omega)$;
   that is the halfway point, a quarter turn around the equator from
   $|+\rangle$, where $P(+)=P(-)=\tfrac12$ and the outcome is as
   undetermined as it ever gets.

   </details>

3. Explain, using the derivation of $U(dt)$, why a non-Hermitian generator
   would fail to produce a norm-preserving evolution.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the first-order term in $U^\dagger(dt)U(dt)$ is proportional to
   $H^\dagger - H$, so the moment $H$ is not Hermitian that term is
   nonzero and the norm starts drifting immediately, at first order in the
   time step.

   The derivation wrote $U(dt) = I - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt + O(dt^2)$ and
   multiplied out:

   $$
   U^\dagger U = \left(I+\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H^\dagger dt\right)\left(I-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt\right) = I + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}\left(H^\dagger - H\right)dt + O(dt^2)
   $$

   Requiring $U^\dagger U = I$ leaves $H^\dagger = H$ with nothing else to
   trade against, because the $O(dt^2)$ remainder cannot cancel a term of
   order $dt$ as $dt\to0$. So Hermiticity is not one way to preserve the
   norm; it is the only way.

   Make it concrete. Take $H = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z -
   \tfrac{i\hbar\Gamma}{2}I$ with real $\Gamma>0$, a Hamiltonian widely
   used in optics precisely because it is not Hermitian. Then $H^\dagger -
   H = i\hbar\Gamma I$, and

   $$
   U^\dagger(dt)U(dt) = I - \Gamma\,dt\,I + O(dt^2)
   $$

   Every state's squared norm shrinks by a factor $1-\Gamma\,dt$ per step,
   and chaining the steps gives $\| |\psi(t)\rangle \|^2 = e^{-\Gamma
   t}$. Total probability leaks away exponentially.

   That example is also the reason non-Hermitian generators are not
   nonsense, and knowing why sharpens the point. They are a deliberately
   incomplete bookkeeping device: the missing probability has gone
   somewhere real, usually into a decay channel that the model has
   declined to represent. The full system including that channel evolves
   unitarily under a Hermitian Hamiltonian. So a non-Hermitian $H$
   signals "this is a subsystem, and I am not tracking where the
   amplitude went," rather than "Postulate 4 is optional."

   The wrong turn is to suppose the problem could be patched by dividing
   by the norm after each step. That restores the number and destroys the
   linearity, since the divisor depends on the state; superposition then
   fails, and with it every interference result in this course.

   </details>

## Summary

- Requiring $U(dt)=I-\tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt$ to be unitary forces
  $H^\dagger=H$: the generator of time evolution must be Hermitian.
- This gives $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$, and differentiating it gives the
  Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,d|\psi\rangle/dt = H|\psi\rangle$.
- For a two-level system, $U(t)$ *is* `rotationAboutAxis` with
  $\theta=\omega t$: evolution under a qubit Hamiltonian is Bloch-sphere
  precession, so no new mathematics is needed to compute it.
