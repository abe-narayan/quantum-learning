## A framework that never mentioned computing

This course built the postulates of quantum mechanics from the ground
up: states, observables, measurement, dynamics, uncertainty, and two
solvable systems (a generic two-level system and the harmonic
oscillator). None of that mentioned computing.

It did not need to. A qubit is a two-dimensional Hilbert space, and every
rule a quantum-computing course states about qubits turns out to be one
of this course's four postulates with $n=2$ substituted in. The rest of
this lesson is that substitution, done explicitly, entry by entry.

Here is the smallest case in full. The Hadamard gate is the first gate
any quantum-computing course introduces; Postulate 4 says a gate has to
be unitary, meaning $H^\dagger H = I$. Rather than assert it, compare the
matrix against the product:

## Recap: A Qubit Is a Two-Level Quantum System

| This course's postulate | Quantum Computing's version |
|---|---|
| State = unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^n$, up to phase | Qubit state $\alpha\lvert0\rangle+\beta\lvert1\rangle$, $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2+\lvert\beta\rvert^2=1$ |
| Observable = Hermitian operator | The computational-basis measurement (a projective measurement in the $\{\lvert0\rangle,\lvert1\rangle\}$ basis) |
| Measurement = Born rule | $P(0)=\lvert\alpha\rvert^2$, $P(1)=\lvert\beta\rvert^2$: the same formula, in the same words |
| Dynamics = unitary evolution | Quantum gates ($X,H,S,\ldots$), every gate is a unitary matrix |
| Uncertainty relation | The Pauli operators' commutators, worked directly in this course's Expectation Values lesson |
| Two-level Hamiltonian evolution | Bloch-sphere rotation, computed by this platform's `rotationAboutAxis`, reused directly in the Time Evolution lesson |
| Tensor products, multiple systems | Multi-qubit states, the same construction, this course's Mathematical Foundations prerequisite already built it in general |

Nothing on the right side of this table is a new postulate. Every entry
is a direct instance of the corresponding entry on the left, specialized
to $\mathbb{C}^2$.

## Questions that need the whole course at once

**1. A quantum gate is applied to a qubit. Why must every gate be
<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>, using this course's own derivation rather than "because that's
the rule"?**

Postulate 4 requires evolution to be unitary because <Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> must
be preserved, and the Unitary Operators lesson proved that unitarity is
the necessary and sufficient condition for that. A "gate" is a name for a
short, discrete stretch of this course's continuous $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$
evolution, run for a fixed useful interval instead of watched
continuously. The check drawn above, $H^\dagger H = I$, is what that
requirement looks like when the interval is the one a Hadamard takes.

**2. The Hadamard gate takes $|0\rangle\to|+\rangle$. Using the
interference result from this course, explain why measuring a qubit
prepared this way in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ <Term id="basis">basis</Term> gives no
information about phase, but measuring in the $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$
basis does.**

This is the Superposition, Interference, and Phase lesson's result with
qubit labels on it. $|+\rangle$ written in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$
basis has equal-magnitude coefficients, so measuring in that basis gives
$P(0)=P(1)=\tfrac12$ no matter what phase gate ran beforehand. The phase
gate does change $\varphi$; the computational-basis measurement is simply
blind to it. Measure in $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$ instead and the same
$\varphi$ drives the outcome through $P(+)=\tfrac{1+\cos\varphi}{2}$.

This is why a quantum algorithm almost always ends with a basis change
before its final measurement: the answer is usually stored in a phase,
and a phase read in the wrong basis reads as nothing at all.

**3. Two qubits are combined via a <Term id="tensor-product">tensor product</Term>. What guarantees the
combined 4-dimensional state is automatically normalized, if each qubit
was normalized on its own?**

This is the Tensor Products and Composite Systems lesson's result:
$\langle u\otimes v,u\otimes v\rangle = \langle u,u\rangle\langle
v,v\rangle = 1\cdot1=1$, from the multiplicative rule for inner products
on simple tensors. No renormalization step is needed, and none is
available to fix things if you get it wrong, which is a useful check when
building multi-qubit states by hand.

## Two habits worth unlearning here

<Callout type="note">
**Treating quantum computing as needing its own separate physical
postulates.** Every rule a quantum-computing course states about qubits
and gates is a specific instance of the same four postulates this course
built for general quantum systems.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Forgetting that "qubit" just means "the smallest nontrivial Hilbert
space."** Nothing in the postulates singles out two dimensions. Qutrits
(three levels) and qudits obey the identical four statements; two is the
choice hardware and error correction find easiest to work with, not a
mathematical requirement.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A qubit gate must satisfy $U^\dagger U=I$. Using the Time Evolution
   lesson's derivation, explain what physical requirement this
   corresponds to.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   That total probability stays at $1$ while the gate runs. Nothing else.

   Trace it back through the two steps this course took. The Unitary
   Operators lesson proved $U^\dagger U = I$ is equivalent to preserving
   every inner product, hence every norm. Postulate 4 requires evolution
   to preserve the norm because $\sum_i P(\lambda_i) = \||\psi\rangle\|^2$
   under the Born rule, so a norm that drifted would be probabilities that
   no longer summed to one. And the Time Evolution lesson showed the
   condition bites at the level of an infinitesimal step: expanding
   $U(dt) = I - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}H\,dt$ and demanding $U^\dagger U = I$
   leaves the first-order term $\tfrac{i}{\hbar}(H^\dagger - H)\,dt$,
   which vanishes only for Hermitian $H$.

   A gate is then not a new kind of object. It is $U(t)=e^{-iHt/\hbar}$
   run for one fixed, useful interval instead of watched continuously. The
   $H^\dagger H = I$ check drawn at the top of this lesson is that
   requirement evaluated for the interval a Hadamard occupies.

   Two wrong answers are worth separating, because both sound reasonable.

   "So the gate is reversible." Reversibility is a consequence, since
   $U^{-1}=U^\dagger$ always exists, but mere invertibility is a strictly
   weaker condition and does not imply unitarity. The matrix
   $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ is invertible and sends
   $|+\rangle$ to a vector of squared norm $\tfrac52$. Reversible is not
   the requirement; norm-preserving is, and it happens to imply
   reversible.

   "So the gate has real eigenvalues." That is Postulate 2's condition on
   observables, and it is the wrong postulate. A unitary's eigenvalues lie
   on the unit circle and are generally complex; the $S$ gate's are $1$
   and $i$. Observables and evolutions are different roles played by
   different classes of operator, and the Hamiltonian is the one object
   that sits in both.

   </details>

2. The CNOT gate acts on two qubits. Using the tensor-product machinery
   from Mathematical Foundations, what is the dimension of the space it
   acts on?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $4$, so CNOT is a $4\times4$ unitary matrix.

   The Tensor Products and Composite Systems lesson proved dimensions
   multiply: $\dim(V\otimes W) = \dim V\cdot\dim W$. Each qubit carries
   $\mathbb{C}^2$, so the pair carries $\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2 =
   \mathbb{C}^4$, with basis
   $\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}$, the four ways to
   choose one basis vector from each factor.

   The error this question is built to catch is answering $2+2=4$ by
   addition and getting the right number for the wrong reason. The two
   rules diverge immediately at three qubits: the direct sum would give
   $6$ and the tensor product gives $8$, and the tensor product is the
   correct one. That exponential growth, $2^n$ rather than $2n$, is the
   entire reason simulating $n$ qubits on a classical machine gets
   expensive, and it comes from this multiplication rule and nowhere else.

   Worth naming too is what the dimension buys. A $4$-dimensional space
   contains vectors that are *not* of the form
   $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ for any single-qubit states, and
   those are the entangled ones. Had the pair been described by nothing more
   than the two qubits' own state vectors side by side, every joint state would
   factor by construction and there would be no room for the entangled ones at
   all. CNOT's usefulness is precisely that it maps unentangled inputs into
   that extra room.

   </details>

3. Measure a qubit in the $Z$ basis, then immediately in the $X$ basis,
   then in the $Z$ basis once more. Find the probability that the third
   reading matches the first, and name the postulate that produces it.
   Then explain why the uncertainty relation $\Delta Z\,\Delta X \ge
   \tfrac12\lvert\langle[Z,X]\rangle\rvert$ is *not* what forces that
   answer, given this course's insistence that the relation is not a
   statement about measurement disturbance.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Exactly $\tfrac12$, whatever the qubit's initial state was. The work is
   done by the collapse clause of Postulate 3, not by the uncertainty
   relation.

   Follow the state. The first $Z$ measurement returns some outcome and
   leaves the qubit in the matching eigenstate; say $|0\rangle$, though
   $|1\rangle$ runs identically. The $X$ measurement then acts on
   $|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|+\rangle+|-\rangle)$, so

   $$
   P(+) = \left|\langle+|0\rangle\right|^2 = \frac12, \qquad P(-) = \frac12
   $$

   and the state becomes $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$. Either one, measured
   in $Z$, splits evenly again: $|\langle0|\pm\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$. So
   the third reading matches the first with probability $\tfrac12$, which
   is the same as guessing. Every trace of the first outcome is gone.

   Compare the run without the middle step: measure $Z$, then $Z$ again,
   and the second reading matches with probability $1$, because the state
   is already an eigenstate. So the $X$ measurement is what destroyed the
   information, and Postulate 3's last clause is where that is written
   down. It says a measurement leaves the system in the eigenstate
   belonging to the outcome, and $|+\rangle$ is not a $Z$-eigenstate. That
   single sentence is the whole mechanism.

   Now the second half, which is the part worth getting right. It is
   tempting to reach for $\Delta Z\,\Delta X \ge
   \tfrac12|\langle[Z,X]\rangle|$ and say the trade-off forced the
   randomization. It did not, and the numbers refute it outright.
   $[Z,X]=2iY$, so the bound in the state $|0\rangle$ is
   $\tfrac12|2i\langle Y\rangle_{|0\rangle}| = 0$. The relation reduces to
   $0 \ge 0$ there and forbids nothing at all, while the disturbance is
   nonetheless total. A statement that is vacuous in the state cannot be
   what forces an outcome in that state.

   The two results also answer different questions. The uncertainty
   relation compares spreads of two observables in *one* state, each
   estimable on its own fresh batch of copies with no measurement of the
   other happening anywhere, which is exactly why the Expectation Values
   lesson insists it is not a disturbance claim. This question is about
   three measurements on *one* qubit in sequence, where each collapse
   supplies the input state for the next.

   What the two share is a common cause rather than an implication. Both
   ultimately trace to $[Z,X]\ne0$ as an *operator*, which is equivalent
   to $Z$ and $X$ having no shared eigenbasis. No shared eigenbasis means
   a $Z$-eigenstate is necessarily spread across $X$-outcomes and back
   again, which is the sequential story; and it is also the condition
   under which the uncertainty bound can be nonzero in *some* state, even
   though it happens to vanish in this one. Non-commutativity of the
   operators is the root; the expectation value of the commutator in one
   particular state is a much weaker, state-dependent shadow of it.

   </details>

4. A two-level system's Hamiltonian is $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}X$
   instead of $\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$. Using the rotation formula from
   Time Evolution, what axis does this Hamiltonian's evolution rotate the
   Bloch sphere about?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   About the $x$ axis, at angular velocity $\omega$.

   The Time Evolution lesson wrote any traceless two-level Hamiltonian as
   $H = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)$ and showed

   $$
   U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)I - i\sin\!\left(\frac{\omega t}{2}\right)(\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma)
   $$

   which is rotation about $\hat n$ by angle $\omega t$. Matching
   $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}X$ against that form gives $\hat n\cdot
   \vec\sigma = X$, so $\hat n = \hat x$. Reading the Hamiltonian is
   reading the rotation axis: whichever Pauli operator appears *is* the
   axis.

   The consequences follow without further calculation, and checking them
   is the fastest way to confirm the identification. Eigenstates of $H$
   are now $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, the poles of the $x$ axis, and
   those are the states that hold still; they are stationary under this
   $H$ exactly as $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ were under the $Z$
   Hamiltonian. Meanwhile $|0\rangle$, which sat frozen before, now
   precesses, sweeping the $y$-$z$ great circle and returning after
   $\omega t = 2\pi$.

   The wrong turn is to expect the axis to depend on the starting state.
   It does not. $\hat n$ comes from $H$ alone; the state decides only
   which circle around that axis gets traced, or whether the state sits
   at a pole and traces nothing. This is why "stationary" is always
   relative to a particular Hamiltonian: $|0\rangle$ is stationary under
   $\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ and is not stationary under
   $\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}X$, with no change to the state itself.

   </details>

## Summary

- A qubit is a 2-dimensional complex Hilbert space; every quantum
  computing rule (superposition, gates, measurement) is a direct instance
  of this course's four postulates.
- Interference, uncertainty, and time evolution all reduce to identities
  this course already derived, evaluated at $\mathbb{C}^2$.
- Nothing about quantum computing requires new physics beyond what this
  course built, only a choice of which Hilbert space to specialize to.
