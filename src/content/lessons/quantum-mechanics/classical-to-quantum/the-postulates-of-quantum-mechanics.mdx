## Motivation

Everything up to this point has been in two separate pieces. The
Mathematical Foundations course built the linear-algebra toolkit,
vectors, inner products, Hermitian and unitary operators, the Born rule,
as pure mathematics, without saying which physical theory any of it was
for. The last three lessons argued the physical side on its own:
experiment forces amplitudes rather than classical probabilities, and
those amplitudes have to be complex, since only a complex phase can
supply the continuously variable interference the double-slit data
actually shows. This lesson is where the two pieces click together. Four
short statements, and nothing more is needed: they *are* quantum
mechanics, in the sense that literally every calculation in the rest of
this course, and in the Quantum Computing pillar built on top of it, is
just these four statements applied to one more specific system.

## Intuition

A postulate isn't proven the way a theorem is. It's the starting
assumption a theory is built from, justified by how well its
*consequences* match experiment, not by derivation from something more
basic. Quantum mechanics has just four of them, and by this point in the
course you already have all the mathematical machinery each one needs.

## Mathematical Development

### Postulate 1: States

The physical state of a system is a normalized vector $|\psi\rangle$ in a
complex Hilbert space, with $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$
representing the identical physical situation for any real $\alpha$.

*Why a vector space, and why complex:* interference (lesson 2) requires
combining multiple contributions, $|\psi\rangle = c_1|{\rm path}_1\rangle +
c_2|{\rm path}_2\rangle+\cdots$, which is exactly a linear combination,
the defining operation of a vector space. The coefficients need to be
complex specifically because only a complex phase supplies the continuous
interference parameter lesson 3 showed the data requires. Normalization
and the phase-invariance clause are exactly the content of the
Probability and Quantum States lesson, derived there rather than merely
asserted.

### Postulate 2: Observables

Every measurable physical quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator
$A$ acting on that Hilbert space. Its possible measured values are
exactly $A$'s eigenvalues.

*Why Hermitian:* a measurement outcome is a real number, full stop. The
Hermitian Operators lesson proved Hermiticity is exactly the algebraic
condition guaranteeing real eigenvalues, and (for repeated identical
measurements) that different outcomes correspond to orthogonal,
perfectly-distinguishable states.

### Postulate 3 (the Born rule): Measurement

If $A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ (spectral decomposition),
measuring $A$ on state $|\psi\rangle$ gives outcome $\lambda_i$ with
probability $P(\lambda_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, and the state
becomes $|e_i\rangle$ immediately afterward.

*Why this rule specifically:* this is exactly the amplitude-squared rule
lessons 2 and 3 showed experiment requires, generalized from two
interfering paths to a full basis of possible outcomes. The Probability
and Quantum States lesson additionally checked this rule is *consistent*:
total probability across all outcomes always sums to exactly $1$,
guaranteed by the completeness relation.

### Postulate 4: Dynamics

Between measurements, a state evolves according to $|\psi(t)\rangle =
U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$ for a unitary operator $U(t)$.

*Why unitary:* the Unitary Operators lesson proved unitary operators are
exactly the ones that preserve inner products, hence norms. Evolution
has to keep $|\psi\rangle$ normalized (total probability can't drift away
from $1$ just from time passing), and unitarity is the precise, necessary
and sufficient condition for that. This course's Time Evolution and the
Schrödinger Equation lesson makes $U(t)$ fully explicit.

### The complete picture

| Postulate | States what | Justified by |
|---|---|---|
| 1. States | Physical state = unit vector, up to global phase | Interference needs superposition; phase invariance derived in Probability & Quantum States |
| 2. Observables | Measurable quantity = Hermitian operator | Real outcomes require real eigenvalues |
| 3. Measurement | Outcome probability = $\lvert\langle e_i\lvert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$ | The exact rule interference data requires |
| 4. Dynamics | Time evolution = unitary operator | Normalization must be preserved over time |

Every calculation the rest of this course, and the entire Quantum
Computing pillar, performs is an application of these four statements to
a specific system.

## Physical Interpretation

Notice what's *not* here: nothing about position, momentum, particles, or
waves specifically. The postulates are deliberately general. They say
nothing about what the Hilbert space or the particular observables *are*
for any given physical system, only how states, observables, measurement,
and evolution relate to each other once you've identified them. A qubit
(finite-dimensional Hilbert space, spin-like observables) and a particle
in space (infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, position and momentum
observables) are both governed by the identical four postulates; only
the specific Hilbert space and operators differ. Later lessons in this
course apply the postulates to position and momentum; the Quantum
Computing pillar applies the exact same postulates to qubits.

## Worked Example

**A two-level system (any system with exactly two possible outcomes for
some observable $A$, with eigenvalues $+1,-1$ and eigenvectors
$|e_+\rangle,|e_-\rangle$) is prepared in state
$|\psi\rangle=\cos(\pi/6)|e_+\rangle+\sin(\pi/6)|e_-\rangle$. Find
$P(+1)$, $P(-1)$, and $\langle A\rangle$.**

By Postulate 3: $P(+1)=|\cos(\pi/6)|^2 = (\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})^2=\tfrac34$,
and $P(-1)=|\sin(\pi/6)|^2=(\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac14$. Check:
$\tfrac34+\tfrac14=1$ ✓, consistent with $|\psi\rangle$ being normalized
(Postulate 1). Expectation value:
$\langle A\rangle = (+1)(\tfrac34)+(-1)(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating the postulates as things to be "proven" from something deeper.**
They're the theory's starting assumptions, justified by matching every
experiment performed against them for over a century, not derived from a
more fundamental theory. (Whether they *can* be derived from something
more basic is an active, unresolved area of theoretical physics, not a
gap in this course.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting Postulate 1's phase-invariance clause when checking whether
two written-down states are "the same."** $|\psi\rangle$ and
$-|\psi\rangle$ (global phase $\alpha=\pi$) are the identical physical
state, even though they look different as vectors.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **State**: a normalized vector, up to global phase. **Observable**: a
  Hermitian operator, whose eigenvalues are the possible outcomes.
  **Measurement**: outcome probabilities via
  $P(\lambda_i)=\lvert\langle e_i\lvert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$, collapsing the
  state to $\lvert e_i\rangle$. **Dynamics**: evolution by a unitary
  operator.
- Every one of the four is justified by a specific earlier result; none
  is arbitrary.
- The postulates say nothing about which physical system they apply to;
  that specificity comes from choosing the Hilbert space and operators.
