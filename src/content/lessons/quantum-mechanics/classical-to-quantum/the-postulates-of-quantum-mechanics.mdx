## Two halves that have not met yet

Everything up to this point has been in two separate pieces. The
Mathematical Foundations course built the linear-algebra toolkit,
vectors, inner products, Hermitian and unitary operators, the Born rule,
as pure mathematics, without saying which physical theory any of it was
for. The last three lessons argued the physical side on its own:
experiment forces amplitudes rather than classical probabilities, and
those amplitudes have to be complex, since only a complex phase can
supply the continuously variable interference the double-slit data
shows. This lesson is where the two pieces meet. What follows are four
statements: three of them assign a piece of mathematics to a piece of
physics, and the fourth says how the mathematics moves in time. Nothing
else gets added later. What changes from system to system is only which
Hilbert space and which operators you plug in.

## What a postulate has to earn

A postulate is the starting assumption a theory is built from, not
something proven the way a theorem is: it earns its place by how well
its *consequences* match experiment, not by derivation from something
more basic. Quantum mechanics has just four of them, and by this point in the
course you already have all the mathematical machinery each one needs.

One more thing is worth saying before the list. These four have been
stable since the late 1920s, and every experiment aimed at them since has
agreed with them. What has *not* stabilized is what they mean, which is a
separate question this track returns to at the very end, in Advanced
Topics' capstone. The formalism below is not in dispute.

## The four, stated in order

### Postulate 1: States

The physical state of a system is a <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> vector $|\psi\rangle$ in a
complex <Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term>, with $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$
representing the identical physical situation for any real $\alpha$.

*Why a <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term>, and why complex:* interference (lesson 2) requires
combining multiple contributions, $|\psi\rangle = c_1|{\rm path}_1\rangle +
c_2|{\rm path}_2\rangle+\cdots$, and that is a linear combination, the
defining operation of a vector space. The coefficients have to be complex
because only a complex phase supplies the continuous interference
parameter lesson 3 showed the data requires. Normalization and the
phase-invariance clause are not extra assumptions bolted on here: the
Probability and Quantum States lesson derived both.

### Postulate 2: Observables

Every measurable physical quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator
$A$ acting on that Hilbert space. Its possible measured values are
exactly $A$'s eigenvalues.

*Why Hermitian:* a measurement outcome is a real number, full stop. The
Hermitian Operators lesson proved Hermiticity is exactly the algebraic
condition guaranteeing real eigenvalues, and (for repeated identical
measurements) that different outcomes correspond to orthogonal,
perfectly-distinguishable states.

### Postulate 3 (the Born rule): Measurement

If $A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ (spectral decomposition),
measuring $A$ on state $|\psi\rangle$ gives outcome $\lambda_i$ with
probability $P(\lambda_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, and the state
becomes $|e_i\rangle$ immediately afterward.

*Why this rule specifically:* it is the amplitude-squared rule lessons 2
and 3 showed experiment requires, generalized from two interfering paths
to a full <Term id="basis">basis</Term> of possible outcomes. The Probability and Quantum
States lesson also checked that it is *consistent*: total probability
across all outcomes sums to $1$, guaranteed by the completeness relation.

The last clause of Postulate 3 is easy to read past. It says measurement
does not merely report a value; it leaves the system in the eigenstate
belonging to the value reported. That has an operational consequence you
can test.

### Postulate 4: Dynamics

Between measurements, a state evolves according to $|\psi(t)\rangle =
U(t)|\psi(0)\rangle$ for a unitary operator $U(t)$.

*Why unitary:* the Unitary Operators lesson proved unitary operators are
exactly the ones that preserve inner products, hence norms. Evolution
has to keep $|\psi\rangle$ normalized (total probability can't drift away
from $1$ just from time passing), and unitarity is the precise, necessary
and sufficient condition for that. This course's Time Evolution and the
Schrödinger Equation lesson makes $U(t)$ fully explicit.

### The complete picture

| Postulate | States what | Justified by |
|---|---|---|
| 1. States | Physical state = unit vector, up to global phase | Interference needs superposition; phase invariance derived in Probability & Quantum States |
| 2. Observables | Measurable quantity = Hermitian operator | Real outcomes require real eigenvalues |
| 3. Measurement | Outcome probability = $\lvert\langle e_i\lvert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$ | The rule the interference data forces |
| 4. Dynamics | Time evolution = unitary operator | Normalization must be preserved over time |

Every calculation the rest of this course, and the entire Quantum
Computing track, performs is an application of these four statements to
a specific system.

## What the postulates deliberately leave out

Notice what's *not* here: nothing about position, momentum, particles, or
waves specifically. The postulates are deliberately general. They say
nothing about what the Hilbert space or the particular observables *are*
for any given physical system, only how states, observables, measurement,
and evolution relate to each other once you've identified them. A qubit
(finite-dimensional Hilbert space, spin-like observables) and a particle
in space (infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, position and momentum
observables) are both governed by the same four postulates; only the
specific Hilbert space and operators differ.

One qualification belongs with that claim rather than after it. Postulate
3 is written above for an observable whose spectrum is discrete, with one
eigenvector per outcome. That is what a qubit has, and what every
calculation in this course uses. Position and momentum have neither:
their spectra are continuous, and $\hat x$ has no normalizable
eigenvector at all, so the sum over $i$ becomes an integral and
$P(\lambda_i)$ becomes a probability *density*. The physical content of
the postulate survives that translation intact, though the formula does
not, and
[What Is a Wavefunction?](/lessons/quantum-mechanics/wave-mechanics/what-is-a-wavefunction)
in the Wave Mechanics course carries it out step by step. Later lessons in
this course apply the postulates to position and momentum; the Quantum
Computing track applies the same four, unchanged, to qubits.

## A two-level system, worked end to end

**A two-level system (any system with exactly two possible outcomes for
some observable $A$, with eigenvalues $+1,-1$ and eigenvectors
$|e_+\rangle,|e_-\rangle$) is prepared in state
$|\psi\rangle=\cos(\pi/6)|e_+\rangle+\sin(\pi/6)|e_-\rangle$. Find
$P(+1)$, $P(-1)$, and $\langle A\rangle$.**

By Postulate 3: $P(+1)=|\cos(\pi/6)|^2 = (\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})^2=\tfrac34$,
and $P(-1)=|\sin(\pi/6)|^2=(\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac14$. Check:
$\tfrac34+\tfrac14=1$ ✓, consistent with $|\psi\rangle$ being normalized
(Postulate 1). Expectation value:
$\langle A\rangle = (+1)(\tfrac34)+(-1)(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12$.

## Starting assumptions are not theorems

<Callout type="note">
**Treating the postulates as things to be "proven" from something deeper.**
They're the theory's starting assumptions, justified by matching every
experiment performed against them for over a century, not derived from a
more fundamental theory. (Whether they *can* be derived from something
more basic is an active, unresolved area of theoretical physics, not a
gap in this course.)
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting Postulate 1's phase-invariance clause when checking whether
two written-down states are "the same."** $|\psi\rangle$ and
$-|\psi\rangle$ (<Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term> $\alpha=\pi$) are the identical physical
state, even though they look different as vectors.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A two-level system is in state
   $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}|e_+\rangle+\frac{2}{\sqrt5}|e_-\rangle$
   (same eigenvalues as the worked example). Find $P(+1)$ and $\langle
   A\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $P(+1)=\tfrac15=0.2$ and $\langle A\rangle = -0.6$.

   Postulate 3 says the probability is the squared magnitude of the
   coefficient on the corresponding eigenvector:

   $$
   P(+1) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\right|^2 = \frac15, \qquad P(-1) = \left|\frac{2}{\sqrt5}\right|^2 = \frac45
   $$

   Check normalization first, because if it fails, nothing downstream is
   meaningful: $\tfrac15+\tfrac45=1$, so Postulate 1 is satisfied and this
   really is a state.

   The expectation value weights each eigenvalue by its probability:

   $$
   \langle A\rangle = (+1)\left(\tfrac15\right)+(-1)\left(\tfrac45\right) = \tfrac15-\tfrac45 = -\tfrac35 = -0.6
   $$

   The error to avoid is squaring the wrong thing. $\tfrac{2}{\sqrt5}$
   squared is $\tfrac45$, not $\tfrac{2}{5}$ and not $\tfrac{4}{\sqrt5}$;
   the denominator gets squared along with the numerator. Getting that
   wrong here shows up immediately as probabilities that fail to sum to
   $1$, which is why checking normalization is worth the ten seconds.

   One structural point. $\langle A\rangle = -0.6$ is a number the
   observable can never return: $A$ has only two possible outcomes, $+1$
   and $-1$. An expectation value is the long-run average over many
   identically prepared copies, not a prediction about any single
   measurement. A student who reports "the measurement gives $-0.6$" has
   confused a statistic of the distribution with a member of it.

   </details>

2. Explain, citing the specific earlier lesson, why Postulate 2 requires
   Hermitian operators rather than allowing any linear operator.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the Hermitian Operators lesson proved that Hermiticity is
   exactly what guarantees two things a measurement needs: real
   eigenvalues, and an orthonormal eigenbasis.

   The first is the obvious one. Postulate 2 says the possible measured
   values *are* the eigenvalues, and a laboratory reading is a real
   number. An operator with complex eigenvalues would predict outcomes no
   instrument can display. Hermiticity delivers real eigenvalues, and the
   same lesson showed the converse direction too, so this is not merely a
   convenient sufficient condition.

   The second is the one that gets skipped, and it is the reason "any
   operator with real eigenvalues" is not good enough. Take

   $$
   A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Its eigenvalues are $1$ and $2$, both perfectly real, and it is not
   Hermitian. Its eigenvectors are $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$, whose inner
   product is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, not zero. Now run Postulate 3 on the
   state $|\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$: the two
   Born-rule numbers come out $0$ and $\tfrac12$, summing to $\tfrac12$
   instead of $1$. Half the probability has vanished. The postulate is not
   merely inelegant for such an operator, it is inconsistent.

   Orthogonality is also what makes distinct outcomes perfectly
   distinguishable. If two outcomes belonged to non-orthogonal
   eigenvectors, a system left in one of them after a measurement would
   still have a nonzero chance of yielding the other outcome on an
   immediate repeat, and the repeatability the collapse clause promises
   would fail.

   So the requirement is not "Hermitian because measurements are real."
   It is "Hermitian because the spectral theorem for Hermitian operators
   is precisely the statement that the Born rule is well defined," and
   that theorem is what the Hermitian Operators lesson proved.

   </details>

3. Explain, citing the specific earlier lesson, why Postulate 4 requires
   unitary operators rather than allowing any invertible operator.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the Unitary Operators lesson proved that unitary operators are
   exactly the ones preserving inner products, hence norms, and total
   probability has to stay at $1$ as time passes.

   Invertibility alone is far too weak. Take
   $M=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, which is invertible and not
   unitary, and apply it to the normalized state
   $|+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$:

   $$
   M|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}2\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \|M|+\rangle\|^2 = \frac{4}{2}+\frac{1}{2} = \frac52
   $$

   Total probability has grown to $2.5$ after one step of "evolution."
   Nothing physical happened to make outcomes more likely; the map simply
   does not conserve length.

   The reply that tempts most students is: fine, divide by the norm
   afterwards. That does restore the number, and it costs more than it
   looks. The dividing factor depends on which state you started from, so
   the corrected map is no longer linear. Superposition then breaks:
   evolving $c_1|\psi_1\rangle+c_2|\psi_2\rangle$ stops being the same
   thing as evolving each piece and adding. Every interference result this
   course has built rests on that linearity, and nonlinear quantum
   evolutions are also known to permit faster-than-light signalling. The
   patch is worse than the disease.

   The precise logic is worth stating in the direction the lesson proved
   it. Preserving the norm of every vector is equivalent to preserving
   every inner product, which is equivalent to $U^\dagger U = I$. So
   unitarity is not one candidate condition among several that happen to
   work. It is a restatement of the requirement itself, which is why
   Postulate 4 can name it without further justification.

   </details>

4. Why does Postulate 1 need a full vector space rather than, say, a
   single real number per state?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the interference data from earlier in this course requires
   states to be *added*, and requires the result of that addition to
   depend on a continuous relative phase. A single real number per state
   supports neither.

   Take the requirements one at a time.

   States have to combine. The double-slit result was that a particle
   reaching the screen has a contribution from each path and the
   contributions combine before any probability is computed. "Combine
   linearly" is the definition of a vector space's addition, so the state
   set needs an addition operation with a scaling operation beside it.
   That already rules out labelling a state by one number and calling the
   combination "the sum of the labels," because you would still have to
   say what physical state the summed label names, and for a single real
   number the answer runs straight into the next requirement.

   The combination has to carry a continuous relative phase. The last
   lesson showed a single sign bit is not enough, so the coefficients have
   to come from a field with a continuum of directions, which is
   $\mathbb{C}$. A state labelled by one real number has one degree of
   freedom and no phase to speak of.

   Probabilities have to come from overlaps in an arbitrary basis. The
   Born rule reads $P = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, an inner product
   between the state and each possible outcome. An inner product is a
   structure on a vector space; there is nowhere to hang it on a bare
   number.

   And the number of outcomes has to be free. A system with $n$
   distinguishable outcomes needs $n$ mutually orthogonal states, which is
   what dimension $n$ means. One real number per state gives no way to say
   how many perfectly distinguishable alternatives a system has.

   The instructive near-miss is a classical probability distribution,
   which does have addition and scaling and can represent $n$
   alternatives. It fails on the phase: mixing distributions gives
   $P = \lambda P_1 + (1-\lambda)P_2$, which never produces a cross term
   and so can never send a probability to zero where both contributions
   are positive. Postulate 1 does not just need *a* space with structure;
   it needs a complex one, and the previous three lessons are the argument
   for each of those words.

   </details>

## Summary

- **State**: a normalized vector, up to global phase. **Observable**: a
  Hermitian operator, whose eigenvalues are the possible outcomes.
  **Measurement**: outcome probabilities via
  $P(\lambda_i)=\lvert\langle e_i\lvert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$, collapsing the
  state to $\lvert e_i\rangle$. **Dynamics**: evolution by a unitary
  operator.
- Every one of the four is justified by a specific earlier result; none
  is arbitrary.
- The postulates say nothing about which physical system they apply to;
  that specificity comes from choosing the Hilbert space and operators.
