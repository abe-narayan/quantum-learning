## Vivid, memorable, and not a derivation

Heisenberg's original 1927 argument for $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$
used a thought experiment: a gamma-ray microscope that necessarily kicks
a particle's momentum around when you use short enough wavelengths to
pin down its position. It is a vivid picture, and it is not a proof. It
says nothing about why a similar bound should hold for other pairs of
observables, and it never produces the factor of $\tfrac12$; the number
just has to be put in by hand.

The Postulates lesson already established the piece this lesson needs:
a state generally doesn't give one definite value for an <Term id="observable">observable</Term>,
only a probability distribution over outcomes, and that distribution has
a spread. This lesson makes "how much spread" quantitative, then finds
the condition under which two observables are forced into a *trade-off*
between their spreads. The uncertainty principle comes out the far end as
a theorem with a proof, not a story about disturbing what you measure.

## Average and spread, as any laboratory would

If you measured the same observable on many identically-prepared copies
of the same state, you would get a distribution of outcomes with some
average and some spread around that average, the way any repeated
measurement in a laboratory does. Quantum uncertainty is that ordinary
statistical spread, computed for the Born-rule distribution.

What makes it more than ordinary statistics is that some *pairs* of
observables cannot both have small spread in the same state. Nothing in
classical statistics forbids two quantities from both being sharp at
once, so if quantum mechanics forbids it, the prohibition has to come
from somewhere specific in the formalism. Finding that place is the work
of this lesson.

## Cauchy-Schwarz, applied to two observables

### Expectation value and uncertainty, formally

For a normalized state $|\psi\rangle$ and <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term> observable $A$, the
**expectation value** is $\langle A\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$
(already used in the Hermitian Operators lesson), and the **variance** is

$$
\operatorname{Var}(A) = \langle (A-\langle A\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2
$$

the same "mean of the squared deviation" formula as ordinary
statistics, since $P(\lambda_i)$ from the <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> is a probability
distribution in the usual sense. The **uncertainty** is
$\Delta A = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(A)}$, the standard deviation of
measurement outcomes.

### Setting up the derivation

Let $A,B$ be Hermitian, and define the *shifted* operators
$\tilde A = A - \langle A\rangle I$, $\tilde B = B - \langle B\rangle I$
(still Hermitian, since $\langle A\rangle,\langle B\rangle$ are real
numbers). By construction, $\langle\tilde A\rangle=\langle\tilde
B\rangle=0$, and $\operatorname{Var}(A)=\langle\tilde A^2\rangle$,
$\operatorname{Var}(B)=\langle\tilde B^2\rangle$. Also note
$[\tilde A,\tilde B]=[A,B]$ exactly: subtracting a constant multiple of
the identity never changes a commutator, since the identity commutes with
everything.

Define $|f\rangle = \tilde A|\psi\rangle$, $|g\rangle=\tilde B|\psi\rangle$.
Then $\langle f|f\rangle = \langle\psi|\tilde A^\dagger\tilde
A|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\tilde A^2|\psi\rangle=\operatorname{Var}(A)$
(using $\tilde A^\dagger=\tilde A$, Hermitian), and similarly
$\langle g|g\rangle=\operatorname{Var}(B)$.

### Applying Cauchy-Schwarz

By the <Term id="cauchy-schwarz-inequality">Cauchy-Schwarz</Term> inequality (Inner Products lesson):

$$
|\langle f|g\rangle|^2 \le \langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle = \operatorname{Var}(A)\operatorname{Var}(B)
$$

Now examine $\langle f|g\rangle = \langle\psi|\tilde A\tilde B|\psi\rangle$ more closely.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Split the operator product into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts. This is an algebraic identity true for any two operators; nothing physical has been assumed yet.">
$$
\tilde A\tilde B = \tfrac12(\tilde A\tilde B+\tilde B\tilde A) + \tfrac12(\tilde A\tilde B-\tilde B\tilde A) = \tfrac12\{\tilde A,\tilde B\} + \tfrac12[\tilde A,\tilde B]
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Check the two halves separately. Using (MN)† = N†M† and Ã† = Ã, B̃† = B̃: the anticommutator's adjoint is (ÃB̃ + B̃Ã)† = B̃Ã + ÃB̃, which is the anticommutator back, so it is Hermitian and its expectation value is real. The commutator's adjoint is (ÃB̃ − B̃Ã)† = B̃Ã − ÃB̃, which is minus the commutator, so it is anti-Hermitian and its expectation value is purely imaginary. Same style of argument as the Hermitian Operators lesson, with one sign flipped.">
$$
\langle f|g\rangle = \underbrace{\tfrac12\langle\{\tilde A,\tilde B\}\rangle}_{\text{real}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\langle[\tilde A,\tilde B]\rangle}_{\text{purely imaginary}}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Squared magnitude of a real-plus-imaginary sum is the sum of the squares of each part; dropping the non-negative real-part-squared term only weakens the bound, never breaks it. The commutator can also lose its tildes at this point: Ã and B̃ differ from A and B by multiples of the identity, which commute with everything, so [Ã,B̃] = [A,B] exactly.">
$$
|\langle f|g\rangle|^2 = \left(\tfrac12\langle\{\tilde A,\tilde B\}\rangle\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac12\langle[\tilde A,\tilde B]\rangle/i\right)^2 \ge \left(\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|\right)^2
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### The result

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Chain the Cauchy-Schwarz step from above together with the bound just derived.">
$$
\operatorname{Var}(A)\operatorname{Var}(B) \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2 \ge \left(\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|\right)^2
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Take square roots. Every quantity here is non-negative, so the inequality survives in both directions.">
$$
\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \tfrac12\left|\langle[A,B]\rangle\right|
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

This is the **generalized uncertainty relation**, and it is a theorem:
every step above is Cauchy-Schwarz plus the definitions of Hermitian and
anti-Hermitian. No measurement disturbance was invoked, no experimental
limitation, no physical argument beyond the postulates already in hand.
Notice also where the $\tfrac12$ came from. It is the $\tfrac12$ in the
symmetric/antisymmetric split of the first step, carried through
untouched. Heisenberg's microscope could never have produced it.

### When is there no trade-off at all?

If $[A,B]=0$ (the operators **commute**), the right-hand side is zero,
and the inequality places no restriction at all. Both observables *can*
be made simultaneously as sharp as you like (and in finite dimensions, or
whenever the spectra are purely discrete, simultaneous eigenstates of both
exist; with continuous spectra the eigenstates can fail to live in the
Hilbert space at all, which
[Continuous Spectra and Rigged Hilbert Space](/lessons/quantum-mastery/hilbert-space-and-spectral-theory/continuous-spectra-and-rigged-hilbert-space)
takes up properly). Uncertainty trade-offs are specifically a consequence
of **non-commuting** observables, rather than a universal fact about all
pairs.

## Substituting the canonical commutator in

The familiar position-momentum uncertainty principle, $\Delta x\,\Delta p
\ge \hbar/2$, is this relation with $[x,p]=i\hbar$ substituted in (the
commutator is introduced properly in the Position and Momentum lesson).
Substituting gives $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \tfrac12|i\hbar| = \hbar/2$,
with no further physics required.

So the position-momentum bound is not about clumsy instruments. It is a
structural fact about two operators that fail to commute, and it survives
intact for perfect, idealized measurements made on separate copies of the
state, where nothing is being disturbed at all.

## Checking the bound for X and Y in the zero state

**For Pauli operators $A=X$, $B=Y$ (from the Mathematical Foundations
course), $[X,Y]=2iZ$. In the state $|0\rangle$ (a $Z$-eigenstate,
$\langle Z\rangle=1$), verify the uncertainty relation.**

$\langle[X,Y]\rangle = 2i\langle Z\rangle = 2i$, so the right-hand side is
$\tfrac12|2i| = 1$. For the left-hand side: $\langle X\rangle=0$,
$\langle X^2\rangle=\langle I\rangle=1$ (since $X^2=I$), so
$\operatorname{Var}(X)=1$, $\Delta X=1$; identically $\Delta Y=1$. So
$\Delta X\,\Delta Y = 1 \ge 1$: the bound is **exactly saturated**, not
merely satisfied. (This is expected: $|0\rangle$ is a special "minimum
uncertainty" state for this pair, not a coincidence of the numbers
chosen.)

## Nothing in the derivation disturbs anything

<Callout type="mistake">
**Describing uncertainty as "measuring one property disturbs the other."**
The derivation above never mentions a measurement disturbing anything.
Both $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ are properties of one state and two
operators, and each can be estimated on its own fresh batch of identical
copies, with no measurement of the other observable happening anywhere.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming every pair of observables has a nontrivial trade-off.**
Commuting observables ($[A,B]=0$) have no uncertainty relation forcing
any trade-off at all.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $A=Z, B=X$ (so $[Z,X]=2iY$, by antisymmetry of the commutator
   applied to the relation used in the worked example), in state
   $|0\rangle$: find $\langle[Z,X]\rangle$ and the right-hand side bound.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\langle[Z,X]\rangle = 0$, and the bound is $0$.

   The commutator itself is not zero. Multiplying out confirms the
   relation quoted in the question:

   $$
   [Z,X] = 2iY = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   But the bound is not built from the commutator, it is built from the
   commutator's *expectation value in the given state*, and

   $$
   \langle[Z,X]\rangle = 2i\langle Y\rangle_{|0\rangle} = 2i\cdot 0 = 0
   $$

   because $|0\rangle$ sits at the north pole of the Bloch sphere, where
   $\langle X\rangle = \langle Y\rangle = 0$. The bound is
   $\tfrac12|0| = 0$.

   That is the result to sit with, because it looks like a contradiction
   and is not. $Z$ and $X$ do not commute, yet in this particular state
   the uncertainty relation imposes no trade-off whatsoever. Check the
   left-hand side to see why nothing is broken: $|0\rangle$ is an
   eigenstate of $Z$, so $\Delta Z = 0$, while $\Delta X = 1$. The
   product is $0$, and $0 \ge 0$ holds, saturated.

   The wrong turn here is extremely common and worth naming precisely. It
   is reading $\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ as
   though the right-hand side were a property of the pair $(A,B)$, so
   that non-commuting operators always carry a nonzero floor. It is a
   property of the pair *and the state*. Position and momentum are the
   exception that creates the habit: $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$ is a
   number times the identity, so its expectation value is the same in
   every state and the floor never drops. For $Z$ and $X$ it drops to
   zero at both poles, which the bar-chart explorer above shows happening
   as $\theta$ moves.

   Question 4 explains the zero from the other direction: whenever a
   state makes one observable definite, it forces
   $\langle[A,B]\rangle=0$ for *every* $B$.

   </details>

2. In the state $|+\rangle$, $X$ is definite (it's an eigenstate of $X$),
   so $\Delta X=0$. Compute $\Delta Z$ in $|+\rangle$, then compute
   $\langle[Z,X]\rangle$ in $|+\rangle$ and verify the uncertainty
   relation still holds even with one side exactly zero.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\Delta Z = 1$, $\langle[Z,X]\rangle = 0$, and the relation reads
   $0 \ge 0$.

   For $\Delta Z$, use $\operatorname{Var}(Z)=\langle Z^2\rangle-\langle
   Z\rangle^2$. In $|+\rangle$ the two computational outcomes are equally
   likely, so $\langle Z\rangle = \tfrac12(+1)+\tfrac12(-1) = 0$, and
   $Z^2 = I$ gives $\langle Z^2\rangle = 1$. Hence
   $\operatorname{Var}(Z) = 1 - 0 = 1$ and $\Delta Z = 1$.

   For the right-hand side, $\langle[Z,X]\rangle = 2i\langle
   Y\rangle_{|+\rangle}$, and $|+\rangle$ sits on the $+x$ axis of the
   Bloch sphere where $\langle Y\rangle = 0$. So
   $\langle[Z,X]\rangle = 0$ and the bound is $0$.

   Putting the two sides together:

   $$
   \Delta Z\,\Delta X = (1)(0) = 0 \;\ge\; \tfrac12\left|\langle[Z,X]\rangle\right| = 0
   $$

   The relation holds, and it holds with equality.

   The point of the question is what does *not* happen. A student who has
   absorbed the slogan "non-commuting observables cannot both be sharp"
   expects a contradiction here: $X$ is perfectly sharp, $Z$ and $X$ do
   not commute, so surely something must forbid this. Nothing does. What
   the theorem actually forbids is both sides being sharp while
   $\langle[A,B]\rangle$ is nonzero, and the moment $\Delta X$ hits zero
   the right-hand side is dragged to zero with it. The two events are not
   a coincidence; question 4 shows one implies the other.

   Notice also that $\Delta Z = 1$ is the largest it can be for a Pauli
   operator, whose outcomes are $\pm1$. Making $X$ perfectly definite has
   pushed $Z$ to maximal spread. So the trade-off is real and visible in
   the numbers, even though the inequality is not what enforces it here.
   The inequality is a floor, and a floor of zero simply stops
   constraining; the actual behaviour comes from the states themselves.

   </details>

3. If $[A,B]=0$ for two observables, what does the derivation above say
   about whether a state can be a simultaneous eigenstate of both?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Strictly, it says only that nothing forbids it. The derivation gives
   permission, not existence.

   Set $[A,B]=0$ in the result and the right-hand side is zero, so the
   inequality reads $\Delta A\,\Delta B\ge0$. That is true of any two
   non-negative numbers and rules nothing out. In particular it no longer
   rules out $\Delta A = \Delta B = 0$, which is what a simultaneous
   eigenstate would mean. Removing an obstruction is not the same as
   producing the thing the obstruction was blocking, and the derivation
   does exactly the first.

   Existence is a separate theorem, and it comes with a hypothesis worth
   keeping. The lesson states it above: in finite dimensions, or whenever
   the spectra are purely discrete, two commuting Hermitian operators do
   admit a common eigenbasis. With continuous spectra the argument can
   fail in a specific way, because the would-be eigenvectors need not be
   normalizable and so need not live in the Hilbert space at all;
   $\hat x$ is the standard example, and Quantum Mastery's lesson on
   rigged Hilbert space is where that is handled properly. Dropping the
   hypothesis and asserting "commuting operators always share an
   eigenbasis" is the mistake this question is guarding against.

   There is a second, smaller overreach to avoid. Even where the common
   eigenbasis does exist, it does not follow that *the state you happen to
   have* is one of those simultaneous eigenstates. Commuting observables
   guarantee such states are available, not that every state is one. Take
   $A=Z$ and $B=Z$, which certainly commute; the state $|+\rangle$ is an
   eigenstate of neither, and $\Delta Z = 1$ in it. The relation is
   satisfied, $1\cdot1\ge0$, with nothing sharp anywhere.

   So the honest summary is three-layered. Commuting kills the forced
   trade-off; a separate theorem, under stated hypotheses, supplies a
   basis of simultaneous eigenstates; and being in one of them is a fact
   about your particular state, not about the operators.

   </details>

4. Explain, using the derivation, why $\operatorname{Var}(A)=0$ (a
   definite value for $A$) forces $\langle[A,B]\rangle=0$ for that
   particular state, for any $B$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\operatorname{Var}(A)$ is the squared *length* of the vector
   $|f\rangle=\tilde A|\psi\rangle$, and a vector of length zero is the
   zero vector, whose overlap with anything at all is zero.

   Follow the derivation's own construction. It set
   $|f\rangle = \tilde A|\psi\rangle$ and showed
   $\langle f|f\rangle = \operatorname{Var}(A)$. If
   $\operatorname{Var}(A)=0$ then $\langle f|f\rangle = 0$, and
   positive-definiteness of the inner product (the Inner Products lesson)
   says the only vector with zero norm is $|f\rangle = 0$ itself.

   Now take any Hermitian $B$ and build $|g\rangle = \tilde B|\psi\rangle$
   as the derivation does. Then

   $$
   \langle f|g\rangle = 0
   $$

   because $|f\rangle$ is the zero vector, with no inequality needed. But
   the derivation also computed that same overlap in two pieces:

   $$
   \langle f|g\rangle = \underbrace{\tfrac12\langle\{\tilde A,\tilde B\}\rangle}_{\text{real}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12\langle[A,B]\rangle}_{\text{purely imaginary}}
   $$

   A complex number is zero only if its real and imaginary parts are both
   zero separately. The second piece is the whole imaginary part, so
   $\langle[A,B]\rangle = 0$. Since $B$ was arbitrary, this holds for
   every observable at once.

   It is worth taking this route rather than the shortcut of putting
   $\Delta A = 0$ into $\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge
   \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ and reading off $0 \ge
   \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$. That shortcut does give the right
   answer here, but it multiplies $\Delta A$ by $\Delta B$ and so quietly
   assumes $\Delta B$ is finite, which for an unbounded operator on an
   infinite-dimensional space is exactly the kind of assumption that
   fails. Going back to $\langle f|g\rangle$ never forms that product.

   This is also the general fact behind questions 1 and 2. In $|0\rangle$,
   $\operatorname{Var}(Z)=0$, so $\langle[Z,B]\rangle$ vanishes for
   *every* $B$, and $B=X$ was just one instance. In $|+\rangle$,
   $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0$ does the same job with the roles swapped.
   Neither zero was a numerical accident of the Pauli matrices.

   The reading to resist is "so a definite value for $A$ makes $A$ commute
   with everything." It does not. $[Z,X]=2iY$ is a nonzero operator
   whatever state you are in. What collapses is its expectation value in
   this one state, and move to a state on the equator and the same
   commutator's expectation value comes back.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\operatorname{Var}(A)=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2$;
  $\Delta A = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(A)}$: ordinary statistics applied
  to the Born-rule distribution.
- $\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$, derived from
  Cauchy-Schwarz applied to the shifted operators $\tilde A,\tilde B$.
- The bound is exactly zero (no trade-off) whenever $[A,B]=0$.
- This is a structural theorem about non-commuting Hermitian operators,
  not a story about measurement disturbance.
