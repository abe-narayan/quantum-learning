## Signed reals add and cancel too

The last lesson showed amplitudes have to add before squaring. It didn't
explain why those amplitudes need to be *complex* specifically. Signed
real numbers can also add constructively ($(+1)+(+1)=2$) or destructively
($(+1)+(-1)=0$). This lesson makes that question precise, and answers it.

## One bit of direction, and no in-between

A signed real number carries one bit of "direction" information: its
sign. Two same-signed amplitudes always reinforce fully; two
opposite-signed amplitudes always cancel fully (if equal in magnitude).
There's no in-between. But the actual double-slit fringe pattern varies
*continuously* as you move across the screen: fully bright at the center,
smoothly dimming through every intermediate brightness, down to fully
dark, then smoothly brightening again. A quantity with only two possible
"directions" cannot produce a smooth continuum of outcomes. Something with
a continuously adjustable direction can, and that is what a complex
number's phase gives you.

## The knob a sign cannot supply

Before formalizing the intuition above, pin down a prediction about the
algebra itself.

### Real amplitudes can't produce partial interference

Suppose, for contradiction, amplitudes were always real numbers (signed,
but not complex). Two contributions $\psi_1,\psi_2\in\mathbb{R}$ combine
as $(\psi_1+\psi_2)^2$. Expand it:

$$
(\psi_1+\psi_2)^2 = \psi_1^2+\psi_2^2+2\psi_1\psi_2
$$

The cross term $2\psi_1\psi_2$ has a fixed sign for any fixed pair of real
numbers. You can dial its *magnitude* by changing $\psi_1$ or $\psi_2$,
but not smoothly sweep it from "fully constructive" through "no effect"
to "fully destructive" using a *single* continuously-adjustable
parameter that leaves the individual probabilities $\psi_1^2,\psi_2^2$
unchanged. The observed fringe pattern demands exactly that:
a single knob, position on the screen, that continuously retunes the
interference from bright to dark while the two paths' individual
intensities stay essentially fixed.

### The complex phase supplies that missing knob

Write $\psi_2 = |\psi_2|e^{i\theta}$ (polar form, from the Complex
Numbers lesson), and take $\psi_1$ real and positive for simplicity (a
complex $\psi_1$ only shifts where $\theta$ is measured from, not the
shape of the argument). The cross term from the last lesson's expansion
is $\psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1 = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\psi_1^*\psi_2) =
2\psi_1|\psi_2|\cos\theta$, using $\mathrm{Re}(e^{i\theta})=\cos\theta$
directly from Euler's formula. The cross term is therefore proportional
to $\cos\theta$, which sweeps through every value in $[-1,1]$ as $\theta$
turns once around the circle. That is the constructive-to-destructive
continuum the experiment shows, driven by one real parameter that leaves
$|\psi_1|$ and $|\psi_2|$ untouched. Polar form and Euler's formula,
built for their own sake in the Complex Numbers lesson,
turn out to be physically indispensable: the phase $\theta$ *is* the
knob nature turns.

The plot below is the whole argument in one picture. The brand curve is
$|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2$ as $\theta$ runs once around the circle, with both
magnitudes pinned at $0.5$ the entire time. The flat grey line is the
classical prediction $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2$, which cannot move because
nothing about the magnitudes changed. The two dashed lines mark the only
two heights signed real amplitudes can produce: $1.0$ when the signs
match, $0$ when they oppose. Everything the curve passes through between
those dashed lines is territory reals cannot reach, and it is where most
of a real fringe pattern lives.

Notice where the curve crosses the flat line: at $\theta=\pi/2$ and
$\theta=3\pi/2$. The first of those is the $\theta=\pi/2$ row of the
previous lesson's table, the one place quantum and classical agreed
there; the second is its mirror image on the far side of the turn, which
a three-row table had no way to show. Seeing the whole sweep makes clear
that agreement happens at two isolated crossings, not over a region.

### Where the phase physically comes from

In the double-slit setup, the phase difference between the two paths
comes from the difference in distance each path travels to reach a given
point on the screen. A longer path accumulates more phase, in direct
analogy to how a wave's phase advances with distance traveled. This is
the same idea de Broglie's matter-wave picture and the Schrödinger
equation (a later lesson in this course) make precise; for now, the
important point is structural: *some* continuously-variable phase is
required by the data, and complex numbers are the minimal, natural
mathematical object that has such a phase built in.

## Could a real vector space fake it?

<Callout type="note">
A deeper question, could a real (but higher-dimensional) <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term>
somehow reproduce every quantum prediction, without ever invoking complex
numbers explicitly, turns out to be subtler than it looks, and wasn't
fully settled until very recently. Experiments completed in the 2020s,
building on a theoretical argument that "real quantum mechanics" predicts
different results from ordinary complex quantum mechanics in certain
multi-particle entangled scenarios (specifically, independent sources
feeding a shared network), ruled out that specific class of real-vector-space
theories directly. That argument needs entanglement machinery well beyond
this lesson, so it isn't derived here. The question does have a genuine,
experimentally-tested answer for the scenarios tried, not just a
mathematical convenience.
</Callout>

## Three paths, all carrying the same sign

**Three paths contribute equal-magnitude real amplitudes
$\psi_1=\psi_2=\psi_3=0.4$. What is $(\psi_1+\psi_2+\psi_3)^2$, and why
can no relabeling of signs alone reproduce a case where each *pair*
partially, not fully, interferes?**

With all positive, $(\psi_1+\psi_2+\psi_3)^2 = (1.2)^2=1.44$ (fully
constructive). Flipping any sign only ever fully cancels or fully
reinforces that particular pair; e.g. $\psi_1=0.4,\psi_2=-0.4$ cancels
those two completely regardless of $\psi_3$. There is no assignment of
signs that makes, say, the $\psi_1$-$\psi_2$ pair interfere at 30% of
maximum while the $\psi_1$-$\psi_3$ pair interferes at 70%. Signs are a
2-valued choice per amplitude, but the experiment needs a continuous
value per *pair*. Complex phases, one continuously-adjustable angle per
amplitude, supply exactly that: $\theta_1-\theta_2$ and $\theta_1-\theta_3$
can be tuned independently to any relative interference strength at all.

## A minus sign is one phase out of a continuum

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating "negative amplitude" and "complex phase" as the same idea.** A
negative sign is one special case of a complex phase ($\theta=\pi$), not
a substitute for the whole continuum $\theta\in[0,2\pi)$ provides.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming phase is directly <Term id="observable">observable</Term>, the way position is.** A single
amplitude's phase changes nothing about *its own* probability
($|\psi|^2$ doesn't depend on $\theta$). Phase only becomes physically
meaningful *relative* to another amplitude it's added to, as the explorer
above demonstrates directly.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For real amplitudes $\psi_1=0.5,\psi_2=-0.5$, compute
   $(\psi_1+\psi_2)^2$. Is any value strictly between full constructive
   and full destructive interference reachable by changing only the sign
   of $\psi_2$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $(0.5-0.5)^2 = 0$. And no, nothing in between is reachable.

   The sign of $\psi_2$ is a two-valued choice, so flipping it gives
   exactly two outcomes and no others:

   $$
   (0.5+0.5)^2 = 1, \qquad (0.5-0.5)^2 = 0
   $$

   Those are the two dashed reference lines on the plot above. Every
   height between them, which is most of a real fringe pattern, is
   unreachable by any choice of sign.

   The reason is structural rather than arithmetic. Expanding gives
   $\psi_1^2+\psi_2^2+2\psi_1\psi_2$, and the sign flip changes only the
   third term, from $+0.5$ to $-0.5$. There is no third option, because
   there is no third sign. Nothing continuous is being varied.

   The tempting objection is that you could reach intermediate values by
   changing the *magnitude* of $\psi_2$ instead, and that is true but
   answers a different question. Setting $\psi_2 = -0.3$ gives
   $(0.5-0.3)^2 = 0.04$, an intermediate number. But the individual
   probabilities moved too: $\psi_2^2$ went from $0.25$ to $0.09$, so one
   path's own brightness changed. The fringe pattern in the experiment
   does not work that way. Sweep across the screen and each slit's
   individual intensity stays essentially constant while the combined
   brightness runs through every value. That is the knob the sign cannot
   supply, and the magnitude cannot supply it either without violating the
   condition that $|\psi_1|$ and $|\psi_2|$ hold still.

   </details>

2. For $\psi_1=0.5$, $\psi_2=0.5e^{i\theta}$, find every value of $\theta$
   in $[0,2\pi)$ at which the interference term contributes exactly zero,
   so the quantum and classical predictions coincide. How many are there,
   and why is that count neither zero, nor one, nor infinite?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Exactly two: $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\theta = 3\pi/2$. At both, the two
   predictions agree at $0.5$.

   The interference term derived above is

   $$
   \psi_1^*\psi_2+\psi_2^*\psi_1 = 2|\psi_1||\psi_2|\cos\theta = 2(0.5)(0.5)\cos\theta = 0.5\cos\theta
   $$

   With both magnitudes fixed and nonzero, the term vanishes exactly where
   $\cos\theta = 0$, which on one full turn happens at $\pi/2$ and
   $3\pi/2$ and nowhere else. Check either one directly: at
   $\theta=\pi/2$, $\psi_1+\psi_2 = 0.5+0.5i$, whose squared magnitude is
   $0.25+0.25=0.5$, matching the classical
   $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2 = 0.25+0.25 = 0.5$.

   The count is worth thinking about, because each of the three wrong
   counts corresponds to a different misreading.

   Not zero, because $\cos\theta$ is continuous and runs from $+1$ to
   $-1$, so it cannot get from the fully constructive end to the fully
   destructive end without passing through zero. Agreement between the
   quantum and classical rules is forced somewhere, not accidental.

   Not one, because $\cos\theta$ crosses zero twice per turn, once on the
   way down and once on the way back up. This is the point the previous
   lesson's three-row table could not show, and it is why the parametric
   plot above sweeps a full $2\pi$ rather than stopping at $\pi$: the
   second crossing is the mirror image of the first, and a table that
   stops halfway makes the agreement look like an endpoint rather than a
   crossing.

   Not infinite, because $\cos\theta$ is not identically zero. It would
   be identically zero only if $|\psi_1|$ or $|\psi_2|$ were zero, that
   is, if one of the two paths were closed. With both open, agreement is
   confined to two isolated angles out of a continuum, and everywhere else
   the quantum prediction and the classical one are genuinely different
   numbers.

   </details>

3. Explain in your own words why "a continuous knob that doesn't change
   $|\psi_1|$ or $|\psi_2|$" is something a sign bit structurally cannot
   provide, but a phase angle can.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a sign has two values and an angle has a continuum of them, and
   in both cases the magnitude is left untouched, so the difference is
   entirely in how many settings the knob has.

   Look at what each object is. A nonzero real number factors as
   (magnitude) times (sign), where the sign is drawn from a two-element
   set $\{+1,-1\}$. A nonzero complex number factors as (magnitude) times
   $e^{i\theta}$, where $e^{i\theta}$ is drawn from the whole unit circle.
   Both factorizations separate "how big" from "which direction," and in
   both cases turning the direction knob leaves the magnitude alone, so
   $|\psi_1|^2$ and $|\psi_2|^2$ hold still either way. The two-element
   set is simply a very small subset of the circle: $\{+1,-1\}$ is the
   pair of points $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi$.

   Now feed each into the cross term. For real amplitudes it is
   $2\psi_1\psi_2$, which takes exactly two values as the sign flips. For
   complex ones it is $2|\psi_1||\psi_2|\cos\theta$, and as $\theta$ turns
   once around the circle $\cos\theta$ sweeps continuously through every
   value in $[-1,1]$, hitting the two real cases at the two ends and
   filling in everything between them. The complex version does not
   replace the real one; it interpolates it.

   The experiment is what forces the larger set. Moving across the screen
   changes the path-length difference smoothly, so whatever the direction
   knob is, it has to be adjustable smoothly, and the resulting brightness
   has to vary smoothly with it. A quantity with two settings can produce
   two brightnesses. The fringe pattern shows a continuum of them, at
   fixed single-slit intensities. So the direction knob has more than two
   settings, and the unit circle is the smallest and most natural object
   that supplies them.

   The wrong turn worth naming is treating a minus sign as "the same idea
   as a phase, just written more simply." It is not a simplification, it
   is one point of the circle. Any argument that only ever needs full
   reinforcement and full cancellation can indeed get by with signs, which
   is why the real-number picture survives for so long in introductory
   wave optics. The moment partial interference at fixed intensities has
   to be accounted for, the two-element set runs out of values and there
   is nothing to fall back on.

   </details>

## Summary

- Signed real amplitudes only ever produce full constructive or full
  destructive interference for a fixed pair of magnitudes, a 2-valued
  outcome.
- Experimentally, interference varies continuously, requiring a
  continuously-adjustable parameter (a phase) that leaves the individual
  probabilities unchanged.
- A complex number's polar-form phase $\theta$ is exactly that parameter;
  the cross term in $\lvert\psi_1+\psi_2\rvert^2$ depends on $\theta$
  continuously via $\cos\theta$.
- Whether a real (but higher-dimensional) alternative could work is a
  genuine, now experimentally settled question. Complex amplitudes are
  confirmed necessary, not merely convenient.
