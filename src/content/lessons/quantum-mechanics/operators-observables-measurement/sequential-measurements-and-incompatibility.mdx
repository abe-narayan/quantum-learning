<LessonHook eyebrow="Certainty, then a factor of two">
Does the order you measure things in change what you find? For ordinary compatible quantities, no. For Z and X on a qubit, yes, and by a specific, checkable amount: a factor of two.
</LessonHook>

## Abstract since the first course, concrete here

"Incompatible observables disturb each
other" has been stated abstractly (uncertainty relations, non-commuting
operators) since the very first course on this platform. This lesson
makes it fully concrete: one specific qubit, three measurements, real
numbers at every step.

## What a shared eigenbasis would have bought

If $Z$ and $X$ shared an eigenbasis, measuring one right after the other
would never change anything. You'd already be sitting in an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term>
of both. Since $[X,Z]\ne0$ (proven earlier this course), no such shared
eigenstate exists, and this lesson traces what breaks, step by step.

## Three measurements, tracked step by step

### Step 1: measure $Z$ on $|0\rangle$

Start in $|0\rangle$, already a $Z$-eigenstate. $P(Z{=}0)=1$, with
certainty. No surprise, and the state doesn't change.

One notational point, since this lesson runs two conventions side by side
and never mixes them. A $Z$ outcome is written as the *bit* a readout would
report, $Z{=}0$ for the eigenvalue $+1$ and $Z{=}1$ for the eigenvalue
$-1$; an $X$ outcome is written as its *eigenvalue*, $X{=}{+}1$ or
$X{=}{-}1$. Both are standard, and $Z$ and $X$ have the identical spectrum
$\{+1,-1\}$ despite the different labels.

That certainty is the thing the rest of the lesson puts at risk, so it is
worth guessing its fate now, before any of the arithmetic is in front of
you.

### Step 2: measure $X$

Rewrite $|0\rangle$ in the $X$-eigenbasis:
$|0\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|+\rangle+|-\rangle)$ (from the Measurement
lesson's $|\pm\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)$
X-basis definition). Using the generalized <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> from
last lesson with the rank-1 projectors $P_+=|+\rangle\langle+|$,
$P_-=|-\rangle\langle-|$:

$$
P(X{=}{+}1) = |\langle+|0\rangle|^2 = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12, \qquad P(X{=}{-}1) = \frac12
$$

Suppose the outcome is $+1$. The state collapses to
$|\psi\rangle=|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$.

### Step 3: measure $Z$ again

$$
P(Z{=}0) = |\langle0|+\rangle|^2 = \left|\frac1{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12, \qquad P(Z{=}1) = \frac12
$$

**The certainty from Step 1 is gone.** Before the $X$ measurement, $Z$
was $100\%$ predictable. After it, $Z$'s outcome is a coin flip. The $X$
measurement did not only add new information; it *erased* the old
certainty.

### Does the order matter?

Run the numbers the other way: measure $X$ first, then $Z$. Starting
from $|0\rangle$, $P(X{=}{+}1)=\tfrac12$ (Step 2's calculation, unchanged
since it didn't use Step 1's outcome). <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">Collapse</Term> to $|+\rangle$, then
$P(Z{=}0)=\tfrac12$ (Step 3's calculation). The *joint* probability of
seeing $Z{=}0$ together with $X{=}{+}1$ depends on which one you measure
first:

$$
P(Z{=}0\text{ first, then }X{=}{+}1) = 1\times\frac12 = \frac12, \qquad P(X{=}{+}1\text{ first, then }Z{=}0) = \frac12\times\frac12 = \frac14
$$

Same two outcomes, same starting state, half the probability depending
on which measurement comes first. This is the concrete, checkable
content of "order matters" for incompatible observables: not a vague
slogan, a factor of two you can verify by direct calculation.

## What incompatible means operationally

This is what "incompatible" means operationally, not just algebraically:
measuring $X$ forces the state into an $X$-eigenstate, and an
$X$-eigenstate is (by the last course's basis-change formulas) an
*equal* superposition in the $Z$-basis, as far from a $Z$-eigenstate as
a normalized state can get. The disturbance is not a technological
limitation or measurement noise. It is what the state has become.

## Redoing step three after the other outcome

**Suppose instead the Step 2 outcome had been $X{=}{-}1$. Redo Step 3.**

Collapse to $|\psi\rangle=|-\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$.

$$
P(Z{=}0) = |\langle0|-\rangle|^2 = \left|\frac1{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12
$$

Exactly the same $50/50$ result. The *specific* $X$ outcome doesn't
matter for how thoroughly it scrambles $Z$; either $X$-eigenstate is
equally far (in this precise sense) from either $Z$-eigenstate.

## No gentler measurement is available

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking the disturbance is somehow avoidable with a "gentler"
measurement.** For incompatible observables, any measurement
that extracts $X$'s definite value necessarily leaves $Z$ maximally
uncertain. This is a feature of the state, not an artifact of a
particular measuring apparatus.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to renormalize (or re-expand) between steps.** Each
measurement's probabilities must be computed using the *post-collapse*
state from the previous step, not the original $|0\rangle$. Step 3's
probabilities come from $|+\rangle$, not from $|0\rangle$ directly.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Redo this entire sequence starting from $|1\rangle$ instead of
   $|0\rangle$ and confirm you still get $50/50$ for the final $Z$
   measurement.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Same result: $P(Z{=}1)=P(Z{=}0)=\tfrac12$ at the third measurement.

   **Step 1.** $|1\rangle$ is a $Z$-eigenstate, so the first $Z$
   measurement returns $Z{=}1$ with certainty and leaves the state alone.

   **Step 2.** Rewrite in the $X$-basis. From
   $|\pm\rangle=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ it follows that
   $|1\rangle=(|+\rangle-|-\rangle)/\sqrt2$, so

   $$
   P(X{=}{+}1) = |\langle+|1\rangle|^2 = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12, \qquad P(X{=}{-}1) = \left|\frac{-1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12
   $$

   The minus sign in the second amplitude disappears under the squared
   magnitude, which is the first thing worth checking: it is a relative
   phase, and it changes nothing about these probabilities.

   **Step 3.** Say the outcome was $+1$, so the state is $|+\rangle$. Then
   $P(Z{=}0)=|\langle0|+\rangle|^2=\tfrac12$ and $P(Z{=}1)=\tfrac12$. Had
   the outcome been $-1$, the state would be $|-\rangle$ and the same two
   numbers come out. The certainty from step 1 is gone either way.

   Why the starting state made no difference is worth saying rather than
   just observing. Every $Z$-eigenstate has equal-magnitude components in
   the $X$-basis, and every $X$-eigenstate has equal-magnitude components
   in the $Z$-basis. That mutual property has a name, *mutually unbiased
   bases*, and it is what makes the $50/50$ appear here regardless of
   which $Z$-eigenstate you start from and regardless of which $X$
   outcome occurs. On the Bloch sphere it is the statement that the $z$
   and $x$ axes are perpendicular, so a state at either $z$ pole projects
   equally onto both $x$ poles.

   The slip to avoid is carrying a sign into a probability. Writing
   $P(X{=}{-}1)=-\tfrac12$ from the $-1/\sqrt2$ amplitude produces a
   negative probability and a total of zero, which the check
   $P(+1)+P(-1)=1$ catches immediately. Amplitudes carry signs and phases;
   probabilities never do.

   </details>

2. If Steps 1 and 3 measured the *same* compatible pair (say, $Z$ then
   $Z$ again, with no $X$ in between), what would Step 3's probabilities
   be, and why?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $P(Z{=}0)=1$ and $P(Z{=}1)=0$: the second reading reproduces the first
   with certainty.

   The reason is the collapse clause plus idempotence. The first
   measurement leaves the state in the eigenspace belonging to the outcome
   it reported, so here the state is $|0\rangle$. Applying the same
   projector again returns it unchanged, $P_0|0\rangle=|0\rangle$, giving
   probability $\||0\rangle\|^2=1$, and the orthogonal projector
   annihilates it, giving exactly zero for the other outcome. Nothing about
   this depends on how much time is allowed to pass, provided the state is
   not evolving in between.

   The contrast with the three-step sequence is the whole lesson in one
   comparison. $Z$, then $Z$: certainty preserved. $Z$, then $X$, then
   $Z$: certainty destroyed, down to a coin flip. The only difference is
   the intervening measurement, and it is incompatible with $Z$. Repeated
   measurement is not gentle *in general*; it is gentle when nothing
   incompatible happens in between.

   Two clarifications keep the claim honest.

   The question says "the same compatible pair," and the strongest version
   of the result covers a genuinely different second observable too. If
   $[Z,B]=0$, then measuring $Z$, then $B$, then $Z$ again also reproduces
   the first $Z$ outcome with certainty, because collapsing onto an
   eigenspace of $B$ within the shared eigenbasis leaves the state an
   eigenstate of $Z$. Compatibility, not sameness, is what the guarantee
   rests on.

   And "with no $X$ in between" is doing real work beyond ruling out a
   measurement. If the system evolves under a Hamiltonian that does not
   commute with $Z$, the state drifts out of the $Z$-eigenstate on its own
   and the second reading is no longer certain, with no measurement
   performed at all. The certainty here belongs to the state staying put,
   which the lesson's setup supplies by measuring immediately.

   </details>

3. Using $[X,Z]=-2iY\ne0$ from earlier in this course, explain in one
   sentence why you should have *expected* Step 3 to be non-trivial,
   before doing any calculation.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $[X,Z]\ne0$ means, by the theorem two lessons back, that $X$
   and $Z$ share no eigenbasis, so the $X$-eigenstate the second
   measurement forces the qubit into cannot also be a $Z$-eigenstate, and
   the third measurement therefore has something genuinely undetermined
   left to decide.

   The logic runs in three linked steps, each already proven.
   Non-commuting implies no shared eigenbasis (the if-and-only-if theorem).
   No shared eigenbasis means every $X$-eigenstate is a nontrivial
   superposition in the $Z$-basis, hence has $\Delta Z>0$. And the
   measurement postulate says step 2 leaves the state in an $X$-eigenstate.
   Chaining them predicts a non-certain third reading without computing a
   single inner product.

   What the commutator does *not* predict is the number. It says the
   answer is not $1$; it does not say it is $\tfrac12$. That comes from
   $X$ and $Z$ being maximally incompatible in the specific sense of
   question 1, and a different pair of non-commuting observables would
   scramble the first outcome less thoroughly. So the commutator is a
   qualitative gate and the explicit calculation supplies the magnitude,
   which is the same division of labour as in the uncertainty relation:
   $[A,B]\ne0$ says a trade-off can exist, and the state decides how
   severe it is.

   The wrong version of this reasoning reaches for the uncertainty
   relation $\Delta X\,\Delta Z\ge\tfrac12|\langle[X,Z]\rangle|$ instead.
   That bound is a statement about two spreads in *one* state, each
   measurable on its own fresh batch of copies, and it is not a claim
   about measurements performed in sequence. Worse, it goes vacuous
   exactly where it is being invoked: in $|0\rangle$,
   $\langle[X,Z]\rangle=-2i\langle Y\rangle=0$, so the bound reads
   $0\ge0$ and forbids nothing at all, while the disturbance is total.
   What is doing the work is the commutator's being nonzero as an
   *operator*, which is a state-independent fact about $X$ and $Z$ and is
   exactly what the shared-eigenbasis theorem consumes.

   </details>

## Summary

- Measuring $Z$, then $X$, then $Z$ again on $|0\rangle$ takes a certain
  outcome ($P(Z{=}0)=1$) to a maximally uncertain one
  ($P(Z{=}0)=\tfrac12$), a fully explicit, numeric demonstration of
  disturbance by an incompatible measurement.
- The result doesn't depend on which $X$-outcome occurred. Both
  $X$-eigenstates are equally "unaligned" with the $Z$-basis.
- Measurement order itself is physically meaningful: $P(Z{=}0\text{ then
  }X{=}{+}1)=\tfrac12$ but $P(X{=}{+}1\text{ then }Z{=}0)=\tfrac14$, the
  same pair of outcomes at different probabilities depending on which
  observable goes first.
- This is the concrete face of the abstract $[X,Z]\ne0$ result proven
  earlier in this course.
