## A consequence, turned into a criterion

From Classical to Quantum proved that commuting observables have no
uncertainty tradeoff: $[A,B]=0$ makes the general bound $\Delta A\,\Delta
B\ge\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ vacuous ($\ge 0$, always true). That
result was a *consequence*. This lesson proves the *reason* why.

## The easy direction, and the one with content

If two operators share a common eigenbasis, applying either one to a basis
vector only rescales it, and rescaling commutes with rescaling in any
order. That direction is easy. The content is in the converse: commuting
is not merely a *consequence* of sharing a basis; it is *equivalent* to
it, so a commutator you can compute in two lines settles a question about
what can be measured together.

## Both directions of the if-and-only-if

### Shared eigenbasis $\implies$ commute

Suppose $\{|e_i\rangle\}$ is an eigenbasis for both $A$ and $B$:
$A|e_i\rangle=a_i|e_i\rangle$, $B|e_i\rangle=b_i|e_i\rangle$. Then for
every basis vector,

$$
AB|e_i\rangle = A(b_i|e_i\rangle) = b_ia_i|e_i\rangle = a_ib_i|e_i\rangle = B(a_i|e_i\rangle) = BA|e_i\rangle
$$

So $AB$ and $BA$ agree on every vector of a basis, hence agree on every
vector (by linearity: any vector is a linear combination of basis
vectors). Therefore $AB=BA$, i.e. $[A,B]=0$.

### Commute $\implies$ shared eigenbasis (nondegenerate case)

Suppose $[A,B]=0$ and $A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle$ with $a$ a **nondegenerate**
<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> (its eigenspace is exactly one-dimensional, spanned only by
$|a\rangle$). Consider $A\big(B|a\rangle\big)$:

$$
A\big(B|a\rangle\big) = (AB)|a\rangle = (BA)|a\rangle = B\big(A|a\rangle\big) = B(a|a\rangle) = a\big(B|a\rangle\big)
$$

using $AB=BA$ in the middle step. So $B|a\rangle$ is *also* an
eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $a$, but the eigenvalue $a$ is
nondegenerate, so its eigenspace is only one-dimensional, meaning
$B|a\rangle$ must be proportional to $|a\rangle$:
$B|a\rangle=\mu|a\rangle$ for some scalar $\mu$. That is the statement
that $|a\rangle$ is *also* an eigenvector of $B$, with eigenvalue $\mu$.
Since this holds for every nondegenerate eigenvector of
$A$, $A$ and $B$ share an eigenbasis wherever $A$'s spectrum is
nondegenerate.

(The degenerate case needs one more step. $B$ restricted to a degenerate
eigenspace of $A$ is itself Hermitian on that subspace, so it has *its
own* <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal eigenbasis</Term> *within* the eigenspace, by the spectral
theorem applied one dimension down. That basis is simultaneously an
eigenbasis for $A$, trivially, and the extra step makes it one for $B$
too. Not spelled out in full here; it is the tool the Complete Sets of
Commuting Observables lesson picks up next.)

## Where the finite-dimensional hypothesis earns its keep

"Compatible observables" and "commuting observables" are the same thing,
now proven rather than merely named. When $[A,B]=0$, in finite dimensions
(or whenever the spectra are purely discrete), a state can be prepared
with perfectly definite values of *both* $A$ and $B$ simultaneously.
Measuring one doesn't disturb the other's value, because they share the
same eigenbasis and a measurement of either just projects onto (a subspace
of) that shared basis. That hypothesis is doing real work: operators with
continuous spectra can commute and still have no shared eigenbasis, since
they may have no eigenvectors in the Hilbert space at all. [Continuous
Spectra and Rigged Hilbert Space](/lessons/quantum-mastery/hilbert-space-and-spectral-theory/continuous-spectra-and-rigged-hilbert-space)
is where that case gets handled honestly.

## Do X and Z share an eigenbasis?

**Do Pauli-$X$ and Pauli-$Z$ share an eigenbasis? Check by computing
$[X,Z]$ directly.**

$$
XZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ZX = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
[X,Z] = XZ-ZX = \begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{pmatrix} = -2i\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = -2i\,Y \ne 0
$$

Since $[X,Z]\ne0$, $X$ and $Z$ do **not** commute, so by this lesson's
theorem they cannot share an eigenbasis, confirmed directly: $Z$'s
eigenbasis is $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, $X$'s is
$\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$, and neither $|0\rangle$ nor $|1\rangle$ is an
eigenvector of $X$ (applying $X$ swaps them instead of rescaling either
one).

## A shared eigenvector is weaker than a shared basis

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming any two Hermitian operators share *some* common eigenvector.**
That's much weaker than sharing a full eigen*basis*, and isn't guaranteed
even then: $[A,B]\ne0$ generically means *no* nonzero vector is an
eigenvector of both.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing "commute" with "equal" or "similar."** $[A,B]=0$ says nothing
about $A$ and $B$ being close to each other as operators. The identity
$I$ commutes with *everything*, including operators wildly different from
itself.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $[Z,X]$ (the operators in the *opposite* order from the worked
   example) and confirm it's the negative of $[X,Z]$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   ZX = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad XZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   $$
   [Z,X] = ZX - XZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix} = 2i\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = 2i\,Y
   $$

   The worked example found $[X,Z]=-2iY$, so indeed $[Z,X]=-[X,Z]$, both
   confirmed against the engine's `commutator(PAULI_Z, PAULI_X)`.

   Antisymmetry is not a fact about the Pauli matrices; it is immediate
   from the definition, $[B,A]=BA-AB=-(AB-BA)=-[A,B]$, and holds for any
   two operators whatsoever. So the arithmetic here is a check on the
   arithmetic, not a discovery. What it is worth checking *for* is the
   two places a sign gets lost: writing $ZX$ where $XZ$ was meant (matrix
   products do not commute, which is the entire subject), and mishandling
   the extraction of $2i$ from $\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}$,
   since $Y$ itself carries $\mp i$ off its diagonal.

   The physically important point is what the sign does *not* change. The
   uncertainty bound is $\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$, with an absolute
   value, so $X$ and $Z$ constrain each other identically whichever is
   named first, as they must: nothing physical distinguishes the two
   orderings of a question about compatibility. Likewise "do they share an
   eigenbasis" is a symmetric question, and both commutators vanish or
   neither does.

   Where the sign is real is in dynamics rather than compatibility. A
   commutator with $H$ sets the *direction* an expectation value moves,
   through Ehrenfest's theorem, and there flipping the order reverses the
   sense of the motion. Compatibility discards the sign; evolution does
   not.

   </details>

2. Two Hermitian operators satisfy $[A,B]=0$. If $A$'s spectrum is
   completely nondegenerate, explain in one sentence why this alone
   guarantees $A$ and $B$ share a full eigenbasis (not just one shared
   eigenvector).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the nondegenerate argument applies to *every* eigenvector of
   $A$, not to a favoured one, and a nondegenerate $A$ on a $d$-dimensional
   space has $d$ of them forming a complete basis, so proving each is also
   an eigenvector of $B$ delivers a full shared eigenbasis rather than a
   single shared vector.

   Spelled out, the derivation above showed that for any eigenvector
   $|a\rangle$ of $A$, commuting makes $B|a\rangle$ another eigenvector of
   $A$ with the same eigenvalue $a$. Nondegeneracy says that eigenspace is
   one-dimensional, so $B|a\rangle$ has nowhere to go but back along
   $|a\rangle$: $B|a\rangle=\mu|a\rangle$. Nothing in that chain singled
   out which eigenvector was chosen, so it runs identically for each of
   $A$'s $d$ eigenvectors, and the spectral theorem guarantees those $d$
   vectors are a basis of the whole space.

   The two hypotheses are both load-bearing, and it is worth seeing what
   each one is preventing.

   Drop commuting, and the very first step fails: $B|a\rangle$ is
   generally not an eigenvector of $A$ at all, and $A$ and $B$ typically
   share no nonzero eigenvector, as the Common Mistakes note warns.

   Drop nondegeneracy, and the argument stalls where the parenthetical in
   the lesson picks it up. $B|a\rangle$ is still an $A$-eigenvector with
   eigenvalue $a$, but now the eigenspace has room, so $B|a\rangle$ can be
   some *other* vector inside it and $|a\rangle$ need not be an eigenvector
   of $B$. The conclusion is recovered, but only after an extra step:
   $B$ restricted to that eigenspace is Hermitian on it, so it has its own
   orthonormal eigenbasis *within* the eigenspace, and any basis of a
   degenerate eigenspace is automatically an $A$-eigenbasis. The shared
   basis exists either way; what nondegeneracy buys is that it is forced
   rather than constructed, and that it is unique up to phases.

   And one hypothesis is hiding in the background of both: finite
   dimensions, or at least purely discrete spectra. Commuting operators
   with continuous spectra can have no eigenvectors in the Hilbert space to
   share, which is why the lesson keeps repeating that qualification rather
   than stating the theorem bare.

   </details>

3. Are $X$ and $Y$ (Pauli matrices) compatible observables? Justify using
   this lesson's criterion, not just a memorized fact.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. They are incompatible, because their commutator is nonzero.

   The criterion this lesson proved is that compatibility is *equivalent*
   to commuting, so the whole question reduces to one product computed
   twice:

   $$
   XY = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad YX = \begin{pmatrix}-i&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   $$
   [X,Y] = XY - YX = \begin{pmatrix}2i&0\\0&-2i\end{pmatrix} = 2i\,Z \ne 0
   $$

   Since $[X,Y]\ne0$, the theorem's "if and only if" says no shared
   eigenbasis exists, so no state has definite values of both $X$ and $Y$
   at once.

   Confirm it independently, since a two-line calculation deserves a
   second opinion. $X$'s eigenvectors are $|\pm\rangle =
   (|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $Y$'s are $(|0\rangle\pm
   i|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Those are four different vectors, and no vector
   appears on both lists: on the Bloch sphere they are the two poles of the
   $x$ axis and the two poles of the $y$ axis, four distinct points. The
   algebraic answer and the geometric one agree.

   The reason the question insists on the criterion rather than the
   memorized fact is that memory generalizes badly here. "Different Pauli
   matrices never commute" is true, and it stops being useful the moment
   the operators are not Paulis. The commutator calculation transfers
   without modification: it settles $Z\otimes I$ against $I\otimes Z$
   (they commute, later this course), $H$ against $L^2$ in the hydrogen
   atom, and any pair a future course puts in front of you. It is also the
   only route that works when the eigenvectors are not already known,
   which is the usual situation.

   One caution worth attaching. $[X,Y]=2iZ$ is nonzero as an *operator*,
   which is what settles compatibility. That is a separate question from
   whether $\langle[X,Y]\rangle$ is nonzero in some particular state, which
   is what enters the uncertainty bound and which does vanish for states on
   the equator of the Bloch sphere. Compatibility is a property of the pair
   of operators; the size of the trade-off is a property of the pair and
   the state together.

   </details>

## Summary

- Two operators share an eigenbasis if and only if they commute, in
  finite dimensions (or whenever the spectra are purely discrete), proven
  in both directions (the nondegenerate direction fully; the degenerate
  direction sketched, completed next lesson).
- "Compatible observables" is exactly synonymous with "commuting
  observables," not a separate physical assumption.
- $X$ and $Z$ (and $X,Y$; $Y,Z$) are incompatible: $[X,Z]=-2iY\ne0$,
  confirmed directly and consistent with their genuinely different
  eigenbases.
