Commuting <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term> operators can always be simultaneously diagonalized. There exists one basis that is an eigenbasis for both operators at once, so a single state can have a perfectly definite value of both observables together.

## Motivation

From Classical to Quantum proved that commuting observables have no
uncertainty tradeoff: $[A,B]=0$ makes the general bound $\Delta A\,\Delta
B\ge\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$ vacuous ($\ge 0$, always true). That
result was a *consequence*. This lesson proves the *reason* why.

## Intuition

If two operators share a common eigenbasis, applying either one to a
basis vector just rescales it, and rescaling commutes with rescaling,
whatever order you do it in. The interesting content is the converse:
commuting is not just a *consequence* of sharing a basis, it's
*equivalent* to it.

## Mathematical Development

### Shared eigenbasis $\implies$ commute

Suppose $\{|e_i\rangle\}$ is an eigenbasis for both $A$ and $B$:
$A|e_i\rangle=a_i|e_i\rangle$, $B|e_i\rangle=b_i|e_i\rangle$. Then for
every basis vector,

$$
AB|e_i\rangle = A(b_i|e_i\rangle) = b_ia_i|e_i\rangle = a_ib_i|e_i\rangle = B(a_i|e_i\rangle) = BA|e_i\rangle
$$

So $AB$ and $BA$ agree on every vector of a basis, hence agree on every
vector (by linearity: any vector is a linear combination of basis
vectors). Therefore $AB=BA$, i.e. $[A,B]=0$.

### Commute $\implies$ shared eigenbasis (nondegenerate case)

Suppose $[A,B]=0$ and $A|a\rangle=a|a\rangle$ with $a$ a **nondegenerate**
<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> (its eigenspace is exactly one-dimensional, spanned only by
$|a\rangle$). Consider $A\big(B|a\rangle\big)$:

$$
A\big(B|a\rangle\big) = (AB)|a\rangle = (BA)|a\rangle = B\big(A|a\rangle\big) = B(a|a\rangle) = a\big(B|a\rangle\big)
$$

using $AB=BA$ in the middle step. So $B|a\rangle$ is *also* an
eigenvector of $A$ with eigenvalue $a$, but the eigenvalue $a$ is
nondegenerate, so its eigenspace is only one-dimensional, meaning
$B|a\rangle$ must be proportional to $|a\rangle$:
$B|a\rangle=\mu|a\rangle$ for some scalar $\mu$. That's exactly the
statement that $|a\rangle$ is *also* an eigenvector of $B$ (with
eigenvalue $\mu$). Since this holds for every nondegenerate eigenvector of
$A$, $A$ and $B$ share an eigenbasis wherever $A$'s spectrum is
nondegenerate.

(The degenerate case needs one more step. $B$ restricted to a degenerate
eigenspace of $A$ is itself Hermitian on that subspace, so it has *its
own* <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal eigenbasis</Term> *within* the eigenspace, by the spectral
theorem applied one dimension down. That basis is simultaneously an
eigenbasis for $A$, trivially, and now the extra step makes it one for
$B$ too. Not spelled out in full here, but this is exactly the tool the
Complete Sets of Commuting Observables lesson uses next.)

## Physical Interpretation

"Compatible observables" and "commuting observables" are the same thing,
now proven rather than merely named. When $[A,B]=0$, a state can be
prepared with perfectly definite values of *both* $A$ and $B$
simultaneously. Measuring one doesn't disturb the other's value, because
they share the same eigenbasis and a measurement of either just projects
onto (a subspace of) that shared basis.

## Worked Example

**Do Pauli-$X$ and Pauli-$Z$ share an eigenbasis? Check by computing
$[X,Z]$ directly.**

$$
XZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ZX = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
[X,Z] = XZ-ZX = \begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\2&0\end{pmatrix} = -2i\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = -2i\,Y \ne 0
$$

Since $[X,Z]\ne0$, $X$ and $Z$ do **not** commute, so by this lesson's
theorem they cannot share an eigenbasis, confirmed directly: $Z$'s
eigenbasis is $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, $X$'s is
$\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$, and neither $|0\rangle$ nor $|1\rangle$ is an
eigenvector of $X$ (applying $X$ swaps them instead of rescaling either
one).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming any two Hermitian operators share *some* common eigenvector.**
That's much weaker than sharing a full eigen*basis*, and isn't guaranteed
even then: $[A,B]\ne0$ generically means *no* nonzero vector is an
eigenvector of both.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing "commute" with "equal" or "similar."** $[A,B]=0$ says nothing
about $A$ and $B$ being close to each other as operators. The identity
$I$ commutes with *everything*, including operators wildly different from
itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Two operators share an eigenbasis if and only if they commute, proven
  in both directions (the nondegenerate direction fully; the degenerate
  direction sketched, completed next lesson).
- "Compatible observables" is exactly synonymous with "commuting
  observables," not a separate physical assumption.
- $X$ and $Z$ (and $X,Y$; $Y,Z$) are incompatible: $[X,Z]=-2iY\ne0$,
  confirmed directly and consistent with their genuinely different
  eigenbases.
