## When one eigenvalue names two states

The very first worked example of this course used $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$,
an operator whose eigenvalue $1$ doesn't tell you which of two
orthogonal states you're in, only that you're *somewhere* in their <Term id="span">span</Term>.
If $N$ were the only observable you ever had access to, you'd have no way
to fully label the state. This lesson builds the fix: find a second
observable that commutes with $N$ (so it's simultaneously measurable,
last lesson's theorem) and takes *different* values on the degenerate
eigenvectors.

## Which shelf, and which position on it

Think of $N$'s eigenvalue as a coarse label, "which shelf," and a
second, compatible observable as a finer label, "which position on the
shelf." Together, the two labels uniquely address every state, even
though neither one alone does the job.

## Resolving a degeneracy, and what completeness means

### Resolving a degeneracy

Take $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ from the first lesson, with its
2-dimensional degenerate eigenspace $\mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$
for eigenvalue $1$. Any **diagonal** matrix automatically commutes with
$N$ (diagonal matrices always commute with each other: multiplying two
diagonal matrices in either order just multiplies corresponding diagonal
entries). So take

$$
M = \mathrm{diag}(5, -5, 7)
$$

$[N,M]=0$ trivially, so $N$ and $M$ are compatible (last lesson). And $M$
takes a *different* value on each of $N$'s degenerate eigenvectors:
$M|0\rangle=5|0\rangle$, $M|1\rangle=-5|1\rangle$. The pair of eigenvalues
$(N,M)$ now uniquely labels every basis state:

| State | $N$ | $M$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\lvert0\rangle$ | 1 | 5 |
| $\lvert1\rangle$ | 1 | $-5$ |
| $\lvert2\rangle$ | 2 | 7 |

Read the rows, not the columns: no two rows carry the same pair, which is
what "uniquely labels" means. The $N$ column on its own repeats, so $N$
cannot tell $|0\rangle$ from $|1\rangle$. The $M$ column happens not to
repeat, which makes $M$ alone sufficient here, but that is an accident of
a three-dimensional toy example and nothing to build on. What generalizes
is the *pair* $(N,M)$ working as a joint label, because in real physical
systems there is usually no single observable that suffices by itself.

### What "complete" means

A set of mutually commuting observables $\{A,B,C,\ldots\}$ is a
**complete set of commuting observables** (a CSCO) if their *joint*
eigenvalues $(a,b,c,\ldots)$ uniquely label every basis state: no two
distinct basis states share the same full tuple of eigenvalues. Every
observable in the set is compatible with every other (all commute
pairwise, so the last lesson guarantees a shared eigenbasis exists for
the whole set at once), and together they leave no degeneracy
unresolved.

## Where quantum numbers come from

This is the structure behind labeling real quantum states with
multiple **quantum numbers**. A preview, not derived here: the hydrogen
atom's energy alone doesn't uniquely specify a state (many different
orbital shapes share the same energy). It takes a complete set,
conventionally $\{H, L^2, L_z\}$ (energy, total angular momentum
squared, and one component of angular momentum), to fully label a state
as $|n,l,m\rangle$. That's a future course's derivation; the *reason* it
needs three compatible observables, not one, is this lesson's
idea.

## Confirming two observables commute by hand

**Confirm $[N,M]=0$ for the $N,M$ above by direct matrix multiplication.**

$$
NM = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5&0&0\\0&-5&0\\0&0&7\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5&0&0\\0&-5&0\\0&0&14\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
MN = \begin{pmatrix}5&0&0\\0&-5&0\\0&0&7\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5&0&0\\0&-5&0\\0&0&14\end{pmatrix}
$$

$NM=MN$, so $[N,M]=0$, as claimed. Diagonal matrices commuting
is not a coincidence specific to this example; it holds for *any* two
diagonal matrices, since multiplying diagonal matrices only ever
multiplies matching diagonal entries, an operation that plainly doesn't
care about order.

### A non-diagonal partner, worked in full

Every $M$ so far has been diagonal in the same basis as $N$, which is
what made $[N,M]=0$ automatic. Whether being diagonal was doing the real
work is a separate question. Consider

$$
\tilde M = \begin{pmatrix}0&5&0\\5&0&0\\0&0&7\end{pmatrix}
$$

which is Hermitian but plainly not diagonal: it mixes $|0\rangle$ and
$|1\rangle$.

  options={[

  correctValue="block"
  explanation="It holds. The identity worked out just below, (NM̃ − M̃N)ᵢⱼ = (nᵢ − nⱼ)M̃ᵢⱼ, says a commuting M̃ may carry a nonzero entry wherever nᵢ = nⱼ. The only off-diagonal entries M̃ uses sit in the |0⟩,|1⟩ block, where N's eigenvalues are both 1, so those terms vanish. **Confirm it by direct multiplication.** $N\tilde M$ scales the *rows*
of $\tilde M$ by $(1,1,2)$; $\tilde M N$ scales the *columns* by
$(1,1,2)$:

$$
N\tilde M = \begin{pmatrix}0&5&0\\5&0&0\\0&0&14\end{pmatrix} = \tilde M N
$$

The two off-diagonal $5$s sit in rows and columns where $N$'s
eigenvalue is the same ($1$ and $1$), so scaling rows or columns makes
no difference there, and the corner entry picks up the factor $2$
either way. So $[N,\tilde M]=0$.

That row-versus-column reading is worth stating once as a formula, since
it settles every such question at a glance. For any diagonal
$N=\mathrm{diag}(n_1,\ldots,n_d)$ and any matrix $M$, left-multiplying
scales row $i$ by $n_i$ and right-multiplying scales column $j$ by $n_j$,
so

$$
(NM-MN)_{ij} = (n_i - n_j)\,M_{ij}
$$

entry by entry. Commuting with $N$ therefore permits $M_{ij}\neq0$
exactly where $n_i=n_j$: on the diagonal always, and off it only inside a
degenerate block. $\tilde M$ uses that allowance and nothing more.

**Does $\tilde M$ resolve the degeneracy?** Its upper block is
$5\sigma_x$, with eigenvectors and eigenvalues

$$
|\pm\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \tilde M|\pm\rangle = \pm5|\pm\rangle, \qquad \tilde M|2\rangle = 7|2\rangle
$$

(check: $\tilde M(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2 = (5|1\rangle+5|0\rangle)/\sqrt2 = +5|+\rangle$).
The joint labels are again unique:

| State | $N$ | $\tilde M$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\lvert+\rangle$ | 1 | 5 |
| $\lvert-\rangle$ | 1 | $-5$ |
| $\lvert2\rangle$ | 2 | 7 |

So $\{N,\tilde M\}$ is a perfectly good CSCO. The difference from the
diagonal example is *which* shared eigenbasis the pair selects:
$\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle,|2\rangle\}$ rather than
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$. Both are eigenbases of $N$
(any basis of a degenerate eigenspace is), and the second observable is
what picks one out. That is the CSCO idea in its full form: the set does
not merely label a basis you already had; it *determines* the basis.

## A compatible observable that resolves nothing

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any second observable resolves a degeneracy.** It must both
commute with the first *and* take different values on the degenerate
eigenvectors. A compatible observable that happens to be constant across
the whole degenerate eigenspace resolves nothing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking a CSCO must consist of exactly two observables.** The number
needed depends entirely on how much degeneracy there is to resolve. One
suffices for a fully nondegenerate spectrum, and real physical systems
(like hydrogen) can need three or more.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1)$ (every eigenvalue equal, total
   degeneracy), what is the smallest additional information needed to
   fully label a basis state? Is $N$ itself useful at all here?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   One extra observable suffices, provided its three eigenvalues are
   distinct. And no, $N$ contributes nothing: it is the identity in
   disguise, so it takes the same value on every state and separates
   none of them.

   $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,1)=I$. Its only eigenvalue is $1$, with a
   three-dimensional eigenspace, namely the entire space. Measuring it
   returns $1$ with certainty on any state whatsoever and leaves the state
   untouched, since the projector is $I$. Knowing the outcome tells you
   what you already knew.

   To label a basis of $\mathbb{C}^3$ you need the joint eigenvalues to
   take three distinct values, and a single observable can do that on its
   own if its spectrum is fully nondegenerate: $M=\mathrm{diag}(5,-5,7)$
   gives $|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|2\rangle$ the labels $5,-5,7$ and the job is
   done. So the answer to "smallest" is one observable, and $\{N,M\}$ is a
   CSCO only in the trivial sense that $\{M\}$ already was.

   Two things this brings out.

   The identity commutes with everything, so it can be added to any set of
   commuting observables without breaking commutativity, and it never
   advances completeness. Compatibility is cheap; *resolving power* is what
   costs something. A useful second observable has to satisfy two separate
   conditions, and the Common Mistakes warning above names them both: it
   must commute with the first, and it must take different values across
   the degenerate eigenspace. $I$ passes the first and fails the second.

   And "one observable suffices" is a statement about mathematics, not
   about laboratories. A nondegenerate $M$ always exists on a
   finite-dimensional space, but there is no guarantee that any *physically
   measurable* quantity of a given system has a fully nondegenerate
   spectrum. Hydrogen is the standard case: no single measurable observable
   labels its states, so the CSCO $\{H,L^2,L_z\}$ grows to three, which is
   the next question.

   </details>

2. Give a different diagonal $M'$ (not equal to the one above) that also
   resolves $N$'s degeneracy, and confirm it works using the table method
   from the worked example.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $M'=\mathrm{diag}(3,-1,9)$ works, and so does the more surprising
   $M''=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,0)$. Any diagonal matrix whose first two entries
   differ will do.

   Take $M'=\mathrm{diag}(3,-1,9)$ first. It is diagonal, so
   $[N,M']=0$ automatically, and the engine's commutator confirms it. The
   joint labels, read as rows:

   $|0\rangle$ carries $(N,M')=(1,3)$; $|1\rangle$ carries $(1,-1)$;
   $|2\rangle$ carries $(2,9)$. No two rows agree, so the pair is a CSCO.

   Now $M''=\mathrm{diag}(0,1,0)$, which is worth more attention because
   it looks as though it should fail. Its own spectrum is degenerate:
   eigenvalue $0$ on both $|0\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$. But the labels are
   $(1,0)$, $(1,1)$ and $(2,0)$, and those three pairs are still distinct.
   $M''$ separates $|0\rangle$ from $|1\rangle$, which is the only job
   $N$ left undone, and $N$ separates $|0\rangle$ from $|2\rangle$, which
   is the only job $M''$ left undone. Between them nothing is unresolved.

   That is the general condition, and it is weaker than it first looks.
   $M'$ does not need three distinct eigenvalues. It needs to distinguish
   states *within each of $N$'s degenerate eigenspaces*, and it may freely
   repeat values across different eigenspaces, because $N$ has already
   separated those. Completeness is a property of the set, not a
   requirement imposed on each member.

   The failure case is the one the lesson's warning describes: any
   $M'$ constant on $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, such as
   $\mathrm{diag}(4,4,9)$, commutes with $N$ perfectly well and resolves
   nothing, leaving both states labelled $(1,4)$. Commuting was never the
   hard part.

   </details>

3. Explain, in your own words, why $\{H,L^2,L_z\}$ needing three
   observables (rather than one) to label a hydrogen state is consistent
   with this lesson's central idea, even though you haven't derived
   $L^2$ or $L_z$ yet.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because hydrogen's energy is massively degenerate, and this lesson's
   idea is precisely that you keep adding compatible observables until the
   joint labels become unique. Three is simply how many that takes here.

   The structure is the same as $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$, scaled up. A
   hydrogen energy level $E_n$ is a single eigenvalue whose eigenspace has
   dimension $n^2$: at $n=3$ that is nine distinct states behind one
   number. $H$ alone therefore locates a state only within a
   nine-dimensional subspace, exactly as $N$'s eigenvalue $1$ located a
   state only within a two-dimensional one.

   Adding $L^2$ splits that eigenspace by orbital shape, into the
   $s,p,d,\ldots$ families, and it is a legitimate partner because it
   commutes with $H$. That still leaves degeneracy: at $n=3$ the $d$
   family has five states sharing both $E_3$ and the same $L^2$
   eigenvalue. Adding $L_z$, which commutes with both, separates those
   five by orientation. Only after the third observable is every state
   uniquely labelled, and the familiar $|n,l,m\rangle$ notation is the
   joint eigenvalue tuple written down.

   Two points make this more than an analogy.

   The count is not a convention. Three is forced by how much degeneracy
   there is and by which compatible observables the system's symmetry
   makes available; a different potential with less symmetry would need
   fewer, and adding a magnetic field, which is what Zeeman did, lifts some
   of the degeneracy so that fewer labels are needed to say the same thing.
   The Common Mistakes note above makes the general point: the size of a
   CSCO is set by the system, not fixed at two.

   And every member has to commute with every other, pairwise, or the
   labels would not coexist. $\{H,L^2,L_z\}$ satisfies that; $\{H,L_x,L_z\}$
   would not, because $L_x$ and $L_z$ fail to commute, and no state has
   definite values of both. So the choice of *which* three is constrained
   too, not just how many, and that is the previous lesson's theorem doing
   work inside this one.

   </details>

## Summary

- A degenerate eigenvalue only locates a state within a subspace; a
  second, compatible (commuting) observable that varies across that
  subspace can resolve the ambiguity.
- A complete set of commuting observables (CSCO) is a mutually-commuting
  collection whose *joint* eigenvalues uniquely label every basis state.
- This is the general mechanism behind multi-quantum-number labeling in
  real systems, previewed here and derived concretely in later courses.
