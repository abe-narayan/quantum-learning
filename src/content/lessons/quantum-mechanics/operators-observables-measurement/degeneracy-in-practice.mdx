## Running the machinery end to end

The target is a Bell state, built originally in the Quantum Gates &
Circuits course. There is no new physics in this lesson. It runs this
course's machinery end to end on a system you already know, which is the
best way to find out whether the machinery makes sense to you.

## Look at one qubit and degeneracy appears

$\hat Z$ on a single qubit has two nondegenerate eigenvalues, no
degeneracy to worry about. Move to *two* qubits, and an <Term id="observable">observable</Term> that
only looks at *one* of them ($\hat Z$ on qubit 0, ignoring qubit 1
entirely) automatically becomes degenerate on the 4-dimensional combined
space: two different values of qubit 1 give the *same* eigenvalue. The
degeneracy arises here for the most ordinary reason there is, an
observable that ignores part of the system, rather than from a contrived
construction.

## Two observables, then two measurements in turn

### Building the observables

Let $Z_0=Z\otimes I$ (Pauli-$Z$ on qubit 0, identity on qubit 1) and
$Z_1=I\otimes Z$ (identity on qubit 0, Pauli-$Z$ on qubit 1), both
constructed directly via this platform's existing `Matrix.tensor()`, no
new engine code needed. Their eigenvalues on the four basis states:

| State | $Z_0$ | $Z_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| $\lvert00\rangle$ | $+1$ | $+1$ |
| $\lvert01\rangle$ | $+1$ | $-1$ |
| $\lvert10\rangle$ | $-1$ | $+1$ |
| $\lvert11\rangle$ | $-1$ | $-1$ |

$Z_0$ alone is degenerate ($+1$ on both $|00\rangle,|01\rangle$); so is
$Z_1$. But the *pair* $(Z_0,Z_1)$ gives four distinct tuples, a CSCO for
this space, of the kind Complete Sets of Commuting Observables described
abstractly.

Written out as matrices, both operators are diagonal, and the degeneracy
is visible as repeated entries down the diagonal:

Each matrix repeats each of its two eigenvalues twice, which is the
degeneracy stated in numbers: $Z_0$ cannot tell $|00\rangle$ from
$|01\rangle$, because it assigns them the same diagonal entry. What makes
the pair complete is that they repeat in *different patterns*, $(+,+,-,-)$
against $(+,-,+,-)$, so reading both diagonals down together gives four
distinct labels.

### Why they commute

Operators acting on *different* tensor factors always commute: using
$(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)$,

$$
Z_0Z_1 = (Z\otimes I)(I\otimes Z) = (ZI)\otimes(IZ) = Z\otimes Z = (IZ)\otimes(ZI) = (I\otimes Z)(Z\otimes I) = Z_1Z_0
$$

so $[Z_0,Z_1]=0$, confirmed by computation rather than asserted. Nothing
in that line used any property of $Z$: $A\otimes I$ and $I\otimes B$
commute for *any* $A,B$, because they act on independent subsystems.

### Measuring $Z_0$ on an entangled state

Take the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+
|11\rangle)$. In amplitude order $(|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,
|11\rangle)$, this is $\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0,0,\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)$.
$Z_0$'s eigenvalue $+1$ projector is $P_+=\mathrm{projectorOntoSubspace}
(\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle\})$ (degenerate, spans two basis states). By
the generalized <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term>,

$$
P(Z_0{=}{+}1) = \left|\frac1{\sqrt2}\right|^2 + |0|^2 = \frac12
$$

Suppose the outcome is $+1$. $P_+|\Phi^+\rangle$ keeps only the
$|00\rangle,|01\rangle$ components: $\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0,0,0\right)$,
whose norm is $1/\sqrt2$ rather than $1$, since half the probability
belonged to the outcome that did not occur. Dividing through by that norm
<Term id="normalization">renormalizes</Term> the state to exactly $|00\rangle$.

### Then measuring $Z_1$

On the now-collapsed $|00\rangle$, $Z_1$'s eigenvalue is $+1$ with
**certainty** ($|00\rangle$ is already a $Z_1$-eigenstate). The full pair
$(Z_0,Z_1)=(+1,+1)$ now uniquely identifies the state as $|00\rangle$,
which is the CSCO machinery of this course doing on a real two-qubit
state what the abstract argument said it would.

## Perfect correlation, seen through the formalism

This *is* the Bell state's famous perfect correlation, seen through this
course's formalism instead of asserted as a special fact: measuring
qubit 0 as $0$ forces qubit 1 to also be $0$, because the only
$|\Phi^+\rangle$-component consistent with $Z_0{=}{+}1$ is $|00\rangle$.
There is no $|01\rangle$ component in $|\Phi^+\rangle$ at all. The
"spooky" correlation from Bell States and Entanglement is this lesson's
projector-and-collapse mechanics applied to an entangled input, with no
extra ingredient.

## The same measurement with the other outcome

**Suppose the $Z_0$ outcome had instead been $-1$. What state results,
and what does $Z_1$ then give with certainty?**

$P_-$ keeps the $|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ components of $|\Phi^+\rangle$:
only $|11\rangle$ is present, so the state collapses to $|11\rangle$
directly. Measuring $Z_1$ on $|11\rangle$ then gives $-1$ with certainty.
The two qubits are perfectly correlated again, at $Z_0=Z_1=-1$ this time,
which is what $|\Phi^+\rangle$ containing only the $|00\rangle$ and
$|11\rangle$ terms amounts to: whichever branch the first measurement
selects, the second one is already decided.

## Commuting is a fact about operators, not states

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking $Z_0$ and $Z_1$ commuting is special to the Bell state.**
It's a property of the *operators* ($A\otimes I$ vs. $I\otimes B$),
independent of which state they're applied to. The Bell state is an
interesting *input* to observables that were already compatible before it
arrived.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting $Z_0$ alone to fully determine the state.** It's degenerate
on this 4-dimensional space by construction. Only the *pair*
$(Z_0,Z_1)$ is a complete set here.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Confirm $Z_0$'s $-1$-eigenspace projector, applied to
   $|\Phi^+\rangle$, gives exactly $|11\rangle$ after normalizing (redo
   the calculation from the worked example explicitly).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $Z_0$'s $-1$ eigenspace is spanned by $|10\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$,
   the two basis states with qubit 0 in $|1\rangle$, so

   $$
   P_- = |10\rangle\langle10| + |11\rangle\langle11|
   $$

   In amplitude order $(|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle)$ the
   Bell state is $\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0,0,\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)$, and
   $P_-$ keeps only the last two entries:

   $$
   P_-|\Phi^+\rangle = \left(0,\,0,\,0,\,\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|11\rangle
   $$

   $$
   P(Z_0{=}{-}1) = \left\|P_-|\Phi^+\rangle\right\|^2 = \frac12, \qquad \frac{P_-|\Phi^+\rangle}{\|P_-|\Phi^+\rangle\|} = \frac{(1/\sqrt2)|11\rangle}{1/\sqrt2} = |11\rangle
   $$

   confirmed against the platform's `projectorOntoSubspace` applied to
   $\{|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}$.

   Notice that the projector is rank $2$, so the outcome is degenerate and
   nothing in the general rule promises a single basis state at the end.
   What produced one is a property of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ rather than of the
   measurement: the state simply has no $|10\rangle$ component to keep. A
   rank-2 projector applied to a state with weight on both of its
   directions would leave a genuine superposition, exactly as the previous
   lesson's $N{=}1$ outcome did.

   The renormalization is the step most often skipped, and here it is
   doing something visible. $P_-|\Phi^+\rangle$ has norm $1/\sqrt2$, not
   $1$, because half the probability belonged to the outcome that did not
   occur. Dividing restores a unit vector, and the amplitude on
   $|11\rangle$ goes from $1/\sqrt2$ to $1$: from "half likely" to
   "certain," which is precisely the information the measurement supplied.

   </details>

2. For the *other* Bell state $|\Psi^+\rangle=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}
   (|01\rangle+|10\rangle)$, find $P(Z_0{=}{+}1)$ and the resulting
   collapsed state.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $P(Z_0{=}{+}1)=\tfrac12$, and the state collapses to $|01\rangle$.

   In amplitude order, $|\Psi^+\rangle =
   \left(0,\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2},0\right)$. The $+1$ projector
   $P_+=|00\rangle\langle00|+|01\rangle\langle01|$ keeps the first two
   entries:

   $$
   P_+|\Psi^+\rangle = \left(0,\,\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\,0,\,0\right), \qquad P(Z_0{=}{+}1) = \left|\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac12
   $$

   and normalizing gives $|01\rangle$. Measuring $Z_1$ afterwards then
   returns $-1$ with certainty, since $|01\rangle$ is a $Z_1$-eigenstate
   with qubit 1 in $|1\rangle$.

   The comparison with $|\Phi^+\rangle$ is the substance. The single-qubit
   statistics are identical: both Bell states give $P(Z_0{=}{+}1)=\tfrac12$,
   so measuring qubit 0 alone cannot tell them apart, and neither can
   measuring qubit 1 alone. What differs is the *correlation*.
   $|\Phi^+\rangle$ collapses to $|00\rangle$ or $|11\rangle$, so the two
   readings always agree; $|\Psi^+\rangle$ collapses to $|01\rangle$ or
   $|10\rangle$, so they always disagree. Perfect correlation against
   perfect anticorrelation, invisible in either qubit's own marginal
   distribution.

   That is worth holding onto because it is what "entanglement is not in
   either part" means concretely. Both states look maximally random from
   qubit 0's point of view, and the difference between them lives entirely
   in the joint outcomes. The $(Z_0,Z_1)$ pair is a CSCO for this space, so
   its joint eigenvalues do distinguish the four basis states; what a
   single member of the set cannot do is distinguish the two entangled
   *superpositions* built from them.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why $X_0=X\otimes I$ and $Z_1=I\otimes Z$ commute even
   though $X$ and $Z$ individually do not. Then decide whether
   $\{X_0,Z_1\}$ is a CSCO for this four-dimensional space, and if it is,
   name the shared eigenbasis it selects.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   They commute because they act on *different* tensor factors, and yes,
   $\{X_0,Z_1\}$ is a CSCO, selecting the basis
   $\{|+\rangle|0\rangle,\,|+\rangle|1\rangle,\,|-\rangle|0\rangle,\,
   |-\rangle|1\rangle\}$.

   **Why they commute.** Use $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D)=(AC)\otimes(BD)$,
   which multiplies the two factors independently:

   $$
   X_0Z_1 = (X\otimes I)(I\otimes Z) = (XI)\otimes(IZ) = X\otimes Z = (IX)\otimes(ZI) = (I\otimes Z)(X\otimes I) = Z_1X_0
   $$

   The awkward pair $X$ and $Z$ never meet. On qubit 0 the product is
   $X$ against $I$, and on qubit 1 it is $I$ against $Z$; multiplying by
   the identity commutes with everything. Their failure to commute is a
   fact about two operators acting on the *same* factor, which is a
   situation this construction never creates. The engine's commutator
   confirms $[X_0,Z_1]=0$ and $[X_0,Z_0]\ne0$, the second being the same
   two matrices reunited on one qubit.

   **Why the pair is complete.** $X_0$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$, with
   eigenvectors $|\pm\rangle$ on qubit 0 and qubit 1 unconstrained, so each
   eigenspace is two-dimensional and $X_0$ alone is degenerate. $Z_1$ is
   degenerate in the same way, splitting on qubit 1 instead. Take the joint
   labels:

   $|+\rangle|0\rangle$ carries $(+1,+1)$; $|+\rangle|1\rangle$ carries
   $(+1,-1)$; $|-\rangle|0\rangle$ carries $(-1,+1)$; $|-\rangle|1\rangle$
   carries $(-1,-1)$. Four distinct pairs across four basis states, so no
   degeneracy survives and the set is complete.

   The general lesson is that the tensor structure supplies compatible
   observables for free, one per subsystem, and each is automatically
   degenerate on the whole space because it ignores the rest of it. That is
   the most ordinary source of degeneracy there is, and assembling one
   observable per factor into a CSCO is the standard cure. It is also why
   $\{X_0,Z_1\}$ selects a *different* basis from $\{Z_0,Z_1\}$: the
   observables you choose to measure decide which basis the labels refer
   to, and both are legitimate complete sets for the same space.

   </details>

## Summary

- $Z_0=Z\otimes I$ and $Z_1=I\otimes Z$ each have degenerate eigenvalues
  alone, but form a complete set of commuting observables together.
- Operators on different tensor factors always commute, a general,
  directly-verified fact, not specific to this example.
- Measuring $Z_0$ on the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$ and collapsing gives
  exactly the correlated outcome Bell States and Entanglement already
  described qualitatively, now derived from this course's own
  machinery.
