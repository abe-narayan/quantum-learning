## The one relation with no commutator to lean on

Every uncertainty relation built so far, $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$,
the general $\Delta A\Delta B\ge\tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$, came from
a commutator between two *operators*. Energy-time uncertainty,
$\Delta E\,\Delta t\gtrsim\hbar/2$, is quoted constantly but can't come
from that same route: in ordinary quantum mechanics (fixed Hamiltonian,
no relativity), **time is a parameter, not an operator**. There's no
$\hat t$ to take a commutator with $\hat H$. This lesson derives the
relation honestly, from a different starting point.

## Ask how long a change takes instead

Instead of asking "how spread out is time," ask a question that *does*
make sense: "how long does it take for this state to visibly change?"
Define that as a *timescale*, not an uncertainty in an operator's
eigenvalues, and the energy-time relation falls out of tools you already
have: Ehrenfest's theorem and the ordinary operator uncertainty bound.

## A timescale first, then the bound that follows

### A timescale for any observable

For an <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $A$ with no explicit time dependence, define its
**characteristic evolution timescale** in state $|\psi(t)\rangle$ as

$$
\Delta t_A \equiv \frac{\Delta A}{\left|\,d\langle A\rangle/dt\,\right|}
$$

In words: "how long it takes $\langle A\rangle$ to change by one
standard deviation, at its current rate of change." A large $\Delta t_A$
means $\langle A\rangle$ is changing slowly (relative to its own spread);
a small one means it's changing fast.

### Applying the general uncertainty bound to $H$ and $A$

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Apply the general operator uncertainty relation from Expectation Values and Uncertainty to H and this same A.">
$$
\Delta E\,\Delta A \ge \frac12\big|\langle[H,A]\rangle\big|
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Ehrenfest's theorem (Wave Packet Dynamics and Dispersion) relates the same commutator directly to A's rate of change, for any observable with no explicit time dependence.">
$$
\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,A]\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \big|\langle[H,A]\rangle\big| = \hbar\left|\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}\right|
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute this into the uncertainty bound from the first step.">
$$
\Delta E\,\Delta A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}\right|
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Divide both sides by |d⟨A⟩/dt| and recognize the definition of Δt_A from above.">
$$
\Delta E\,\Delta t_A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Derived, not asserted, and explicit about what $\Delta t$ means here: not
an uncertainty in a measured time, but the shortest timescale on which
*any* observable of the system can change appreciably.

## Sharp energy means everything moves slowly

A state with a very sharply defined energy ($\Delta E$ small) must have
*every* observable's expectation value changing slowly, a large
$\Delta t_A$ for every choice of $A$. Taken to its limit, this is
the stationary-state result already known from two earlier courses:

- **From Classical to Quantum**'s Stationary States lesson: an energy
  <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> has $\Delta E=0$ exactly.
- **This relation**, then, demands $\Delta t_A=\infty$ for *every*
  observable $A$: $\langle A\rangle$ never changes at all.
- **Directly confirmed**: an energy eigenstate's $|\psi(t)|^2$ (and hence
  every expectation value built from it) is exactly time-independent,
  proven in both the finite-dimensional and continuous-position settings
  already. The energy-time relation and the stationary-state result are
  not two separate facts. One is the extreme limit of the other.

A short lifetime forces a smeared energy level, and that smearing shows up
in what gets emitted: the excited level broadens into a band, and the
emission line built from it broadens with it. This is the natural
linewidth named in the sodium-D-line photo above.

The numbers are unforgiving in both directions. Sodium's excited $3p$
state lives about $\tau=16$ ns, giving
$\Delta E \gtrsim \hbar/(2\tau) \approx 3\times10^{-27}$ J, or roughly
$2\times10^{-8}$ eV. Read as a frequency spread that is $\Delta E/h
\approx 5$ MHz, and the measured natural linewidth, the emission line's
full width at half maximum $1/(2\pi\tau)\approx10$ MHz, is exactly twice
it. The bound fixes the scale; the factor of two comes from the
Lorentzian shape an exponentially decaying state emits, and $\Delta E$
here is the half-width. Against an optical frequency near
$5\times10^{14}$ Hz, that $10$ MHz width makes the line sharp to about
two parts in $10^{8}$. A state that lived a thousand times more briefly would
be a thousand times blurrier. Spectroscopic sharpness and long excited
lifetimes are the same fact.

## Testing the bound on explicit numbers

**A state has $\Delta E = 0.5$ (natural units, $\hbar=1$) and, for some
observable $A$, $\Delta A = 2$ with $|d\langle A\rangle/dt| = 3$. Is the
energy-time bound satisfied?**

$$
\Delta t_A = \frac{\Delta A}{|d\langle A\rangle/dt|} = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667
$$

$$
\Delta E\,\Delta t_A = 0.5 \times 0.667 \approx 0.333
$$

Compare to $\hbar/2=0.5$: $0.333 < 0.5$. **This would violate the
bound**, so no valid <Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term> can have exactly these three
numbers together. Rearranged, $\Delta E=0.5$ forces
$\Delta t_A = \Delta A/|d\langle A\rangle/dt| \ge \hbar/(2\Delta E) = 1$
for every observable, and the stated $\Delta t_A = 0.667$ falls short of
that. The relation constrains which combinations of these quantities can
occur at all; it is not merely an arithmetic check performed after the
fact.

## A timescale, not a measured time

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\Delta t$ as "the uncertainty in a measured time," the way
$\Delta x$ is the uncertainty in a measured position.** Time is never a
measurement outcome with a probability distribution in this framework.
$\Delta t_A$ is a *timescale derived from* $A$'s dynamics, which is a
different kind of quantity entirely.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Trying to derive this the same way as $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$,
via $[\hat E,\hat t]$.** There is no $\hat t$ operator to commute with
anything in ordinary (non-relativistic, fixed-Hamiltonian) quantum
mechanics. This is why the derivation had to go through
Ehrenfest's theorem instead.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For a stationary state, use this lesson's relation directly (not the
   stationary-state result itself) to argue that $\Delta t_A$ must be
   infinite for every $A$, given $\Delta E=0$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Put $\Delta E = 0$ into the bound and there is nowhere for a finite
   $\Delta t_A$ to hide:

   $$
   \Delta E\,\Delta t_A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0\cdot\Delta t_A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}
   $$

   The left side is zero for any finite $\Delta t_A$, and zero is not
   greater than $\hbar/2$. So no finite value is consistent with the bound,
   and $\Delta t_A=\infty$ is the only option left. Since $A$ was
   arbitrary, this holds for every observable at once.

   It is cleaner, and less reliant on infinity as an arithmetic object, to
   run the argument one step earlier, at the line before the division:

   $$
   \Delta E\,\Delta A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}\right|
   $$

   With $\Delta E=0$ the left side is exactly zero, so
   $|d\langle A\rangle/dt|=0$ for every $A$ with finite spread. That is
   the substantive conclusion: no expectation value changes, ever. The
   infinite $\Delta t_A$ is then just $\Delta A/0$ read as a definition,
   and this route never divides by zero.

   The question's parenthetical is the point of the exercise, so it is
   worth saying what it is holding back. The stationary-state result from
   two courses ago proves the same thing from a completely different
   direction: an energy eigenstate evolves as
   $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$, a global phase, which cancels out of every
   squared magnitude. Arriving at the same conclusion from an inequality
   about spreads, having never mentioned the evolution operator, is a
   genuine consistency check across three separate derivations in three
   separate courses. Quoting the earlier result here would have skipped
   the check.

   One thing not to over-read. $\Delta t_A=\infty$ says the state's
   *expectation values* are frozen, which is what "stationary" has meant
   throughout. The state vector itself keeps accruing phase the whole time.

   </details>

2. A state has $\Delta E=2$ (natural units, $\hbar=1$). Find the minimum
   possible value of $\Delta t_A$ for any observable $A$ in this state.
   Then suppose that for one particular observable $\Delta A = 0.5$: what
   is the largest $|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$ the bound permits, and is that
   ceiling a fact about the state, about that observable, or about both?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\Delta t_A \ge 0.25$, and with $\Delta A = 0.5$ the rate is capped at
   $|d\langle A\rangle/dt| \le 2$. The first number is a fact about the
   state alone; the second needs the observable too.

   Rearranging the bound gives

   $$
   \Delta t_A \ge \frac{\hbar}{2\,\Delta E} = \frac{1}{2\times2} = 0.25
   $$

   and since $\Delta E$ is a property of the state, this floor applies to
   *every* observable of that state simultaneously. Nothing about the
   system can shift, relative to its own spread, faster than that.

   Now convert the timescale into a rate, using the definition
   $\Delta t_A=\Delta A/|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$:

   $$
   \frac{\Delta A}{\left|d\langle A\rangle/dt\right|} \ge 0.25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\frac{d\langle A\rangle}{dt}\right| \le \frac{\Delta A}{0.25} = 4\,\Delta A = 4(0.5) = 2
   $$

   So the ceiling on the raw rate is $2$, and it depends on $\Delta A$.
   That is the distinction the question is after. A state with $\Delta E=2$
   forbids any observable from moving by a full standard deviation in less
   than $0.25$ of time, whatever the observable is. But an observable with
   a large spread is permitted a correspondingly large raw rate, simply
   because it has further to travel before it has moved by one $\Delta A$.
   Doubling $\Delta A$ doubles the permitted $|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$ and
   leaves the timescale floor untouched.

   The wrong reading is to treat $\Delta t_A \ge 0.25$ as "nothing can
   change faster than four units per unit time." It says nothing of the
   kind: rates are not bounded, ratios are. This is why the lesson insists
   $\Delta t_A$ is a *timescale derived from* $A$'s own dynamics rather
   than an uncertainty in a measured time, and it is also why the relation
   survives a rescaling of $A$: replacing $A$ by $10A$ multiplies both
   $\Delta A$ and $|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$ by $10$, leaving $\Delta t_A$
   exactly where it was.

   </details>

3. Explain in one or two sentences why a *short-lived* excited state
   (one that decays quickly, small $\Delta t$ for some relevant
   observable) is expected to have a correspondingly large spread in
   energy, a real, measurable effect called "natural linewidth."

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the bound is a product: $\Delta E \ge \hbar/(2\Delta t_A)$, so
   a short characteristic timescale forces a large energy spread, and an
   energy level with a spread emits over a band of frequencies rather than
   at one sharp value.

   Running the lesson's own numbers backwards makes the size concrete.
   Sodium's excited $3p$ state lives about $\tau=16$ ns, giving
   $\Delta E\gtrsim\hbar/(2\tau)\approx3\times10^{-27}$ J, around
   $2\times10^{-8}$ eV, which read as a frequency spread is roughly
   $5$ MHz. The measured full width at half maximum,
   $1/(2\pi\tau)\approx10$ MHz, is twice that, the factor coming from the
   Lorentzian shape an exponentially decaying state emits while
   $\Delta E$ is the half width. Against an optical frequency near
   $5\times10^{14}$ Hz that is a line sharp to about two parts in $10^8$,
   and a state living a thousand times more briefly would be a thousand
   times blurrier.

   Two clarifications, because this is the relation most often misquoted.

   The lifetime is not literally the $\Delta t_A$ of the derivation. The
   derivation's timescale is defined for a specific observable as
   $\Delta A/|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$, whereas a lifetime is a decay
   constant for an unstable state, and connecting the two rigorously needs
   machinery this course has not built. What survives without that
   machinery is the scaling and the order of magnitude, which is what the
   sodium numbers above check, and it is why the lesson writes
   $\Delta E\,\Delta t\gtrsim\hbar/2$ with a wavy inequality when talking
   about lifetimes.

   And nothing here says energy is not conserved during the decay. The
   spread is in what a measurement of the excited level's energy would
   return, not a licence for the atom to borrow energy and repay it. The
   "borrowed energy" story is the standard misreading, and this lesson's
   derivation contains no such step: it came from an inequality between
   spreads plus Ehrenfest's theorem, and neither mentions a loan.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\Delta E\,\Delta t_A\ge\hbar/2$ is derived from the general operator
  uncertainty bound applied to $H$ and any observable $A$, combined with
  Ehrenfest's theorem, not from a commutator with a nonexistent time
  operator.
- $\Delta t_A\equiv\Delta A/|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$ is a timescale, not an
  uncertainty in a measured quantity.
- The relation's extreme limit ($\Delta E=0 \Rightarrow \Delta
  t_A=\infty$) reproduces the already-proven fact that stationary
  states never change, a consistency check across three
  separate derivations in three different courses.
