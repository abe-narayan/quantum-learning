<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
The earlier measurement postulate, P(aᵢ)=|cᵢ|², is exactly right when aᵢ is nondegenerate — but it doesn't even typecheck when several eigenvectors share the same eigenvalue.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

From Classical to Quantum's measurement postulate defined
$c_i=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle$ for a
*single* eigenvector $|a_i\rangle$ — there's no single $|a_i\rangle$ to take
a coefficient of once several eigenvectors
share an eigenvalue. This lesson fixes that using last lesson's projectors.

## Intuition

Instead of asking "how much of this *one* eigenvector is in my state," the
generalized rule asks "how much of my state lies in this *whole
eigenspace*," a question that makes sense whether the eigenspace is one
dimension or several, answered by the projector onto that eigenspace.

## Mathematical Development

### The generalized Born rule

For an observable $A=\sum_i a_iP_i$ (spectral decomposition, distinct
eigenvalues $a_i$, projectors $P_i$ possibly higher-rank) measured on a
normalized state $|\psi\rangle$:

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle
$$

and the state **collapses** to

$$
|\psi\rangle \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{P_i|\psi\rangle}{\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|}
$$

Literally: apply the projector (keeping only the component of
$|\psi\rangle$ lying in that eigenspace), then renormalize. This is
exactly `state.applyMatrix(P_i).normalize()` using operations this
platform's `StateVector` already had.

### Reducing to the familiar case

When $a_i$ is nondegenerate, $P_i=|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$, so

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = \langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = |c_i|^2
$$

using $\langle\psi|a_i\rangle=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*$ (conjugate
symmetry of the inner product), exactly recovering the earlier formula.
The generalized rule isn't a different postulate, it's the same one,
correctly accounting for degeneracy.

### Degenerate case: probabilities add

When $a_i$ is degenerate with orthonormal eigenvectors
$|e_{i,1}\rangle,\ldots,|e_{i,d}\rangle$, expanding
$P_i=\sum_k|e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|$ gives

$$
P(a_i) = \sum_{k=1}^d |\langle e_{i,k}|\psi\rangle|^2
$$

the sum of the individual squared-coefficient probabilities across
every eigenvector *sharing* that eigenvalue. This matches ordinary
probability theory exactly: "probability the outcome is $a_i$" is the sum
over every way the system could produce that outcome.

## Physical Interpretation

Collapse to $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ (rather than, say,
picking one specific eigenvector out of the degenerate subspace) is the
physically correct rule: a measurement that only resolves "the eigenvalue
is $a_i$" gives you no information about *where* in that eigenspace the
state actually was, and, critically, doesn't destroy whatever
information *was* there. Projecting keeps exactly the part of
$|\psi\rangle$ consistent with the observed outcome, nothing more,
nothing less.

## Worked Example

**For $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ and $|\psi\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}
(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$, find $P(N=1)$ and the post-measurement
state.**

$$
P(N{=}1) = \langle\psi|P_1|\psi\rangle = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 + \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 = \frac13+\frac13 = \frac23
$$

$$
P_1|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle, \qquad \|P_1|\psi\rangle\| = \sqrt{\frac23}
$$

$$
|\psi'\rangle = \frac{P_1|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{2/3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle
$$

(A quick check: $P(N{=}2)=|1/\sqrt3|^2=\tfrac13$, and $\tfrac23+\tfrac13=1$,
probabilities sum to 1, as they must.)

Laid out as a branching tree, the single measurement of $N$ has exactly
two possible outcomes, each carrying its own collapsed state:

Both branches' probabilities are computed the same way, $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|^2$,
whether the branch's own eigenspace is degenerate (N=1, two-dimensional)
or not (N=2, one-dimensional) — the generalized rule doesn't need a
special case for either.

The bar chart below shows the *probabilities* that collapse produces,
but not collapse itself: projecting and renormalizing are two distinct
geometric steps, visible if instead of tracking all three components of
$|\psi\rangle$ we track only how much of it lies in each eigenspace --
$\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$ against $\|P_2|\psi\rangle\|$, computed by this
platform's own `projectorOntoSubspace` and `Matrix.apply`, not derived
by hand. Since $N$'s two eigenspaces are orthogonal complements
spanning the whole space, that pair is always a point on the unit
circle.

Notice what each step actually did. Projecting left the horizontal
(eigenspace-1) coordinate exactly alone -- $P_1|\psi\rangle$ sits at
$x=\sqrt{2/3}$, precisely $|\psi\rangle$'s own eigenspace-1 amplitude
-- and only deleted the vertical (eigenspace-2) coordinate, which is
why $P_1|\psi\rangle$ is *shorter* than $|\psi\rangle$: some of the
vector's length belonged to an outcome that didn't happen. Only the
second step, dividing by $\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$, changes the horizontal
coordinate itself, stretching the vector back out to unit length. These
axes aren't the basis kets $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ -- each tracks the
*total* amplitude within one whole eigenspace, so this view deliberately
hides $|\psi'\rangle$'s remaining internal superposition across
$|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ (the very thing the next Common Mistake
callout warns against flattening away) while making the
project-then-renormalize mechanism itself unmistakable.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Collapsing to an arbitrarily chosen single eigenvector.** The correct
post-measurement state is the *normalized projection*, which in general
retains a superposition *within* the degenerate eigenspace. Only if the
pre-measurement state happened to already be a single eigenvector does
collapse leave it looking "simple."
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to renormalize after applying the projector.** $P_i|\psi
\rangle$ generally has norm less than 1 (some probability went to *other*
outcomes). Dividing by $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ is not optional.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $P(a_i)=\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$, with collapse to
  $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$: correct for any eigenvalue,
  degenerate or not.
- This reduces exactly to $|c_i|^2$ in the nondegenerate case, a
  generalization, not a different rule.
- In the degenerate case, the probability is the sum of $|c|^2$ over
  every eigenvector sharing that eigenvalue, and the post-measurement
  state can remain a genuine superposition within the degenerate
  subspace.
