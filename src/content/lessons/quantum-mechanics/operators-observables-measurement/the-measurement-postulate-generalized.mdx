<LessonHook eyebrow="The old formula does not typecheck">
The earlier measurement postulate, P(aᵢ)=|cᵢ|², is exactly right when aᵢ is nondegenerate. When several eigenvectors share an eigenvalue, it does not even typecheck.
</LessonHook>

## No single eigenvector to take a coefficient of

From Classical to Quantum's measurement postulate defined
$c_i=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle$ for a *single* eigenvector $|a_i\rangle$.
Once several eigenvectors share an eigenvalue there is no single
$|a_i\rangle$ to take a coefficient of, and the formula has nothing to
compute. This lesson repairs it using last lesson's projectors, arriving
at the form von Neumann gave in 1932: project onto the eigenspace, then
renormalize.

## Ask about the eigenspace, not the eigenvector

Instead of asking "how much of this *one* eigenvector is in my state," the
generalized rule asks "how much of my state lies in this *whole
eigenspace*," a question that makes sense whether the eigenspace is one
dimension or several, answered by the projector onto that eigenspace.

## The projector form, and what it reduces to

### The generalized Born rule

For an <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $A=\sum_i a_iP_i$ (spectral decomposition, distinct
eigenvalues $a_i$, projectors $P_i$ possibly higher-rank) measured on a
<Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> state $|\psi\rangle$:

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle
$$

and the state **<Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term>** to

$$
|\psi\rangle \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{P_i|\psi\rangle}{\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|}
$$

Two steps, in order: apply the projector (keeping only the component of
$|\psi\rangle$ lying in that eigenspace), then renormalize. In code that
is `state.applyMatrix(P_i).normalize()`, built from operations this
platform's `StateVector` already had.

### Reducing to the familiar case

When $a_i$ is nondegenerate, $P_i=|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$, so

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = \langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = |c_i|^2
$$

using $\langle\psi|a_i\rangle=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*$ (conjugate
symmetry of the inner product), which recovers the earlier formula
unchanged. The generalized rule is the same postulate as before,
correctly accounting for degeneracy, not a different one.

### Degenerate case: probabilities add

When $a_i$ is degenerate with orthonormal eigenvectors
$|e_{i,1}\rangle,\ldots,|e_{i,d}\rangle$, expanding
$P_i=\sum_k|e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|$ gives

$$
P(a_i) = \sum_{k=1}^d |\langle e_{i,k}|\psi\rangle|^2
$$

the sum of the individual squared-coefficient probabilities across
every eigenvector *sharing* that eigenvalue. This is the ordinary rule
from probability theory: "probability the outcome is $a_i$" is the sum
over every way the system could produce that outcome.

## Why collapse keeps the superposition inside the subspace

Collapse to $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ (rather than, say,
picking one specific eigenvector out of the degenerate subspace) is the
physically correct rule. A measurement that only resolves "the eigenvalue
is $a_i$" gives you no information about *where* in that eigenspace the
state was, and it must not destroy the information that was there.
Projecting keeps the part of $|\psi\rangle$ consistent with the observed
outcome, and discards only the part the outcome ruled out.

## A degenerate outcome and the state it leaves behind

**For $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ and $|\psi\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}
(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$, find $P(N=1)$ and the post-measurement
state.**

$$

P_1|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle, \qquad \|P_1|\psi\rangle\| = \sqrt{\frac23}

$$
|\psi'\rangle = \frac{P_1|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{2/3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle
$$

(A quick check: $P(N{=}2)=|1/\sqrt3|^2=\tfrac13$, and $\tfrac23+\tfrac13=1$,
probabilities sum to 1, as they must.)

Both branches' probabilities are computed the same way, $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|^2$,
whether the branch's own eigenspace is degenerate (N=1, two-dimensional)

for either.

not collapse itself. Projecting and renormalizing are two distinct
geometric steps, and they become visible if, instead of tracking all three
components of $|\psi\rangle$, we track only how much of it lies in each
eigenspace: $\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$ against $\|P_2|\psi\rangle\|$, computed
by this platform's own `projectorOntoSubspace` and `Matrix.apply` rather

complements spanning the whole space, that pair is always a point on the

Follow what each step did. Projecting left the horizontal (eigenspace-1)
coordinate untouched: $P_1|\psi\rangle$ sits at $x=\sqrt{2/3}$, which is
$|\psi\rangle$'s own eigenspace-1 amplitude. It deleted only the vertical
(eigenspace-2) coordinate, which is why $P_1|\psi\rangle$ is *shorter*
than $|\psi\rangle$. Some of the vector's length belonged to an outcome
that did not happen. Only the second step, dividing by
$\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$, changes the horizontal coordinate itself,
stretching the vector back out to unit length.

One caveat about reading this diagram: its axes are not the basis kets
$|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. Each axis tracks the *total* amplitude within one
whole eigenspace, so the picture deliberately hides $|\psi'\rangle$'s
remaining internal superposition across $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, which
is the thing the next Common Mistake warns against flattening away. What
it buys in exchange is that the project-then-renormalize mechanism becomes
two visible moves rather than one algebraic formula.

## Picking one eigenvector out is the wrong move

<Callout type="warning">
**Collapsing to an arbitrarily chosen single eigenvector.** The correct
post-measurement state is the *normalized projection*, which in general
retains a superposition *within* the degenerate eigenspace. Only if the
pre-measurement state happened to already be a single eigenvector does
collapse leave it looking "simple."
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to renormalize after applying the projector.** $P_i|\psi
\rangle$ generally has norm less than 1 (some probability went to *other*
outcomes). Dividing by $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ is not optional.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For the worked example's $|\psi\rangle$, verify $P(N{=}2)=\tfrac13$
   directly using the projector $P_2=|2\rangle\langle2|$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   P_2|\psi\rangle = |2\rangle\langle2|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|2\rangle, \qquad P(N{=}2) = \langle\psi|P_2|\psi\rangle = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 = \frac13
   $$

   Computing it the other way round confirms the same number:
   $\|P_2|\psi\rangle\|^2 = (1/\sqrt3)^2 = 1/3$. The two routes agree
   because $P_2$ is both idempotent and Hermitian, so
   $\langle\psi|P_2|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|P_2^\dagger P_2|\psi\rangle =
   \|P_2|\psi\rangle\|^2$. That identity is why the rule can be stated
   either as a sandwich or as a squared length, and it is worth knowing
   both forms: the sandwich generalizes to density matrices, and the
   squared length is what a numerical implementation actually computes.

   Since $a=2$ is nondegenerate, the general rule collapses back to the
   familiar $|c_i|^2$ here, with $c_2=\langle2|\psi\rangle=1/\sqrt3$. That
   is the reduction the lesson proves in general, seen in a single case.

   Together with $P(N{=}1)=\tfrac23$, the probabilities sum to $1$, which
   question 3 shows was guaranteed in advance by completeness rather than
   confirmed by luck.

   The instructive contrast is what the two outcomes do to the state.
   Outcome $2$ collapses $|\psi\rangle$ to $|2\rangle$, a single basis
   vector with nothing left inside it, because its eigenspace is
   one-dimensional. Outcome $1$ collapses it to
   $(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$, still a superposition, because its
   eigenspace has room for one. Same observable, same measurement, and
   whether the result is a definite basis state or a surviving
   superposition depends entirely on the rank of the projector that
   fired.

   </details>

2. If $|\psi\rangle$ had been exactly $|0\rangle$ to begin with, what
   would $P(N{=}1)$ and the post-measurement state be? (No calculation
   needed if you reason about it correctly.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $P(N{=}1)=1$, and the state is left as $|0\rangle$, unchanged.

   The reasoning that avoids the arithmetic: $|0\rangle$ already lies
   entirely inside the eigenvalue-$1$ eigenspace
   $\mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$. A projector acts as the identity
   on vectors already in its subspace, so $P_1|0\rangle=|0\rangle$, giving
   probability $\||0\rangle\|^2=1$ and, after a renormalization that
   divides by $1$, the same state back. Nothing was discarded because
   nothing lay outside.

   That is idempotence read as physics: projecting a state that is already
   projected changes nothing, which is the same fact that makes an
   immediately repeated measurement certain to reproduce its own outcome.

   The trap this question is built around is the assumption that a
   degenerate outcome must leave the state "spread out" across its
   eigenspace. It does not. The post-measurement state is whatever the
   pre-measurement state's component in that eigenspace was, renormalized,
   and if that component happened to be a single basis vector, a single
   basis vector is what you get. The Common Mistakes note above puts the
   same point the other way round: only if the state was already an
   eigenvector does collapse leave it looking simple.

   Two contrasts sharpen it. Had the state been $|2\rangle$ instead, the
   answer would be $P(N{=}1)=0$: $P_1|2\rangle$ is the zero vector, and the
   renormalization step is undefined, correctly, since an outcome of
   probability zero never occurs and there is no post-measurement state to
   compute. And had the state been the worked example's equal
   superposition, $P(N{=}1)=\tfrac23$ with a genuine superposition
   surviving. The rule handles all three without a special case, which is
   the whole point of writing it with projectors.

   </details>

3. Explain briefly why $\sum_iP(a_i)=1$ is guaranteed for *any* normalized
   $|\psi\rangle$, using completeness ($\sum_iP_i=I$) from the first
   lesson of this course.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the sum of the probabilities is the sum of the projectors,
   sandwiched, and completeness turns that sum into the identity:

   $$
   \sum_i P(a_i) = \sum_i \langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle = \left\langle\psi\left|\left(\sum_iP_i\right)\right|\psi\right\rangle = \langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1
   $$

   Only two facts were used: linearity, which lets the sum move inside the
   bracket, and $\sum_iP_i=I$. Normalization supplies the final step.

   What makes this a real guarantee rather than a restatement is that
   completeness is itself a theorem, not an assumption. The spectral
   theorem says a Hermitian operator's eigenvectors form a full orthonormal
   basis of the space, and $\sum_iP_i$ is exactly the projector onto the
   span of all of them, which is everything. So the probabilities sum to
   one because the observable's outcomes exhaust the possibilities, which
   is what "the eigenvectors span the space" means physically.

   Note how little the argument needed. It never mentioned which
   observable, whether any eigenvalue is degenerate, what the ranks of the
   projectors are, or which state was measured. That universality is the
   payoff of writing the Born rule with projectors: the nondegenerate proof
   in the earlier course had to count coefficients one eigenvector at a
   time, and this one does not count anything.

   Two places the argument would break, both worth recognizing.

   If the $P_i$ were not mutually orthogonal, the sum could overcount and
   exceed $1$. Orthogonality comes free for a Hermitian operator's distinct
   eigenvalues, proven in Mathematical Foundations, which is why the
   generalized rule requires a Hermitian observable and not merely any
   operator with real eigenvalues.

   And if some eigenvalues were left out of the sum, completeness fails and
   the total falls below $1$. That is not a hypothetical: it is exactly
   what a *selective* measurement looks like, where an apparatus registers
   only some outcomes, and the missing probability is the chance the
   apparatus stayed silent.

   </details>

## Summary

- $P(a_i)=\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$, with collapse to
  $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$: correct for any eigenvalue,
  degenerate or not.
- This reduces exactly to $|c_i|^2$ in the nondegenerate case, a
  generalization, not a different rule.
- In the degenerate case, the probability is the sum of $|c|^2$ over
  every eigenvector sharing that eigenvalue, and the post-measurement
  state can remain a superposition within the degenerate subspace.
