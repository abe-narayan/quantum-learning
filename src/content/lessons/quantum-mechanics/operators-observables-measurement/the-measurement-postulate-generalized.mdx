<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
The earlier measurement postulate, P(aᵢ)=|cᵢ|², is exactly right when aᵢ is nondegenerate. When several eigenvectors share an eigenvalue, it does not even typecheck.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

From Classical to Quantum's measurement postulate defined
$c_i=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle$ for a *single* eigenvector $|a_i\rangle$.
Once several eigenvectors share an eigenvalue there is no single
$|a_i\rangle$ to take a coefficient of, and the formula has nothing to
compute. This lesson repairs it using last lesson's projectors, arriving
at the form von Neumann gave in 1932: project onto the eigenspace, then
renormalize.

## Intuition

Instead of asking "how much of this *one* eigenvector is in my state," the
generalized rule asks "how much of my state lies in this *whole
eigenspace*," a question that makes sense whether the eigenspace is one
dimension or several, answered by the projector onto that eigenspace.

## Mathematical Development

### The generalized Born rule

For an <Term id="observable">observable</Term> $A=\sum_i a_iP_i$ (spectral decomposition, distinct
eigenvalues $a_i$, projectors $P_i$ possibly higher-rank) measured on a
<Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> state $|\psi\rangle$:

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle
$$

and the state **<Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term>** to

$$
|\psi\rangle \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{P_i|\psi\rangle}{\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|}
$$

Two steps, in order: apply the projector (keeping only the component of
$|\psi\rangle$ lying in that eigenspace), then renormalize. In code that
is `state.applyMatrix(P_i).normalize()`, built from operations this
platform's `StateVector` already had.

### Reducing to the familiar case

When $a_i$ is nondegenerate, $P_i=|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|$, so

$$
P(a_i) = \langle\psi|a_i\rangle\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = \langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*\langle a_i|\psi\rangle = |c_i|^2
$$

using $\langle\psi|a_i\rangle=\langle a_i|\psi\rangle^*$ (conjugate
symmetry of the inner product), which recovers the earlier formula
unchanged. The generalized rule is the same postulate as before,
correctly accounting for degeneracy, not a different one.

### Degenerate case: probabilities add

When $a_i$ is degenerate with orthonormal eigenvectors
$|e_{i,1}\rangle,\ldots,|e_{i,d}\rangle$, expanding
$P_i=\sum_k|e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|$ gives

$$
P(a_i) = \sum_{k=1}^d |\langle e_{i,k}|\psi\rangle|^2
$$

the sum of the individual squared-coefficient probabilities across
every eigenvector *sharing* that eigenvalue. This is the ordinary rule
from probability theory: "probability the outcome is $a_i$" is the sum
over every way the system could produce that outcome.

## Physical Interpretation

Collapse to $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ (rather than, say,
picking one specific eigenvector out of the degenerate subspace) is the
physically correct rule. A measurement that only resolves "the eigenvalue
is $a_i$" gives you no information about *where* in that eigenspace the
state was, and it must not destroy the information that was there.
Projecting keeps the part of $|\psi\rangle$ consistent with the observed
outcome, and discards only the part the outcome ruled out.

## Worked Example

**For $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ and $|\psi\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}
(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$, find $P(N=1)$ and the post-measurement
state.**

$$
P(N{=}1) = \langle\psi|P_1|\psi\rangle = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 + \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 = \frac13+\frac13 = \frac23
$$

$$
P_1|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle, \qquad \|P_1|\psi\rangle\| = \sqrt{\frac23}
$$

$$
|\psi'\rangle = \frac{P_1|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt{2/3}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle
$$

(A quick check: $P(N{=}2)=|1/\sqrt3|^2=\tfrac13$, and $\tfrac23+\tfrac13=1$,
probabilities sum to 1, as they must.)

possible outcomes, each carrying its own collapsed state:

Both branches' probabilities are computed the same way, $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|^2$,
whether the branch's own eigenspace is degenerate (N=1, two-dimensional)
or not (N=2, one-dimensional). The generalized rule needs no special case
for either.

The bar chart below shows the *probabilities* that collapse produces, but
not collapse itself. Projecting and renormalizing are two distinct
geometric steps, and they become visible if, instead of tracking all three
components of $|\psi\rangle$, we track only how much of it lies in each
eigenspace: $\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$ against $\|P_2|\psi\rangle\|$, computed
by this platform's own `projectorOntoSubspace` and `Matrix.apply` rather
than written in by hand. Since $N$'s two eigenspaces are orthogonal
complements spanning the whole space, that pair is always a point on the
unit circle.

<VectorDiagram
  vectors={collapseVectors}
  ariaLabel="Vector diagram whose horizontal axis is the amplitude in the N equals 1 eigenspace and whose vertical axis is the amplitude in the N equals 2 eigenspace. Psi starts as a unit vector at coordinates root two thirds, root one third, about 0.816, 0.577. Projecting onto the N equals 1 eigenspace, labeled P sub 1 ket psi and drawn dashed, drops straight down onto the horizontal axis at 0.816, 0, leaving the horizontal coordinate unchanged and deleting only the vertical one. Renormalizing then stretches that shortened vector back out to the unit circle, landing exactly on the horizontal axis at 1, 0, the post-measurement state psi prime."
/>

Follow what each step did. Projecting left the horizontal (eigenspace-1)
coordinate untouched: $P_1|\psi\rangle$ sits at $x=\sqrt{2/3}$, which is
$|\psi\rangle$'s own eigenspace-1 amplitude. It deleted only the vertical
(eigenspace-2) coordinate, which is why $P_1|\psi\rangle$ is *shorter*
than $|\psi\rangle$. Some of the vector's length belonged to an outcome
that did not happen. Only the second step, dividing by
$\|P_1|\psi\rangle\|$, changes the horizontal coordinate itself,
stretching the vector back out to unit length.

One caveat about reading this diagram: its axes are not the basis kets
$|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. Each axis tracks the *total* amplitude within one
whole eigenspace, so the picture deliberately hides $|\psi'\rangle$'s
remaining internal superposition across $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, which
is the thing the next Common Mistake warns against flattening away. What
it buys in exchange is that the project-then-renormalize mechanism becomes
two visible moves rather than one algebraic formula.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Collapsing to an arbitrarily chosen single eigenvector.** The correct
post-measurement state is the *normalized projection*, which in general
retains a superposition *within* the degenerate eigenspace. Only if the
pre-measurement state happened to already be a single eigenvector does
collapse leave it looking "simple."
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to renormalize after applying the projector.** $P_i|\psi
\rangle$ generally has norm less than 1 (some probability went to *other*
outcomes). Dividing by $\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$ is not optional.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $P(a_i)=\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$, with collapse to
  $P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$: correct for any eigenvalue,
  degenerate or not.
- This reduces exactly to $|c_i|^2$ in the nondegenerate case, a
  generalization, not a different rule.
- In the degenerate case, the probability is the sum of $|c|^2$ over
  every eigenvector sharing that eigenvalue, and the post-measurement
  state can remain a superposition within the degenerate subspace.
