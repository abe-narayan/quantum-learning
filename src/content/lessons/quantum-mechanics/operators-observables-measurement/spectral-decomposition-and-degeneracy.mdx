## The easy case, and what it assumed away

Mathematical Foundations stated the spectral decomposition
$A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ and verified it for Pauli-$Z$,
where every eigenvalue happens to be nondegenerate: each eigenvalue has
exactly one eigenvector. That's the easy case. This lesson builds the
machinery, projection operators, that makes the decomposition precise
and correct even when an eigenvalue has an entire *subspace* of
eigenvectors, which is the rule rather than the exception once systems get
more interesting (multiple energy levels sharing one energy, as you'll
see for real in the future Hydrogen Atom course).

## A projector is a shadow on a wall

A **projection operator** does what the name suggests: given any vector,
it keeps the part lying in some subspace and discards the rest, the
linear-algebra analogue of a shadow cast onto a wall. Every term
$|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|$ in the spectral decomposition is a projector
onto the one-dimensional subspace spanned by $|e_i\rangle$; this lesson's
only real extension is allowing that subspace to be bigger than one
dimension.

Drag $v$ around the plane below and watch its shadow $Pv$ fall onto a
one-dimensional eigenspace (a line) versus a two-dimensional, degenerate
eigenspace (the whole plane, where the shadow of $v$ is $v$ itself, since
nothing is left to discard). Either way, the dashed line dropping straight
down from $v$ to $Pv$ is the "cast a shadow" step, and the small loop at
$Pv$ is $P$ applied a second time, landing on the same point it started
from. That loop is idempotence, drawn rather than proven.

## Two properties, then eigenspaces of any dimension

### Two defining properties

An operator $P$ is a projector if it satisfies:

$$
P^2 = P \qquad \text{(idempotence)}
$$

$$
P^\dagger = P \qquad \text{(Hermitian)}
$$

**Idempotence proven directly** for the rank-1 case $P=|e\rangle\langle
e|$ with $|e\rangle$ normalized:

$$
P^2 = |e\rangle\langle e|e\rangle\langle e| = |e\rangle\cdot1\cdot\langle e| = |e\rangle\langle e| = P
$$

using $\langle e|e\rangle=1$. Physically: projecting twice does nothing
more than projecting once. Once you've kept only the component along
$|e\rangle$, projecting *that* again changes nothing.

Hermiticity is the shorter of the two. Daggering an outer product
reverses it and conjugates each factor, so
$(|e\rangle\langle e|)^\dagger=(\langle e|)^\dagger(|e\rangle)^\dagger=|e\rangle\langle e|$,
and a rank-1 projector is its own adjoint with nothing to check.

Idempotence alone, before any of the physics arrives, already fixes
something about what a projector can measure.

### Projectors onto degenerate eigenspaces

If eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ has $d$ <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal eigenvectors</Term>
$|e_{i,1}\rangle,\ldots,|e_{i,d}\rangle$ (a $d$-dimensional eigenspace,
Mathematical Foundations already noted these can always be chosen
orthonormal via Gram-Schmidt), the projector onto the *whole eigenspace*
is the sum of the individual rank-1 projectors:

$$
P_i = \sum_{k=1}^{d} |e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|
$$

This is still idempotent (each term is individually idempotent, and
cross terms vanish by orthogonality: $\langle e_{i,j}|e_{i,k}\rangle=0$
for $j\ne k$), proven the same way as before with one extra sum. This is
what this platform's `projectorOntoSubspace(vectors)` function builds,
with `outerProduct(ket, bra)` computing each
$|e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|$ term.

### The full spectral decomposition, correctly stated

$$
A = \sum_i \lambda_i P_i, \qquad P_i = \sum_{k} |e_{i,k}\rangle\langle e_{i,k}|
$$

summing over *distinct eigenvalues* $\lambda_i$, with $P_i$ now
possibly higher-rank. Two consistency facts worth naming: the projectors
onto different eigenspaces are mutually orthogonal, $P_iP_j=0$ for
$i\ne j$ (a direct consequence of the eigenvector-orthogonality theorem
from Mathematical Foundations), and they sum to the identity,
$\sum_iP_i=I$ (**completeness**: projecting onto every eigenspace and
adding the results has to reconstruct the original vector, since the
eigenvectors span the whole space).

## Knowing the energy without knowing the state

Degeneracy means an eigenvalue alone doesn't fully identify a unique
basis state. Knowing "the energy is $E_i$" only tells you the state
lives *somewhere* in a $d$-dimensional eigenspace, not exactly where. The
next lesson builds the tool for resolving this: a second, compatible
<Term id="observable">observable</Term> that distinguishes states within the degenerate subspace.

## Decomposing diag(1,1,2)

**Let $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ on $\mathbb{C}^3$. Find its spectral
decomposition.**

Eigenvalue $1$ has eigenspace $\mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ (a
degenerate, 2-dimensional eigenspace, the standard basis vectors $e_0,
e_1$ of the 3-dimensional space); eigenvalue $2$ has eigenspace
$\mathrm{span}\{|2\rangle\}$ (nondegenerate). So:

$$
P_1 = |0\rangle\langle0| + |1\rangle\langle1| = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad P_2 = |2\rangle\langle2| = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
N = 1\cdot P_1 + 2\cdot P_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix} \checkmark
$$

which is $N$ back again, confirming both projectors and the decomposition
in one step.

## One term per eigenvalue, not per eigenvector

<Callout type="warning">
**Writing a separate term for every eigen*vector* instead of every
eigen*value*.** With a degenerate eigenvalue, the correct decomposition
groups all its eigenvectors into one projector $P_i$. Writing
$\lambda_i|e_{i,1}\rangle\langle e_{i,1}| + \lambda_i|e_{i,2}\rangle
\langle e_{i,2}|$ separately gives the same operator, but treating them
as two *different* terms with the same eigenvalue obscures that they're
one eigenspace, which matters for the measurement postulate three lessons
ahead.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming $P^2=P$ for any Hermitian operator.** It's specific to
projectors. Pauli-$Z$ is Hermitian but $Z^2=I\ne Z$. Idempotence is an
*extra* condition beyond Hermiticity.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify $P_1^2=P_1$ directly for the $N$ example above, by matrix
   multiplication.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   P_1^2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} = P_1 \checkmark
   $$

   For a diagonal matrix, squaring squares each diagonal entry
   independently, so the check reduces to $1^2=1$ and $0^2=0$. Those are
   the only two numbers that survive being squared, which is exactly the
   eigenvalue condition $\lambda^2=\lambda$ the prediction above derived
   from idempotence alone. Applying the platform's
   `projectorOntoSubspace` to $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ and squaring the
   result reproduces the same matrix.

   The point worth extracting is that the rank made no difference.
   $P_1$ projects onto a two-dimensional subspace and $P_2$ onto a
   one-dimensional one, and both are idempotent for the same reason: the
   general proof only ever used $\langle e_{i,j}|e_{i,k}\rangle
   =\delta_{jk}$, orthonormality of the vectors spanning the subspace, and
   never how many of them there were. That is why the degenerate case needs
   no separate theorem.

   The seductive wrong instinct here is to expect the degenerate projector
   to behave differently, perhaps by "double counting" the two directions
   it keeps. It cannot, because the cross terms
   $|0\rangle\langle0|1\rangle\langle1|$ vanish: $\langle0|1\rangle=0$.
   Orthogonality is what stops the sum of projectors from interfering with
   itself, and it is why the eigenvectors have to be chosen orthonormal
   (via Gram-Schmidt if they do not start that way) before this
   construction is legitimate at all.

   </details>

2. For a Hermitian operator with three distinct eigenvalues on a
   4-dimensional space, what are the possible dimensions of its
   eigenspaces (listing all ways they could add up to 4)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Only one pattern: dimensions $2,1,1$, in some order. One eigenvalue is
   doubly degenerate and the other two are nondegenerate.

   Two facts pin this down. Every eigenspace has dimension at least $1$,
   since each distinct eigenvalue has at least one eigenvector by
   definition. And completeness, $\sum_iP_i=I$, forces the dimensions to
   sum to the dimension of the whole space, $4$, because the spectral
   theorem for a Hermitian operator supplies a full orthonormal eigenbasis
   with nothing left over. So the question is: in how many ways can three
   positive integers sum to $4$? Only $2+1+1$. There is no $3+1+0$, because
   a dimension of zero would mean the third eigenvalue had no eigenvector
   and was therefore not an eigenvalue.

   Which of the three eigenvalues carries the degeneracy is not determined
   by the counting; that depends on the operator. So there are three
   *assignments* ($\lambda_1$ degenerate, or $\lambda_2$, or $\lambda_3$)
   of the one dimension pattern.

   The general statement worth carrying forward is that the eigenspace
   dimensions form a partition of $\dim\mathcal{H}$ into as many positive
   parts as there are distinct eigenvalues. That immediately gives two
   useful bounds: an operator on a $d$-dimensional space has at most $d$
   distinct eigenvalues, and having exactly $d$ of them means every
   eigenspace is one-dimensional, so the spectrum is completely
   nondegenerate. Every departure from $d$ distinct eigenvalues is
   degeneracy, measured exactly by the shortfall.

   The mistake to name is treating "three distinct eigenvalues on a
   4-dimensional space" as leaving room for choice. It does not. The
   moment you know the count of distinct eigenvalues and the dimension,
   the degeneracy structure is forced, which is precisely why the next
   lesson can promise that one extra observable will be enough to resolve
   this case.

   </details>

3. Confirm $P_1P_2=0$ for the $N$ example's two projectors, by direct
   matrix multiplication.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   P_1P_2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix} \checkmark
   $$

   and $P_2P_1$ gives the zero matrix too, which matters: unlike most
   operator products, this one is symmetric in the order. Both are $0$
   because the two projectors keep *disjoint* sets of diagonal slots, so
   every product term multiplies a $1$ from one matrix against a $0$ from
   the other.

   The structural reason is orthogonality of the eigenspaces. $P_2$ sends
   everything into $\mathrm{span}\{|2\rangle\}$, and $P_1$ then discards
   anything orthogonal to $\mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$, which is
   all of it. This is not a coincidence of $N$'s diagonality: the
   Mathematical Foundations theorem that eigenvectors of a Hermitian
   operator with *distinct* eigenvalues are orthogonal guarantees
   $P_iP_j=0$ for every $i\ne j$, for every Hermitian operator.

   Two consequences that get used later, so worth naming now.

   Together with completeness, $P_1+P_2=I$, this is what makes the
   probabilities in the generalized Born rule sum to one:
   $\sum_iP(a_i)=\langle\psi|\sum_iP_i|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\psi
   \rangle=1$, with the orthogonality guaranteeing there is no
   double counting between outcomes.

   And it is why measuring an observable twice in a row returns the same
   answer. After the first measurement the state lies entirely inside one
   eigenspace, so every *other* projector annihilates it and every other
   outcome has probability exactly zero.

   The check that catches an error fastest is the trace. $\mathrm{Tr}(P_1)
   = 2$ and $\mathrm{Tr}(P_2) = 1$, each equal to the rank, that is, to the
   dimension of the eigenspace projected onto, and the two sum to $3$, the
   dimension of the whole space. A projector whose trace is not a
   non-negative integer, or a set whose traces do not sum to the dimension,
   has gone wrong somewhere upstream.

   </details>

## Summary

- A projector satisfies $P^2=P$ and $P^\dagger=P$; $|e\rangle\langle e|$
  for a normalized $|e\rangle$ is the simplest (rank-1) example.
- A degenerate eigenspace's projector sums the rank-1 projectors of an
  orthonormal basis for that eigenspace: $P_i=\sum_k|e_{i,k}\rangle
  \langle e_{i,k}|$.
- The full spectral decomposition $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$, summed over
  *distinct* eigenvalues, holds correctly whether or not any eigenvalue
  is degenerate.
- `outerProduct` and `projectorOntoSubspace` (this platform's engine
  functions) build these objects, tested against idempotence,
  completeness, and the reconstruction of Pauli-$Z$ from its own
  projectors.
