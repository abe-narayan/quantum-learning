## What each upgrade actually replaced

The upgrades were concrete: projectors instead of bare eigenvectors,
degenerate eigenspaces instead of assuming they don't exist, a proven
(not asserted) criterion for compatibility, and an
energy-time bound derived without pretending time is an operator.

None of that is bookkeeping. Hydrogen, the system the next courses build
toward, has degeneracy everywhere above its ground state:

<EnergyLevelDiagram
  levels={hydrogenDegeneracyLevels}
  unit="eV"
  ariaLabel="Hydrogen's first four energy levels drawn on an energy axis, at about minus 13.6, minus 3.4, minus 1.51 and minus 0.85 electronvolts. Each level is labelled with its degeneracy: n equals 1 is 1-fold, n equals 2 is 4-fold, n equals 3 is 9-fold, n equals 4 is 16-fold. The levels crowd together as n increases."
/>

Each rung is a single energy eigenvalue, so $H$ on its own cannot tell
its $n^2$ member states apart. The $n=3$ level alone hides nine distinct
orbital states behind one number. Writing the Born rule for a measurement
of $H$ on hydrogen, or saying what state the atom is left in afterwards,
is impossible with the tools the earlier courses supplied. That is what
this course fixed.

## Recap: Specific Cases to General Theory

| Earlier, specific version | This course's general version |
|---|---|
| $A=\sum_i\lambda_i\lvert e_i\rangle\langle e_i\rvert$ (Math. Foundations, nondegenerate) | $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$, $P_i$ possibly higher-rank (Spectral Decomposition) |
| "$[A,B]=0$ removes the uncertainty tradeoff" (From Classical to Quantum) | $[A,B]=0\iff$ shared eigenbasis, proven both directions (Simultaneous Eigenstates) |
| A single observable's eigenvalue as a state label | A complete *set* of commuting observables, needed when any one is degenerate (Complete Sets) |
| $P(a_i)=\lvert c_i\rvert^2$ (From Classical to Quantum, implicitly nondegenerate) | $P(a_i)=\langle\psi\rvert P_i\lvert\psi\rangle$, reducing exactly to $\lvert c_i\rvert^2$ (Measurement Postulate, Generalized) |
| "Incompatible observables disturb each other" (stated) | A fully numeric $Z\to X\to Z$ sequence, tracking the disturbance step by step (Sequential Measurements) |
| $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ specifically (Wave Mechanics) | $\Delta E\,\Delta t_A\ge\hbar/2$ for *any* observable $A$, derived via Ehrenfest's theorem (Energy-Time Uncertainty) |

Nothing in the right column contradicts the left column. Wherever a row's
general version was reached by dropping a hidden nondegeneracy assumption,
putting that assumption back recovers the specific version on the left
exactly. Exactly one row does not work that way, and practice question 1
asks you to find it.

## Problems that need degeneracy handled properly

**1. A <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian operator</Term> has a triply-degenerate eigenvalue. Using this
course's tools, how many *additional*, mutually-compatible observables
would generically be needed, at minimum, to fully resolve it into three
individually-labeled states?**

At minimum one, a second <Term id="observable">observable</Term> whose eigenvalues happen to take
three *different* values across the 3-dimensional degenerate eigenspace
resolves it completely (Complete Sets of Commuting Observables). Whether
one suffices in a *specific* physical case depends on whether such an
observable exists and is physically available to measure. The
Worked System lesson's $(Z_0,Z_1)$ pair needed exactly two observables
total (one degenerate on its own, resolved by the second) because that
was the natural symmetry available; a different system's degeneracy
might need more.

**2. A state is measured with observable $A$, giving outcome $a_i$
(degenerate). Immediately after, the *same* observable $A$ is measured
again. What's the probability of getting $a_i$ again, and why, using the
Measurement Postulate, Generalized?**

Probability $1$. After the first measurement, the state is
$P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$, already entirely within the
$a_i$-eigenspace, so $P_i$ applied to it returns it unchanged (projectors
are idempotent, Spectral Decomposition), giving
$P(a_i\text{ again})=\langle\psi'|P_i|\psi'\rangle=1$. Repeated
immediate measurement of the same observable never disturbs its own
outcome. Only an *incompatible* observable measured in between can, as
Sequential Measurements demonstrated.

**3. Two observables $A$ and $B$ satisfy $[A,B]=0$. A student claims this
means measuring $A$ then $B$ always gives the same result as measuring
$B$ then $A$. Is the claim right as stated?**

<PredictBeforeReveal
  question="[A,B] = 0, and the state is not an eigenstate of either. Does measuring A then B give the same individual outcomes as measuring B then A?"
  options={[
    { label: "Yes: commuting observables make order irrelevant in every sense", value: "same" },
    { label: "No, and compatibility says nothing about repeated measurements either", value: "nothing" },
    { label: "The joint probabilities match; the outcomes are still random", value: "probabilities" },
  ]}
  correctValue="probabilities"
  explanation="Compatibility fixes the statistics, not the individual draws. The joint probability of any outcome pair is the same in either order, which is the real content of [A,B] = 0, but each measurement still collapses a superposition and each outcome is still random. What compatibility does buy is repeatability: measure A, then B, then A again, and the third measurement reproduces the first outcome with certainty, because collapsing onto a shared eigenbasis for B leaves the state an eigenstate of A."
/>

Not quite as stated. Compatibility guarantees the two measurements do not
*disturb* each other's probability distribution or destroy each other's
definiteness, but if the *pre-measurement* state is not already an
eigenstate of both, the two individual outcomes remain random, with the
same joint probabilities regardless of order. What is guaranteed is that
a *second* measurement of $A$ after measuring the compatible $B$
reproduces $A$'s *first* outcome with certainty, because
<Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapse</Term> onto a shared eigenbasis for one operator leaves the state an
eigenstate of the other too.

## Generalizations, not replacements

<Callout type="note">
**Treating this course's generalizations as replacing the earlier
postulates.** They're the same postulates, made precise for a case
(degeneracy) the earlier statements quietly assumed away. It is the same
relationship "From Classical to Quantum" had to "Mathematical
Foundations," and "Wave Mechanics" had to "From Classical to Quantum."
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every quantum system has trivial (nondegenerate) spectra.**
Real, physically important systems, including ones a few courses away
on this platform, like the hydrogen atom's energy levels, are highly
degenerate. This course's machinery is required infrastructure for that
content, not a mathematical nicety.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the recap table, identify the one lesson in this course whose
   result was *not* a strict generalization of something already proven,
   and say what the word "generalization" fails to capture about it. Then
   name the two earlier results its derivation actually leans on, and say
   what would break if either were unavailable.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The Energy-Time Uncertainty Relation, the table's last row.

   **Why it is the odd one out.** Every other row takes an earlier
   statement and removes a hidden assumption. $A=\sum_i\lambda_i|e_i\rangle
   \langle e_i|$ becomes $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ by dropping
   nondegeneracy; $P(a_i)=|c_i|^2$ becomes
   $\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$ the same way; a single eigenvalue as a
   label becomes a complete set; a stated compatibility criterion becomes
   a proven equivalence. In each case the general version reduces exactly
   to the earlier one when the assumption is put back, which the recap's
   closing line calls out as a requirement each derivation had to meet.

   $\Delta E\,\Delta t_A\ge\hbar/2$ does not do that. Setting anything to
   a special value does not turn it back into $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge
   \hbar/2$, and the left column pairs the two only because the table needs
   an entry there. The two are different kinds of statement:
   $\Delta x\,\Delta p$ is a product of two *operator* uncertainties,
   while $\Delta t_A$ is not an operator uncertainty at all. There is no
   time operator, so there is no earlier special case for this result to
   be the general version of.

   **What it leans on instead.** Two results, both imported from *other*
   courses rather than generalized from this one.

   The general operator uncertainty bound $\Delta A\,\Delta B\ge
   \tfrac12|\langle[A,B]\rangle|$, proved from Cauchy-Schwarz in From
   Classical to Quantum, applied here to the pair $H$ and $A$. Without it
   there is no inequality to start from, and the derivation has nothing to
   convert.

   Ehrenfest's theorem $d\langle A\rangle/dt=(i/\hbar)\langle[H,A]\rangle$,
   proved in Wave Mechanics, which is what turns $\langle[H,A]\rangle$
   into a rate of change. Without it the bound stays a statement relating
   two spreads through a commutator, with no time in it anywhere, and the
   quantity $\Delta t_A$ never appears.

   So the honest description is that this lesson *derives a new result*
   from two old ones, where the rest of the course *restates old results*
   without the fine print. Both are worth doing, and only one of them is
   generalization.

   </details>

2. A CSCO has three observables. Explain, in your own words, what
   "complete" is doing in that name: complete with respect to what,
   exactly? Then describe two genuinely different ways a set of three
   mutually commuting observables could fail to be complete.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Complete with respect to *labelling the basis*: the joint eigenvalue
   tuple $(a,b,c)$ has to pick out one basis state and no other. Nothing
   else is being claimed.

   Say what "complete" is not, since the word invites three wrong readings.
   It does not mean the set contains every observable of the system, or
   every observable that commutes with the others, and adding more members
   to a complete set leaves it complete. It does not mean each member is
   individually informative; a member can be badly degenerate on its own,
   as $Z_0$ is on two qubits, and still be indispensable to the set. And
   it does not mean the set is minimal, since a complete set can carry
   redundant members. The whole condition is: run down the list of basis
   states, write each one's tuple, and check that no tuple appears twice.

   That leaves exactly one way to fail, two orthogonal states sharing a
   tuple, but it arrives by two genuinely different routes.

   **A member that is trivial where it is needed.** Take
   $A=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$ on $\mathbb{C}^3$ with $B=C=I$. All three
   commute, and $B$ and $C$ take the value $1$ on everything, so
   $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ both carry $(1,1,1)$. This is the Complete
   Sets lesson's warning in its purest form: commuting is easy, and an
   observable that is constant across a degenerate eigenspace resolves
   nothing inside it. The identity is the extreme case, but any $B$ that
   happens to be a multiple of the identity *on that eigenspace* fails the
   same way while looking perfectly nontrivial elsewhere.

   **Members that are non-trivial but redundant.** Take
   $A=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$, $B=A^2=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,4)$ and
   $C=A^3=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,8)$. Now every member has two distinct
   eigenvalues, none is a multiple of the identity, and all three commute.
   They still fail, because each is a function of $A$: wherever $A$ repeats
   a value, so does any function of it, and $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$
   share the tuple $(1,1,1)$ again. Three observables carrying one
   observable's worth of information.

   The second case is the more instructive of the two, because it shows
   that counting members proves nothing. What matters is whether the
   degeneracies *overlap*, and the test is the table, not the tally. The
   two-qubit example makes the same point positively: $Z_0$ and $Z_1$ are
   each degenerate, and they are complete together because their
   repeated values fall in different patterns, $(+,+,-,-)$ against
   $(+,-,+,-)$.

   </details>

3. A state has $\Delta E=0$. You measure an observable $A$, wait a time
   $\Delta t$, then measure $A$ again. Combining this course's
   repeated-measurement result with the energy-time relation, work out
   when the second reading is guaranteed to match the first. Show that the
   guarantee needs a hypothesis about $A$, and give an explicit two-level
   counterexample for an $A$ that fails it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The guarantee holds for every $\Delta t$ exactly when $[A,H]=0$. For an
   $A$ incompatible with $H$ it fails, and the second reading can even be
   *certain to disagree*.

   **The two ingredients, stated carefully.** The repeated-measurement
   result says an *immediate* second measurement of $A$ reproduces the
   first outcome with certainty, because collapse leaves the state inside
   the $a_i$-eigenspace and $P_i$ is idempotent. The energy-time relation
   says $\Delta E=0$ forces $\Delta t_A=\infty$ for every observable, so
   nothing evolves and the waiting costs nothing.

   **Where the naive combination goes wrong.** The second ingredient is a
   statement about $|\psi\rangle$, and the waiting happens *after* the
   first measurement, to a different state: $|\psi'\rangle =
   P_i|\psi\rangle/\|P_i|\psi\rangle\|$. There is no reason in general for
   $|\psi'\rangle$ to still have $\Delta E=0$. The measurement has
   projected the state, and projecting an energy eigenstate can leave
   something that is not one.

   **The hypothesis that repairs it.** If $[A,H]=0$ then $H$ commutes with
   each of $A$'s spectral projectors, so
   $H P_i|\psi\rangle = P_i H|\psi\rangle = E\,P_i|\psi\rangle$: the
   collapsed state is still an $H$-eigenstate with the same energy, still
   has $\Delta E=0$, and is therefore stationary. Nothing changes during
   the wait, and the second reading reproduces the first with certainty for
   any $\Delta t$. Compatibility with $H$ is what the informal argument was
   silently assuming.

   **The counterexample.** Take $H=\tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$ and
   $|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle$, an exact energy eigenstate, so $\Delta E=0$.
   Measure $A=X$, which does not commute with $H$. The outcome is $\pm1$
   with probability $\tfrac12$ each; say $+1$, leaving $|+\rangle$. In
   $|+\rangle$ the energy spread is $\Delta E=\hbar\omega/2$, no longer
   zero, so the state precesses:

   $$
   |\psi(\Delta t)\rangle \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle + e^{i\omega\Delta t}|1\rangle\right), \qquad P(X{=}{+}1\text{ again}) = \cos^2\!\left(\frac{\omega\Delta t}{2}\right)
   $$

   computed with the platform's own `rotationZ` and inner products. At
   $\omega\Delta t=\pi$ that probability is exactly $0$: the second
   measurement is certain to return $-1$, the opposite of the first. At
   $\omega\Delta t=\pi/2$ it is $\tfrac12$, a coin flip. Only at
   $\omega\Delta t=0,2\pi,4\pi,\ldots$ does it return $1$.

   So the correct statement has three parts. For $\Delta t=0$ the second
   reading always matches, whatever $A$ is, by idempotence alone. For any
   $\Delta t$ it matches when $[A,H]=0$. And for an incompatible $A$ it
   generally does not, because $\Delta E=0$ described the state you started
   with and the first measurement is what destroyed it.

   </details>

## Summary

- Spectral decomposition, compatibility, complete sets, generalized
  measurement, and energy-time uncertainty are five faces of the same
  underlying idea: the earlier courses' postulates, made precise for
  degenerate spectra and multi-observable systems.
- Each of those generalizations reduces exactly to its already-proven
  nondegenerate special case, a consistency requirement the derivations had
  to meet rather than a coincidence. The energy-time relation is the one
  result here that is not a generalization at all: it is new, derived from
  two results imported from earlier courses.
- Degeneracy is the generic situation for real physical systems with
  enough symmetry, not a mathematical edge case to note in passing, and
  this course's tools are the honest prerequisite for describing them
  correctly.
