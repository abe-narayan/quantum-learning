<LessonHook eyebrow="A third route, and it is geometric">
Perturbation theory needs a small parameter; the variational method needs a good guessed shape. WKB takes a third, geometric route entirely: treat the wavefunction as a classical wave and demand it close consistently on itself.
</LessonHook>

## Treat the wavefunction as a classical wave

WKB (Wentzel-Kramers-Brillouin) treats the <Term id="wavefunction">wavefunction</Term> as a classical wave
whose local wavelength is set by the local classical momentum
$p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))}$, and demands that the wave close consistently on
itself between the classical turning points.

The idea predates the Schrödinger equation. Bohr and Sommerfeld were
fitting whole numbers of waves into classical orbits in 1913, years
before anyone knew what was doing the waving, and the condition below is
recognisably theirs with one correction added. What the 1926 work
supplied was a derivation from the wave equation, plus a statement of
when the approximation can be trusted: the potential must vary slowly
over one de Broglie wavelength, which fails hardest right at the turning
points, where $p(x)\to0$ and the wavelength diverges.

## Turning points, and the closing condition

### The classically allowed region

For energy $E$ and potential $V(x)$, the classically allowed region is
wherever $E>V(x)$ (a classical particle with that energy could physically
be there); its boundary points (where $E=V(x)$) are the **turning
points**, $x_1<x_2$. The local classical momentum is
$p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))}$, real and positive only inside this region.

For the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>'s $n=1$ level ($E=1.5$), the turning points
sit at $x=\pm\sqrt{2E}\approx\pm1.732$, found by solving $E=V(x)=x^2/2$
directly:

The shaded band is where $E>V(x)$, and it is the region the action
integral $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx$ runs over. Outside it $p(x)$ turns
imaginary, which is why the integral stops at the turning points rather
than sweeping all of $x$. The quantum wavefunction does not stop there;
it decays into the forbidden region, and reproducing that decay correctly
is what the boundary-matching argument behind the $\tfrac12$ handles.

### The quantization condition

Demanding the semiclassical wave be single-valued and vanish smoothly at
both turning points (a technical boundary-matching argument this course
states rather than derives in full) gives:

$$
\int_{x_1}^{x_2} p(x)\,dx = \left(n+\frac12\right)\pi\hbar, \qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots
$$

This is the **Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization condition** (with the
$\frac12$ Maslov correction for two smooth/"soft" turning points, the
standard case for a smooth confining potential like the harmonic
oscillator). This platform's `wkbActionIntegral` computes the left side
as a Riemann sum over the grid restricted to where $E>V(x)$, and
`wkbQuantizedEnergy` bisects on $E$ until the integral matches the right
side for a given $n$. That is the same bisection Wave Mechanics'
`finiteSquareWellGroundStateEnergy` used on a different transcendental
equation.

### Why the harmonic oscillator comes out exact

For $V(x)=\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ the action integral can be done in closed
form. The turning points sit at $\pm a$ with $a^2=2E/(m\omega^2)$, and

$$
\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{2m\left(E-\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\right)}\,dx = m\omega\int_{-a}^{a}\sqrt{a^2-x^2}\,dx = m\omega\cdot\frac{\pi a^2}{2} = \frac{\pi E}{\omega}
$$

using the standard result that the integral of $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ across
its full range is the area of a half-disc, $\pi a^2/2$. Setting that
equal to $(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar$ and cancelling the $\pi$:

$$
\frac{E}{\omega} = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_n = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega
$$

which is the exact spectrum, at every $n$, with no approximation left
over. Nothing about semiclassical validity was invoked: the action
integral for a quadratic potential happens to be exactly linear in $E$,
and a linear condition has an exact solution. Any residual disagreement
in the table below is therefore the grid's fault and not the method's.

## Semiclassical in a literal sense

WKB is a semiclassical method in a literal sense: it is built from the
classical momentum $p(x)$, and quantum mechanics enters only through the
$\hbar$ on the right-hand side of the quantization condition. It works
best when the potential varies slowly over one de Broglie wavelength,
which is the large-quantum-number regime where a system starts to behave
classically.

That framing makes the harmonic oscillator look like a lucky accident,
and the calculation above shows it is not luck but a property of
quadratic potentials, provable in three lines. It is worth separating the
two claims: WKB is *asymptotically* good for generic potentials at large
$n$, and *exactly* right for this one at every $n$, for a reason that has
nothing to do with the asymptotics.

## Four WKB levels against four exact ones

**Compute the WKB-quantized energies for the harmonic oscillator
($m=\omega=1$) at $n=0,1,2,3$, and compare to the exact
$E_n=(n+\frac12)\hbar\omega$.**

Computed on a grid of spacing $dx=0.02$ (the resolution the engine's own
test suite uses; the coarser $dx=0.025$ grid that draws the potential
diagram above gives slightly different residuals, which is itself the
point):

| $n$ | Exact | WKB (this engine) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.5000 | 0.5003 | 0.00026 |
| 1 | 1.5000 | 1.4999 | 0.00008 |
| 2 | 2.5000 | 2.5003 | 0.00031 |
| 3 | 3.5000 | 3.4996 | 0.00038 |

Every WKB value matches to within about $0.05\%$, the worst case being
$n=0$ at $0.00026/0.5\approx0.05\%$. Those residuals are grid-resolution
error in the Riemann-sum action integral and the bisection, as the closed
form above establishes; the method itself contributes nothing to them.
The direct check is that changing the grid changes them: on the coarser
$dx=0.025$ grid the same four differences come out
$0.00035$, $0.00043$, $0.00041$, $0.00057$, moving with the discretization
rather than with $n$.

The table's four energies are exactly the ones the action integral
$\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)dx$ is evaluated over. Watching the local classical
momentum $p(x)=\sqrt{2(E-V(x))}$ itself, across only the classically
allowed region, shows directly why higher $n$ means a bigger action
integral: both the region's width (the turning points) and the peak
momentum grow together.

WKB is usually sold as a large-$n$ approximation, so it's worth pausing
on what that predicts for this specific table before looking at it
again:

## The oscillator's exactness is special

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking WKB is always this accurate.** The harmonic oscillator's
exactness is special (tied to its potential's specific quadratic shape).
For a generic potential, WKB is an *approximation* that improves as $n$
grows, and can be noticeably off for small $n$. This course doesn't
implement a second example to contrast against, but the distinction
matters for correctly interpreting the harmonic oscillator's near-perfect
result here.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $\frac12$ in $(n+\frac12)\pi\hbar$.** Omitting it
(using $n\pi\hbar$ instead) is a common error that shifts every
predicted energy level. The Maslov correction for two soft turning
points is not optional bookkeeping.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Where exactly does the classically allowed region's boundary sit, in
   terms of $E$ and $V(x)$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At the turning points, where $E=V(x)$: the solutions of that equation
   bound the region $E>V(x)$ where a classical particle of energy $E$
   could be found.

   For the harmonic oscillator at $E=1.5$ with $m=\omega=1$, solving
   $1.5=x^2/2$ gives $x=\pm\sqrt{2E}\approx\pm1.732$, the two markers on
   the potential diagram above.

   The single-well picture with two turning points is the case the
   quantization condition as written assumes, and it is not universal.
   A double well at low energy has *four* turning points and a
   disconnected allowed region, so $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}$ is ambiguous and the
   condition has to be restated (each well gets its own action integral,
   with a tunneling correction coupling them). A potential with one wall
   and one open side has only one turning point and no bound states at all
   above it. Counting the turning points is the first thing to do with a
   new potential, before writing anything down.

   </details>

2. Explain in words why $p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))}$ becomes imaginary
   outside the classically allowed region, and why the action integral
   only sums over the region where it's real.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Outside the allowed region $V(x)>E$, so $E-V(x)$ is negative and its
   square root is imaginary. Read through the physics, $E-V$ is the
   classical kinetic energy, so an imaginary $p$ is the algebra reporting
   that a classical particle would need negative kinetic energy to be
   there. It cannot be, and it stops at the turning point.

   The quantum wavefunction does not stop, and the distinction matters.
   Substituting an imaginary $p=i\hbar\kappa$ into $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ turns
   the oscillation into $e^{-\kappa x}$: not a wave but a decaying
   exponential, which is the tunneling tail from Wave Mechanics arriving
   here in semiclassical clothing. So the forbidden region is not empty.
   It just holds no oscillation.

   That is why the action integral stops at the turning points. The
   condition being imposed is that a *wave* fit consistently between two
   walls, and it counts accumulated phase. A decaying exponential
   accumulates no phase and contributes no nodes, so there is nothing for
   it to add to the count.

   The forbidden region is not ignored entirely, though, and this is where
   the $\tfrac12$ comes from. Matching the oscillating solution onto the
   decaying one at a soft turning point costs a phase of $\pi/4$, and
   there are two turning points, so the total is $\pi/2$. Divide through
   by $\pi$ and that is the $\tfrac12$ in $(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar$. The
   number is a record of how the wave connects to the tails it is not
   integrating over, which is why it changes if the turning points
   are hard walls instead of soft ones.

   </details>

3. If a potential's turning points move further apart as $E$ increases,
   what does this imply about how the action integral $\int p\,dx$
   behaves as $E$ grows? (Hint: this behavior is exactly what
   `wkbQuantizedEnergy`'s bisection search relies on.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The action integral increases strictly and monotonically with $E$, and
   for two independent reasons that push the same way.

   The integrand grows. At every fixed $x$ already inside the region,
   $p(x)=\sqrt{2m(E-V(x))}$ is an increasing function of $E$, so the whole
   curve lifts.

   The interval grows. The turning points move apart, so the integral
   covers more $x$, and the newly included stretch contributes a positive
   amount rather than cancelling anything.

   Both effects are visible in the momentum explorer above: each frame's
   dome is both taller and wider than the last, and its area, $\pi E$ for
   this potential, rises accordingly.

   Monotonicity is precisely the property bisection needs, and it is worth
   being explicit that it is a hypothesis rather than a convenience. The
   solver is looking for the $E$ where a continuous function
   $A(E)=\int p\,dx$ crosses the target $(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar$. Continuous
   and strictly increasing means that crossing exists once and only once,
   so bracketing it and halving the bracket converges to the one right
   answer. If $A(E)$ could rise and fall, bisection could bracket one root
   while a second sat elsewhere, and which one it returned would depend on
   where the search happened to start.

   There is a nice reading behind the monotonicity. $\oint p\,dx$ is the
   phase-space area enclosed by the classical orbit, and the quantization
   condition says each quantum state takes up an area of $2\pi\hbar$. So
   "the action grows with energy" is "higher-energy orbits enclose more
   phase space and therefore fit more states underneath them," and the
   number of levels below $E$ is about the enclosed area divided by
   Planck's constant.

   </details>

4. The closed-form action integral above gave $\pi E/\omega$, linear in
   $E$. Explain how that linearity is what makes the WKB condition exact
   here, and what would have to be true of a potential for the same
   argument to fail.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a linear action makes the quantization condition a linear
   equation, whose solution is evenly spaced levels, and the harmonic
   oscillator's true spectrum is evenly spaced. Both sides are linear in
   $n$ with the same slope and the same intercept, so they do not merely
   agree asymptotically. They are the same formula.

   Trace where each piece of the exact answer comes from:

   $$
   \frac{\pi E}{\omega} = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\pi\hbar \implies E_n = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega
   $$

   The linear dependence on $E$ produces the uniform spacing $\hbar\omega$;
   the coefficient $\pi/\omega$ fixes its size; and the Maslov $\tfrac12$
   becomes the zero-point energy. Change any one of them and the match
   fails somewhere.

   For the argument to fail, the action integral has to be nonlinear in
   $E$. That is the generic situation, since linearity came from the
   turning-point width and the peak momentum both scaling as $\sqrt{E}$,
   which is a property of a quadratic potential and not of potentials in
   general. Take $V(x)=|x|$: the turning points sit at $\pm E$ and

   $$
   \int_{-E}^{E}\sqrt{2m\left(E-|x|\right)}\,dx = \frac{4\sqrt{2m}}{3}\,E^{3/2}
   $$

   so WKB predicts $E_n\propto(n+\tfrac12)^{2/3}$, levels that crowd
   together as $n$ rises rather than staying evenly spaced. The exact
   spectrum for that potential is set by zeros of the Airy function and is
   not of that form. WKB is noticeably off for the lowest state and
   improves steadily as $n$ grows, which is the ordinary semiclassical
   behaviour the harmonic oscillator hides.

   A second, independent way to break it, worth separating because it is
   not about linearity at all. The $\tfrac12$ assumes two *soft* turning
   points, where $V$ crosses $E$ at a finite slope. An infinite square
   well has hard walls instead, the connection formulas are different, and
   using $(n+\tfrac12)$ there gives the wrong spectrum even though the
   action integral $L\sqrt{2mE}$ is perfectly tractable. Getting the
   Maslov index right is a separate question from getting the action
   right.

   </details>

## Summary

- WKB quantizes energy via
  $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)dx=(n+\frac12)\pi\hbar$, using the classical
  local momentum and the potential's turning points.
- This platform computes the action integral as a Riemann sum over the
  classically allowed region, and bisects on $E$ to match the
  quantization condition, reusing the same numerical strategy as the
  finite-well ground-state calculation.
- For the harmonic oscillator, WKB reproduces the exact spectrum to
  within grid-resolution numerical error at every $n$ tested, a special
  exactness of this potential, not a general WKB guarantee.
