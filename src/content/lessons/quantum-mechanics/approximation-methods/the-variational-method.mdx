## When nothing in H resembles anything solved

Perturbation theory needed $H=H_0+H'$ with $H'$ small, which is useless
when no part of $H$ resembles anything already solved. That describes
most interacting systems.

The variational method drops the requirement entirely. It asks: given any
guessed normalized wavefunction, what can be said about the ground
energy? The answer is a single inequality, and the surprising part is
that it holds for *every* guess, including bad ones, with no smallness
assumption anywhere.

That one-sidedness is what makes the method usable. A method that
sometimes overestimates and sometimes underestimates tells you little
from a single run. A method that only ever overestimates turns "try
something and see" into a chain of provable upper bounds, each one an
honest statement about a system nobody can solve.

## The theorem, then minimizing over a family

### The variational theorem

For any normalized trial state $|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle$ (not
necessarily an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> of anything),

$$
\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle \geq E_0
$$

where $E_0$ is the true ground-state energy.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Expand the trial state in H's own true eigenbasis. Nobody knows what those eigenstates are, which is the entire problem, but the expansion exists for any normalized state and that is all the proof needs.">
$$
|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle, \qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Sandwich H between the expansion. Cross terms vanish by orthogonality, leaving each eigenstate contributing its own eigenvalue Eₙ weighted by |cₙ|²: the trial energy is a weighted average of the true energies.">
$$
\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A weighted average cannot fall below the smallest thing being averaged. Replace every Eₙ by E₀ and the sum can only go down, and the weights already sum to 1.">
$$
\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\geq\sum_n|c_n|^2E_0=E_0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Using it: minimize over trial parameters

Pick a trial wavefunction family with adjustable parameters. This
course uses a Gaussian, $\psi(x;\sigma)\propto e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}$, with
$\sigma$ (width) as the one free parameter. Compute
$E(\sigma)=\langle\psi_\sigma|H|\psi_\sigma\rangle$ for each $\sigma$,
then minimize over $\sigma$. Every value of $E(\sigma)$ is already an
upper bound on $E_0$; minimizing finds the tightest bound this particular
family can supply. Note what minimization is *not* doing: it is not
converging on the truth, it is choosing the best member of a family that
may not contain the truth at all.

### Reusing this platform's existing machinery

No new continuous-wavefunction infrastructure is needed:
`Wavefunction1D.gaussianPacket` (Wave Mechanics) already builds a
normalized Gaussian on the grid, and `expectationEnergy` (built from the
same kinetic + potential expectation-value machinery Wave Mechanics
verified) already computes $\langle H\rangle$ for it. This lesson's
`minimizeGaussianTrialEnergy` is a thin wrapper: scan $\sigma$ over a
range, evaluate `expectationEnergy` at each, keep the minimum. This is
the same direct-search optimization strategy Quantum Algorithms II's VQE
lesson already used for a different (qubit) variational problem.

## Where the method earns its keep

The method earns its keep when the true answer is unavailable. For a
many-body Hamiltonian with more electrons or spins than can be
diagonalized, minimizing $\langle H\rangle$ over an educated trial family
is often the only route to a number at all, and the theorem guarantees
the error has a known sign: too high, never too low.

A known sign is worth more than it first appears. Two different research
groups with two different trial families can compare answers and the
lower one is provably better, without either group knowing $E_0$. That is
how quantum chemistry makes progress on molecules no computer can
diagonalize, and it is what a variational quantum eigensolver does on
hardware, with a parameterized circuit playing the part of the Gaussian
width $\sigma$ used here.

## Scanning a Gaussian's width against the exact answer

**Minimize the Gaussian trial energy for the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>
($m=\omega=1$), and compare to the exact $E_0=0.5$.**

Scanning $\sigma$ from 0.2 to 3 in 300 steps and calling
`expectationEnergy` at each gives a minimum at $\sigma\approx0.704$ with
$E(\sigma)\approx0.50002$, within $0.004\%$ of the exact $E_0=0.5$.

That agreement is too good to be typical, and the reason is worth
naming: the harmonic oscillator's true ground state *is* a Gaussian, so
the trial family contains the exact answer and the minimization has
nothing to do but find it. The residual $0.00002$ is not method error
either: the true optimum is $\sigma=1/\sqrt2\approx0.7071$, and a
300-step scan of $[0.2,3]$ has no sample there, landing on $0.704$
instead. Evaluate the same $\langle H\rangle$ at $0.7071$ and it returns
$0.500000$. The gap is the resolution of the search, not of the physics.

Run the same procedure on a potential whose ground state is not
Gaussian-shaped, a double well for instance, and the machinery works
identically and still returns a valid upper bound. It is simply a looser
one, because no width of a Gaussian fits the true shape. The theorem
never degrades; only the tightness does.

Before scanning the whole range, it's worth checking whether intuition
about "closer to the optimum" actually predicts the ranking correctly:

Read the two component curves first. Squeezing the trial narrow drives
$\langle T\rangle$ up like $1/\sigma^2$, because a confined packet needs
large momentum components; spreading it wide drives $\langle V\rangle$ up
like $\sigma^2$, because the walls of the well are climbing. Their sum
has to have a minimum somewhere in between, and that is the whole
mechanism behind the variational optimum. It is also, in miniature, why
the ground state has nonzero energy at all: the two costs cannot be
minimized at once.

The two costs are not mirror images, which is what the prediction above
turned on. A $1/\sigma^2$ wall rises far faster than a $\sigma^2$ slope,
so the curve is steep on the narrow side and shallow on the wide one, and
a width that overshoots the optimum can beat one that undershoots by
less. Reading closeness in $\sigma$ off the horizontal axis and expecting
it to rank two trials is exactly the mistake the asymmetry punishes.

The total curve sits at or above the dashed $E_0=0.5$ line everywhere, as
the theorem requires, touching it only at $\sigma\approx0.704$. It has no
way to dip below, whatever width you try.

## Normalize the trial state first

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the trial wavefunction must be normalized before computing
⟨H⟩.** `gaussianPacket` normalizes automatically, but a hand-built trial
function must be explicitly normalized first, or the $\langle H\rangle\geq E_0$
inequality doesn't hold as stated (it needs
$\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=1$ specifically).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a lower ⟨H⟩ always means a "better" trial family in every
sense.** It means a tighter upper bound on $E_0$ specifically. The
theorem says nothing about how close the trial *wavefunction itself* is
to the true ground state, only about the energy expectation value.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. State the variational theorem in one sentence, including which
   direction the inequality goes and why.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   For any normalized trial state,
   $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\ge E_0$, always an upper bound and never a
   lower one, because expanding $|\psi\rangle$ in $H$'s true eigenbasis
   turns the left side into a weighted average of the exact energies
   $\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n$, and no weighted average can fall below the smallest
   number being averaged.

   Three hypotheses are packed into that sentence and all three earn their
   place.

   $|\psi\rangle$ must be normalized. The weights $|c_n|^2$ have to sum to
   $1$ for the final step to work; an unnormalized state scales
   $\langle H\rangle$ by an arbitrary factor and the inequality means
   nothing.

   $H$ must have a lowest eigenvalue for $E_0$ to name something. This is
   true of any bound-state Hamiltonian in this course and is not automatic
   in general.

   The eigenbasis has to exist for the expansion, which Hermiticity of $H$
   supplies. Notice that the proof never asks you to *know* that
   eigenbasis. It exists, the expansion is therefore legitimate, and the
   argument runs to completion using only that. Proving something with an
   object you cannot compute is the move worth taking away from this
   derivation.

   One sharpening: equality holds if and only if $|\psi\rangle$ is itself a
   ground state, since any weight on a higher $E_n$ strictly raises the
   average.

   </details>

2. A badly-chosen fixed width ($\sigma=3$, far from the optimum) gives a
   *higher* trial energy than the optimized $\sigma\approx0.704$. Is this
   consistent with the variational theorem? Explain.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes, and it is what the theorem predicts rather than something it
   merely tolerates. The theorem places a floor under every trial energy
   and no ceiling at all. Both $\sigma=0.704$ and $\sigma=3$ produce
   numbers at or above $E_0=0.5$, and how far above is unconstrained.

   The only result that would contradict the theorem is a trial energy
   *below* $0.5$. A bad guess giving a bad number is the one-sidedness
   working as designed: worse guesses climb higher, and none of them can
   sink underneath.

   The practical consequence is worth stating plainly, because it is the
   method's real limitation. You get the sign of the error for free and
   never its size. Reading $E(\sigma)=0.62$ off some system with no known
   answer tells you $E_0\le0.62$ and nothing else: the truth could be
   $0.61$ or $0.2$. The one escape is comparison. Two different trial
   families, or the same family at two parameter values, can be ranked
   with certainty, since the lower number is provably the better bound.
   Progress comes from ordering guesses against each other, never from
   grading one against the unknown truth.

   </details>

3. If a trial wavefunction family happens to include the true ground
   state exactly (at some specific parameter value), what does the
   variational method's minimization procedure find?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It finds the exact answer: the minimum over the family equals $E_0$,
   attained at that parameter value, with the trial state equal to the
   true ground state.

   The reason is the equality condition from Question 1. $\langle H\rangle
   =E_0$ requires all the weight on the ground eigenstate, so the minimum
   over any family containing that state cannot be lower (the theorem
   forbids it) and cannot be higher (that member achieves it).

   This is the situation in the worked example, which is why the numbers
   look implausibly good. The harmonic oscillator's ground state *is* a
   Gaussian, at $\sigma=1/\sqrt2$, so the family contains the answer and
   the search has only to locate it. The residual $0.00002$ is not the
   method being slightly wrong: a 300-point scan of $[0.2,3]$ simply has no
   sample at $0.7071$, and evaluating there returns $0.500000$.

   Two cautions, since this case is the exception and it is easy to
   generalize from it.

   Containing the answer is not the same as finding it. A coarse scan can
   step over the optimum, and a minimizer on a family with several
   parameters can settle into a local minimum and stop. Exactness is a
   statement about the family, and the search is a separate problem that
   can fail on its own.

   The reverse inference does not hold either. A trial energy very close
   to $E_0$ does not mean the trial *state* is close to the ground state.
   The energy error is second order in the state error near the minimum,
   which is exactly why variational energies are much better than
   variational wavefunctions, and why a variational calculation that
   reports an excellent energy can still give poor expectation values for
   other observables.

   </details>

4. Why doesn't the variational method require identifying a "small"
   part of the Hamiltonian, unlike perturbation theory?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because nothing was expanded. Go back through the three-step proof and
   check what it consumed: a normalized state, an eigenbasis expansion,
   and the observation that an average is at least its smallest term.
   There is no series, so there is no small parameter for a series to
   converge in, and there is nothing to truncate.

   Perturbation theory is a different kind of object. It produces an
   expansion in $\lambda$ that is only useful while successive terms
   shrink, and the lesson before this one showed exactly what its failure
   looks like: slide $\lambda$ up and the second-order term catches the
   first. That requirement is structural, not a matter of care. Without a
   solvable $H_0$ and a genuinely small $H'$, the method has no starting
   point.

   The variational method still needs something, and it is honest to say
   what. It needs a good guess about the *shape* of the answer, encoded in
   the trial family. That is a demand on physical insight rather than on
   the Hamiltonian's structure, which is why the two methods apply to
   different situations rather than one dominating the other.

   The trade in full:

   Perturbation theory gives a systematic route to improvement, another
   order whenever you want one, along with excited states, the corrected
   wavefunctions, and a rough error estimate from the size of the next
   term. It needs a solvable neighbour.

   The variational method gives one number with a guaranteed direction of
   error, for any Hamiltonian at all. It gives no error size, no systematic
   improvement beyond enlarging the family by hand, and no excited states
   without an extra orthogonality constraint that presupposes the ground
   state you just computed.

   The methods are complementary in practice, which is why quantum
   chemistry uses both, often on the same molecule.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle\geq E_0$ for
  any normalized trial state, proven by expanding in $H$'s own true
  (unknown) eigenbasis.
- Minimizing over a trial family's parameters finds the tightest upper
  bound that family can produce, reusing this platform's existing
  `Wavefunction1D`/`expectationEnergy` machinery directly.
- For the harmonic oscillator, a Gaussian trial's optimized energy
  matches the exact $E_0=0.5$ to within $0.004\%$, expected since the
  true ground state is itself Gaussian-shaped here.
