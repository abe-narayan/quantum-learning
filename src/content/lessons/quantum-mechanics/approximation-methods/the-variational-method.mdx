<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
  Guess any <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term> wavefunction at all, however crude, and its energy
  expectation value is guaranteed to sit at or above the true ground
  energy. That single inequality is the entire method.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

Perturbation theory needed $H=H_0+H'$ with $H'$ small, which is useless
when no part of $H$ resembles anything already solved. That describes
most interacting systems.

The variational method drops the requirement entirely. It asks: given any
guessed normalized wavefunction, what can be said about the ground
energy? The answer is a single inequality, and the surprising part is
that it holds for *every* guess, including bad ones, with no smallness
assumption anywhere.

That one-sidedness is what makes the method usable. A method that
sometimes overestimates and sometimes underestimates tells you little
from a single run. A method that only ever overestimates turns "try
something and see" into a chain of provable upper bounds, each one an
honest statement about a system nobody can solve.

## Mathematical Development

### The variational theorem

For any normalized trial state $|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle$ (not
necessarily an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> of anything),

$$
\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle \geq E_0
$$

where $E_0$ is the true ground-state energy.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Expand the trial state in H's own true eigenbasis. Nobody knows what those eigenstates are, which is the entire problem, but the expansion exists for any normalized state and that is all the proof needs.">
$$
|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle, \qquad \sum_n|c_n|^2=1
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Sandwich H between the expansion. Cross terms vanish by orthogonality, leaving each eigenstate contributing its own eigenvalue Eₙ weighted by |cₙ|²: the trial energy is a weighted average of the true energies.">
$$
\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A weighted average cannot fall below the smallest thing being averaged. Replace every Eₙ by E₀ and the sum can only go down, and the weights already sum to 1.">
$$
\sum_n|c_n|^2E_n\geq\sum_n|c_n|^2E_0=E_0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### Using it: minimize over trial parameters

Pick a trial wavefunction family with adjustable parameters. This
course uses a Gaussian, $\psi(x;\sigma)\propto e^{-x^2/4\sigma^2}$, with
$\sigma$ (width) as the one free parameter. Compute
$E(\sigma)=\langle\psi_\sigma|H|\psi_\sigma\rangle$ for each $\sigma$,
then minimize over $\sigma$. Every value of $E(\sigma)$ is already an
upper bound on $E_0$; minimizing finds the tightest bound this particular
family can supply. Note what minimization is *not* doing: it is not
converging on the truth, it is choosing the best member of a family that
may not contain the truth at all.

### Reusing this platform's existing machinery

No new continuous-wavefunction infrastructure is needed:
`Wavefunction1D.gaussianPacket` (Wave Mechanics) already builds a
normalized Gaussian on the grid, and `expectationEnergy` (built from the
same kinetic + potential expectation-value machinery Wave Mechanics
verified) already computes $\langle H\rangle$ for it. This lesson's
`minimizeGaussianTrialEnergy` is a thin wrapper: scan $\sigma$ over a
range, evaluate `expectationEnergy` at each, keep the minimum. This is
the same direct-search optimization strategy Quantum Algorithms II's VQE
lesson already used for a different (qubit) variational problem.

## Physical Interpretation

The method earns its keep when the true answer is unavailable. For a
many-body Hamiltonian with more electrons or spins than can be
diagonalized, minimizing $\langle H\rangle$ over an educated trial family
is often the only route to a number at all, and the theorem guarantees
the error has a known sign: too high, never too low.

A known sign is worth more than it first appears. Two different research
groups with two different trial families can compare answers and the
lower one is provably better, without either group knowing $E_0$. That is
how quantum chemistry makes progress on molecules no computer can
diagonalize, and it is what a variational quantum eigensolver does on
hardware, with a parameterized circuit playing the part of the Gaussian
width $\sigma$ used here.

## Worked Example

**Minimize the Gaussian trial energy for the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term>
($m=\omega=1$), and compare to the exact $E_0=0.5$.**

Scanning $\sigma$ from 0.2 to 3 in 300 steps and calling
`expectationEnergy` at each gives a minimum at $\sigma\approx0.704$ with
$E(\sigma)\approx0.50002$, within $0.004\%$ of the exact $E_0=0.5$.

That agreement is too good to be typical, and the reason is worth
naming: the harmonic oscillator's true ground state *is* a Gaussian, so
the trial family contains the exact answer and the minimization has
nothing to do but find it. The residual $0.00002$ is grid discretization,
not method error.

Run the same procedure on a potential whose ground state is not
Gaussian-shaped, a double well for instance, and the machinery works
identically and still returns a valid upper bound. It is simply a looser
one, because no width of a Gaussian fits the true shape. The theorem
never degrades; only the tightness does.

Before scanning the whole range, it's worth checking whether intuition
about "closer to the optimum" actually predicts the ranking correctly:

/>

Read the two component curves first. Squeezing the trial narrow drives
$\langle T\rangle$ up like $1/\sigma^2$, because a confined packet needs
large momentum components; spreading it wide drives $\langle V\rangle$ up
like $\sigma^2$, because the walls of the well are climbing. Their sum
has to have a minimum somewhere in between, and that is the whole
mechanism behind the variational optimum. It is also, in miniature, why
the ground state has nonzero energy at all: the two costs cannot be
minimized at once.

The total curve sits at or above the dashed $E_0=0.5$ line everywhere, as
the theorem requires, touching it only at $\sigma\approx0.704$. It has no
way to dip below, whatever width you try.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the trial wavefunction must be normalized before computing
⟨H⟩.** `gaussianPacket` normalizes automatically, but a hand-built trial
function must be explicitly normalized first, or the $\langle H\rangle\geq E_0$
inequality doesn't hold as stated (it needs
$\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle=1$ specifically).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a lower ⟨H⟩ always means a "better" trial family in every
sense.** It means a tighter upper bound on $E_0$ specifically. The
theorem says nothing about how close the trial *wavefunction itself* is
to the true ground state, only about the energy expectation value.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\langle\psi_{\text{trial}}|H|\psi_{\text{trial}}\rangle\geq E_0$ for
  any normalized trial state, proven by expanding in $H$'s own true
  (unknown) eigenbasis.
- Minimizing over a trial family's parameters finds the tightest upper
  bound that family can produce, reusing this platform's existing
  `Wavefunction1D`/`expectationEnergy` machinery directly.
- For the harmonic oscillator, a Gaussian trial's optimized energy
  matches the exact $E_0=0.5$ to within $0.004\%$, expected since the
  true ground state is itself Gaussian-shaped here.
