<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Exactly solvable quantum systems are rare, and nudging one destroys its solvability outright. Perturbation theory asks a smaller question instead: if the easy version is fully understood, can the almost-easy version be reached by correcting it, one order at a time?
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

The list of quantum systems with closed-form solutions is short enough to
write out: the free particle, the infinite well, the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic
oscillator</Term>, hydrogen, and a handful of relatives. Everything of physical
interest is somewhere off that list.

And the list is fragile. Add a stray electric field to hydrogen, a
whisper of anharmonicity to the oscillator, a second electron to the
atom, and the closed form is gone. Not harder to find; gone.

Perturbation theory declines to start over. If $H=H_0+H'$ with $H_0$
solved and $H'$ small, it builds the answer as a series of corrections to
$H_0$'s known energies and states, each order requiring nothing but
matrix elements of $H'$ between states you already have. What "small"
has to mean is the question the end of this lesson takes seriously.

The method is usually called Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory:
Schrödinger set it out in his 1926 papers, and Lord Rayleigh had worked
the analogous problem for coupled classical oscillators decades earlier.
The algebra transferred to quantum mechanics essentially unchanged.

## Intuition

Treat $H_0$'s eigenstates as fixed reference points you already
understand completely, and $H'$ as a small push away from each one. To
first order, a state doesn't move at all: it just picks up an energy
shift equal to how much $H'$ pushes back against itself in its own
direction, $\langle n|H'|n\rangle$, no mixing with any other state
required. Real mixing only shows up at second order, where every other
state $m$ contributes a tug proportional to how strongly $H'$ couples $n$
to $m$, and inversely to how far apart their unperturbed energies sit:
close, easily-perturbed neighbors matter far more than distant ones.

## Mathematical Development

### Setup: H₀'s eigenbasis

Let $H_0|n^{(0)}\rangle=E_n^{(0)}|n^{(0)}\rangle$ be already solved (this
course's harmonic oscillator, or hydrogen, are natural choices for
$H_0$). Writing $H'$ as a matrix *in this same eigenbasis*, so that
$H'_{mn}=\langle m^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle$, is the essential setup step
every formula below assumes.

### First-order energy correction

$$
E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle = H'_{nn}
$$

This is the diagonal entry of $H'$ in the $H_0$ eigenbasis, and nothing
else: no sum to evaluate, no other states consulted. The formula follows
from expanding $(H_0+H')|\psi_n\rangle=E_n|\psi_n\rangle$ in powers of
$H'$ and collecting the first-order terms, but using it needs none of
that.

It is worth pausing on how cheap that is. To first order, the energy
shift of a state is the average of the perturbation over the state you
already had. The state's own change does not enter, because the piece of
the state that moves is orthogonal to the piece you are averaging over.

### Second-order energy correction

$$
E_n^{(2)} = \sum_{m\neq n} \frac{|H'_{mn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}
$$

Every *other* state $m$ contributes here, weighted by how strongly $H'$
couples $n$ to $m$ ($|H'_{mn}|^2$) and how close their unperturbed
energies are (a small energy gap in the denominator makes a close-lying
state contribute disproportionately). This formula assumes $E_n^{(0)}$
is non-degenerate. That's a real restriction, not fine print: degenerate
perturbation theory needs an extra diagonalization step this course
doesn't cover.

### First-order state correction

$$
|n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m\neq n} \frac{H'_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|m^{(0)}\rangle
$$

The perturbed state "mixes in" other unperturbed eigenstates here, with
coefficients built from the same matrix elements and energy gaps as
above. By convention, the $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ component of this correction
is taken to be exactly 0, fixing the overall <Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> freedom at
this order.

For the quartic anharmonic oscillator's ground state, this sum is mostly
empty. $x^4$ obeys a selection rule: it only connects states at most four
levels apart and never connects states of opposite parity, so
$H'=\lambda x^4$ only couples $|0^{(0)}\rangle$ to $|2^{(0)}\rangle$ and
$|4^{(0)}\rangle$ among the 8 states in this course's truncated <Term id="basis">basis</Term>.
`firstOrderStateCorrection(H0diag, Hprime, 0)`, computed here from the
exact same $H_0$ and $H'=\lambda x^4$ matrices as the worked example
above, makes that concrete: every coefficient $c_m$ for odd $m$, and for
$m=6$, comes back exactly 0.

Only $|2^{(0)}\rangle$ and $|4^{(0)}\rangle$ can mix in at all, so the
question is just which one dominates:

## Physical Interpretation

Perturbation theory is only as good as "$H'$ small" turns out to be, and
that assumption is easy to accept when nothing is available to check it
against. The worked example below is chosen because something is: the
quartic oscillator's first-order shift has a closed-form value that can
be derived without any perturbation theory at all.

The same machinery is what Hydrogen's fine-structure treatment was
quietly using. Relativistic kinetic corrections and spin-orbit coupling
were both handled there as a small $H'$ on top of the solved Coulomb
$H_0$, and $E_n^{(1)}=H'_{nn}$ is the statement that licensed it.

## Worked Example

**The quartic anharmonic oscillator, $H=H_0+\lambda x^4$ ($H_0$ the
ordinary harmonic oscillator, $m=\omega=\hbar=1$). Find the first-order
ground-state energy shift.**

Using $x=\sqrt{1/2}(a+a^\dagger)$ (Harmonic Oscillator's ladder
operators), a closed-form identity for the harmonic oscillator gives
$\langle0|x^4|0\rangle=\frac34$ exactly, independent of perturbation
theory, a fact about $H_0$'s own ground state alone. So:

$$
E_0^{(1)} = \lambda\langle0|x^4|0\rangle = \frac{3\lambda}{4}
$$

Two routes, one answer. The platform builds the $x^4$ matrix from
`positionOperator` (itself assembled from `annihilationOperator` and
`creationOperator`) and reads off `firstOrderEnergyCorrection(H0diag,
Hprime, 0)`, giving $0.0075$ at $\lambda=0.01$. The closed form gives
$\tfrac34(0.01)=0.0075$. Neither calculation knows about the other.

The corrections stack in order, each one a smaller nudge than the last:
adding `secondOrderEnergyCorrection(H0diag, Hprime, 0)` on top gives the
full second-order-corrected energy directly below, computed from the same
$8$-state truncated basis and the same $H'=\lambda x^4$ matrix, not a
separate calculation.

Near $\lambda=0$ the two approximations sit on top of each other, because
the second-order term is $O(\lambda^2)$ and vanishes faster than the
first. As $\lambda$ grows they separate, and the gap between consecutive
orders is the practical diagnostic: while it is shrinking, the series is
telling you something; once it stops shrinking, the next order would be
no better and the method has run out. Nothing in the formulas announces
this, which is why the check has to be made by hand.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Using H′ in the wrong basis.** These formulas require $H'_{mn}$
computed in $H_0$'s *own* eigenbasis, not an arbitrary basis (like the
position basis directly): the matrix elements $\langle m^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle$
depend on this specific choice.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying second-order perturbation theory to a degenerate $E_n^{(0)}$.**
The $E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}$ denominator is exactly zero for a truly
degenerate pair, and the formula breaks (this platform's engine
deliberately throws rather than silently returning $\pm\infty$ or NaN).
</Callout>

## Summary

- $H=H_0+H'$ with $H_0$ solvable: $E_n^{(1)}=H'_{nn}$,
  $E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq n}|H'_{mn}|^2/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})$,
  $|n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{H'_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}|m^{(0)}\rangle$.
- All three require $H'$ expressed in $H_0$'s eigenbasis, and
  non-degenerate $E_n^{(0)}$.
- The quartic anharmonic oscillator's first-order shift, $\frac34\lambda$,
  agrees with an independently derived matrix element, which makes it a
  check on the machinery rather than a restatement of it.
- The series carries no built-in warning when it stops working. Comparing
  consecutive orders, as the curve above does, is the available substitute.
