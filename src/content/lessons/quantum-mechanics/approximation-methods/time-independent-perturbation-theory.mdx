<LessonHook eyebrow="Nudge a solvable system and solvability dies">
Exactly solvable quantum systems are rare, and nudging one destroys its solvability outright. Perturbation theory asks a smaller question instead: if the easy version is fully understood, can the almost-easy version be reached by correcting it, one order at a time?
</LessonHook>

## A list of solved systems short enough to write out

The list of quantum systems with closed-form solutions is short enough to
write out: the free particle, the infinite well, the <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic
oscillator</Term>, hydrogen, and a handful of relatives. Everything of physical
interest is somewhere off that list.

And the list is fragile. Add a stray electric field to hydrogen, a
whisper of anharmonicity to the oscillator, a second electron to the
atom, and the closed form is gone. Not harder to find; gone.

Perturbation theory declines to start over. If $H=H_0+H'$ with $H_0$
solved and $H'$ small, it builds the answer as a series of corrections to
$H_0$'s known energies and states, each order requiring nothing but
matrix elements of $H'$ between states you already have. What "small"
has to mean is the question the end of this lesson takes seriously.

The method is usually called Rayleigh-Schrödinger perturbation theory:
Schrödinger set it out in his 1926 papers, and Lord Rayleigh had worked
the analogous problem for coupled classical oscillators decades earlier.
The algebra transferred to quantum mechanics essentially unchanged.

## Fixed reference points, and a small push

Treat $H_0$'s eigenstates as fixed reference points you already
understand completely, and $H'$ as a small push away from each one. To
first order, a state doesn't move at all: it just picks up an energy
shift equal to how much $H'$ pushes back against itself in its own
direction, $\langle n|H'|n\rangle$, no mixing with any other state
required. Real mixing only shows up at second order, where every other
state $m$ contributes a tug proportional to how strongly $H'$ couples $n$
to $m$, and inversely to how far apart their unperturbed energies sit:
close, easily-perturbed neighbors matter far more than distant ones.

## Corrections, order by order

### Setup: H₀'s eigenbasis

Let $H_0|n^{(0)}\rangle=E_n^{(0)}|n^{(0)}\rangle$ be already solved (this
course's harmonic oscillator, or hydrogen, are natural choices for
$H_0$). Writing $H'$ as a matrix *in this same eigenbasis*, so that
$H'_{mn}=\langle m^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle$, is the essential setup step
every formula below assumes.

### First-order energy correction

$$
E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle = H'_{nn}
$$

This is the diagonal entry of $H'$ in the $H_0$ eigenbasis, and nothing
else: no sum to evaluate, no other states consulted. The formula follows
from expanding $(H_0+H')|\psi_n\rangle=E_n|\psi_n\rangle$ in powers of
$H'$ and collecting the first-order terms, but using it needs none of
that.

It is worth pausing on how cheap that is. To first order, the energy
shift of a state is the average of the perturbation over the state you
already had. The state's own change does not enter, because the piece of
the state that moves is orthogonal to the piece you are averaging over.

### Second-order energy correction

$$
E_n^{(2)} = \sum_{m\neq n} \frac{|H'_{mn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}
$$

Every *other* state $m$ contributes here, weighted by how strongly $H'$
couples $n$ to $m$ ($|H'_{mn}|^2$) and how close their unperturbed
energies are (a small energy gap in the denominator makes a close-lying
state contribute disproportionately). This formula assumes $E_n^{(0)}$
is non-degenerate. That's a real restriction, not fine print: degenerate
perturbation theory needs an extra diagonalization step this course
doesn't cover.

### First-order state correction

$$
|n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m\neq n} \frac{H'_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}\,|m^{(0)}\rangle
$$

The perturbed state "mixes in" other unperturbed eigenstates here, with
coefficients built from the same matrix elements and energy gaps as
above. By convention, the $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ component of this correction
is taken to be exactly 0, fixing the overall <Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> freedom at
this order.

For the quartic anharmonic oscillator's ground state, this sum is mostly
empty. $x^4$ obeys a selection rule: it only connects states at most four
levels apart and never connects states of opposite parity, so
$H'=\lambda x^4$ only couples $|0^{(0)}\rangle$ to $|2^{(0)}\rangle$ and
$|4^{(0)}\rangle$ among the 8 states in this course's truncated <Term id="basis">basis</Term>.
`firstOrderStateCorrection(H0diag, Hprime, 0)`, computed here from the
exact same $H_0$ and $H'=\lambda x^4$ matrices as the worked example
above, makes that concrete: every coefficient $c_m$ for odd $m$, and for
$m=6$, comes back exactly 0.

Only $|2^{(0)}\rangle$ and $|4^{(0)}\rangle$ can mix in at all, and
$|c_2|$ is the larger of the two: the $1/(E_0^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})$ weight
alone says so, since $|4^{(0)}\rangle$ sits twice as far away. What that
weight does not settle is by how much.

## How to tell whether small was small enough

Perturbation theory is only as good as "$H'$ small" turns out to be, and
that assumption is easy to accept when nothing is available to check it
against. The worked example below is chosen because something is: the
quartic oscillator's first-order shift has a closed-form value that can
be derived without any perturbation theory at all.

The same machinery is what Hydrogen's fine-structure treatment was
quietly using. Relativistic kinetic corrections and spin-orbit coupling
were both handled there as a small $H'$ on top of the solved Coulomb
$H_0$, and $E_n^{(1)}=H'_{nn}$ is the statement that licensed it.

## The quartic oscillator's first-order shift

**The quartic anharmonic oscillator, $H=H_0+\lambda x^4$ ($H_0$ the
ordinary harmonic oscillator, $m=\omega=\hbar=1$). Find the first-order
ground-state energy shift.**

The matrix element $\langle0|x^4|0\rangle$ can be had in two lines from
the Harmonic Oscillator's ladder operators, with no perturbation theory
anywhere in it. With $x=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(a+a^\dagger)$ and $a|0\rangle=0$,

$$
x^2|0\rangle = \tfrac12\left(a^2+aa^\dagger+a^\dagger a+a^{\dagger2}\right)|0\rangle = \tfrac12|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|2\rangle
$$

and since $x^2$ is Hermitian, $\langle0|x^4|0\rangle$ is just the squared
norm of that vector: $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2+\left(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\right)^2=\tfrac14+\tfrac12=\tfrac34$,
exactly. That is a fact about $H_0$'s own ground state alone. So:

$$
E_0^{(1)} = \lambda\langle0|x^4|0\rangle = \frac{3\lambda}{4}
$$

Two routes, one answer. The platform builds the $x^4$ matrix from
`positionOperator` (itself assembled from `annihilationOperator` and
`creationOperator`) and reads off `firstOrderEnergyCorrection(H0diag,
Hprime, 0)`, giving $0.0075$ at $\lambda=0.01$. The closed form gives
$\tfrac34(0.01)=0.0075$. Neither calculation knows about the other.

The corrections stack in order, each one a smaller nudge than the last:
adding `secondOrderEnergyCorrection(H0diag, Hprime, 0)` on top gives the
full second-order-corrected energy directly below, computed from the same
$8$-state truncated basis and the same $H'=\lambda x^4$ matrix, not a
separate calculation.

Near $\lambda=0$ the two approximations sit on top of each other, because
the second-order term is $O(\lambda^2)$ and vanishes faster than the
first. As $\lambda$ grows they separate, and the gap between consecutive
orders is the practical diagnostic: while it is shrinking, the series is
telling you something; once it stops shrinking, the next order would be
no better and the method has run out. Nothing in the formulas announces
this, which is why the check has to be made by hand.

## The basis these formulas require

<Callout type="warning">
**Using H′ in the wrong basis.** These formulas require $H'_{mn}$
computed in $H_0$'s *own* eigenbasis, not an arbitrary basis (like the
position basis directly): the matrix elements $\langle m^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle$
depend on this specific choice.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying second-order perturbation theory to a degenerate $E_n^{(0)}$.**
The $E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}$ denominator is exactly zero for a truly
degenerate pair, and the formula breaks (this platform's engine
deliberately throws rather than silently returning $\pm\infty$ or NaN).
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Explain in words why $E_n^{(1)}=H'_{nn}$ requires no sum over other
   states, while $E_n^{(2)}$ does.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because at first order the unknown state correction drops out of the
   equation, and at second order it does not.

   Follow the algebra once and the asymmetry stops looking arbitrary.
   Write $H=H_0+\lambda H'$ and expand both the state and the energy in
   powers of $\lambda$. Collecting the $O(\lambda)$ terms of
   $H|\psi_n\rangle=E_n|\psi_n\rangle$ gives

   $$
   H_0|n^{(1)}\rangle + H'|n^{(0)}\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle + E_n^{(1)}|n^{(0)}\rangle
   $$

   which still contains the unknown $|n^{(1)}\rangle$. Project it onto
   $\langle n^{(0)}|$ and watch that unknown leave. Because $H_0$ is
   Hermitian it can act leftward on $\langle n^{(0)}|$, giving
   $\langle n^{(0)}|H_0|n^{(1)}\rangle=E_n^{(0)}\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle$,
   which is identical to the first term on the right. The two cancel, and
   what is left is

   $$
   E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)}|H'|n^{(0)}\rangle
   $$

   with no reference to how the state moved. Hermiticity of $H_0$ is the
   load-bearing hypothesis; without it the cancellation does not happen.

   At second order the same projection gives
   $E_n^{(2)}=\langle n^{(0)}|H'|n^{(1)}\rangle$, which needs
   $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ explicitly, and $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ is a sum over every
   other state. So the sum in $E_n^{(2)}$ is not a new ingredient. It is
   the first-order *state* correction, arriving one order late in the
   energy.

   The physical reading of the same fact: to first order the state has not
   yet moved anywhere that matters, so the energy shift is just the
   average of $H'$ over the state you already had. Only at second order
   does the system get credit for rearranging itself in response to the
   perturbation, and rearranging means consulting the states it can
   rearrange into.

   This pattern continues, and the general result is worth knowing about:
   the state correct to order $k$ determines the energy to order $2k+1$.
   Knowing $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ is enough for the energy through third order.

   </details>

2. For the quartic oscillator worked example, is $E_0^{(2)}$ (this
   course's engine gives a small negative number) consistent with the
   general expectation that a repulsive perturbation ($\lambda x^4>0$
   everywhere) still gives a negative second-order correction? (Hint:
   second-order corrections to the *ground* state are always
   $\leq0$. Why?)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes, and the negativity is guaranteed rather than observed. Look at the
   formula for $n=0$:

   $$
   E_0^{(2)} = \sum_{m\neq0}\frac{|H'_{m0}|^2}{E_0^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}
   $$

   Every numerator is a squared magnitude, so $\ge0$. Every denominator
   has $E_0^{(0)}$ as the smallest unperturbed energy in the spectrum, so
   every denominator is negative. Each term is therefore $\le0$ and so is
   the sum. The sign of $H'$ never entered: a repulsive perturbation, an
   attractive one, or a wildly oscillating one all give a non-positive
   second-order shift to the ground state.

   No contradiction with $\lambda x^4>0$ raising the energy, because the
   two orders are answering different questions. First order is
   $+3\lambda/4$, positive and linear in $\lambda$; second order is
   negative and quadratic. For small $\lambda$ the first dominates, the
   net shift is up, and the second-order term is a modest walk-back, as
   the energy-level diagram above shows.

   The reason behind the sign is worth having, because it is not really
   about perturbation theory. Take the unperturbed ground state
   $|0^{(0)}\rangle$ as a trial state for the full $H$. The variational
   principle, which the next lesson develops, says the true ground energy
   cannot exceed the expectation value of $H$ in any trial state:

   $$
   E_0^{\text{exact}} \le \langle 0^{(0)}|H|0^{(0)}\rangle = E_0^{(0)} + E_0^{(1)}
   $$

   So the sum of all corrections beyond first order has to be
   non-positive, and $E_0^{(2)}$ is the leading one. The negativity is the
   variational bound showing up inside a perturbation series.

   Physically this is level repulsion. Coupling pushes pairs of levels
   apart, and the ground state has no level below it to be pushed up by,
   so it can only go down. Excited states have neighbours on both sides
   and their second-order shifts can go either way, which is why the
   guarantee is specific to $n=0$.

   </details>

3. If $H'_{mn}=0$ for all $m\neq n$ at a particular $n$, what does this
   imply about $E_n^{(2)}$ and $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ at that $n$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Both vanish, term by term: every summand in $E_n^{(2)}$ carries a
   factor $|H'_{mn}|^2=0$, and every coefficient in $|n^{(1)}\rangle$
   carries a factor $H'_{mn}=0$. The state does not mix, and the
   second-order energy shift is zero.

   The stronger conclusion is available and is the real answer. Expand
   $H'|n^{(0)}\rangle$ in the $H_0$ basis:

   $$
   H'|n^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_m |m^{(0)}\rangle\,H'_{mn} = H'_{nn}\,|n^{(0)}\rangle
   $$

   since every term but $m=n$ is zero. So $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ is an exact
   eigenvector of $H'$, and therefore of $H=H_0+H'$:

   $$
   H|n^{(0)}\rangle = \left(E_n^{(0)} + H'_{nn}\right)|n^{(0)}\rangle
   $$

   This is not "the corrections happen to vanish at second order." It is
   an exact result at every order. The first-order answer is the whole
   answer, the perturbation series terminates, and $H'$ was never a
   perturbation for this state in the first place, however large
   $H'_{nn}$ may be. Note that "small" was not used anywhere in the
   argument.

   A concrete case from the previous course: a hydrogen atom in a uniform
   magnetic field along $z$ acquires $H'\propto L_z$, which is diagonal in
   the $|n,l,m\rangle$ basis. Every off-diagonal element vanishes, so the
   Zeeman shift proportional to $m$ is exact within that model rather than
   a first-order estimate. Compare an electric field, where
   $H'\propto z$ has the opposite structure: parity forces every diagonal
   element to zero, so the ground state gets no first-order shift at all
   and the leading Stark effect on it is second order. Which entries of
   $H'$ vanish decides everything about how the series behaves.

   </details>

4. Why does the first-order state correction conventionally set its own
   $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ coefficient to exactly 0, rather than leaving it
   undetermined?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the equations genuinely do not determine it, so something has
   to, and zero is the choice that costs nothing and simplifies
   everything.

   The undetermination is structural. The first-order equation can be
   rearranged to

   $$
   \left(H_0 - E_n^{(0)}\right)|n^{(1)}\rangle = \left(E_n^{(1)} - H'\right)|n^{(0)}\rangle
   $$

   and the operator on the left annihilates $|n^{(0)}\rangle$. So if
   $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ is a solution, then $|n^{(1)}\rangle+c|n^{(0)}\rangle$
   is an equally valid solution for any $c$. The component along
   $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ is invisible to the equation. Leaving it undetermined
   would make every downstream formula ambiguous with nothing gained.

   Zero is the right choice because the freedom is not physical. Adding
   $c|n^{(0)}\rangle$ produces
   $(1+\lambda c)|n^{(0)}\rangle+\lambda(\text{rest})$, so a real $c$
   rescales the state and an imaginary $c$ rotates its overall phase.
   Neither changes any probability or expectation value. What the choice
   does change is bookkeeping, and $c=0$ makes the bookkeeping clean: with
   $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, the perturbed state
   satisfies $\langle n|n\rangle=1+\lambda^2\langle n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle
   +\cdots$, with no first-order norm error to correct for, and every
   correction beyond the zeroth is orthogonal to the state it corrects.

   This is called intermediate normalization, $\langle n^{(0)}|n\rangle=1$,
   and the thing to be clear about is its status. It is a convention, not
   a result. Nothing physical follows from it, and nothing physical would
   break under a different choice. It is worth recognizing that class of
   step when it appears, because mistaking a convention for a derived fact
   is how people end up trying to prove things that are simply true by
   agreement.

   </details>

## Summary

- $H=H_0+H'$ with $H_0$ solvable: $E_n^{(1)}=H'_{nn}$,
  $E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq n}|H'_{mn}|^2/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})$,
  $|n^{(1)}\rangle=\sum_{m\neq n}\frac{H'_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)}}|m^{(0)}\rangle$.
- All three require $H'$ expressed in $H_0$'s eigenbasis, and
  non-degenerate $E_n^{(0)}$.
- The quartic anharmonic oscillator's first-order shift, $\frac34\lambda$,
  agrees with an independently derived matrix element, which makes it a
  check on the machinery rather than a restatement of it.
- The series carries no built-in warning when it stops working. Comparing
  consecutive orders, as the curve above does, is the available substitute.
