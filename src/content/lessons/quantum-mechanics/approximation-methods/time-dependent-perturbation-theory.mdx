## Switch on a coupling and wait

A system sits in state $|i\rangle$. At $t=0$ a perturbation $V$ switches
on, coupling it to a nearby state $|f\rangle$. What is the probability of
finding it in $|f\rangle$ at time $t$?

This is the question behind every spectral line, every laser, and every
gate pulse applied to a real qubit, and it cannot be answered by
correcting an energy: energies say what the levels are, not how a system
moves between them. What follows is the leading-order answer, together
with an honest measurement of how far it can be pushed before it starts
reporting probabilities greater than one.

## The first-order probability, and an exact check

### The first-order transition probability

For $H=H_0+V$ with $V$ constant for $t>0$, coupling only $|i\rangle$ and
$|f\rangle$ (matrix element $V_{fi}$), separated by
$\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar$, first-order time-dependent perturbation
theory gives:

$$
P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\omega_{fi}t}{2}\right)
$$

This is derived by integrating $i\hbar\dot c_f=V_{fi}e^{i\omega_{fi}t}$ to
first order in $V$, starting from $c_f(0)=0,c_i(0)=1$ (a standard,
if lengthy, integration this lesson states without re-deriving step
by step). Two limits worth noting: as $\omega_{fi}\to0$ (degenerate
levels), the formula's $\sin^2$ ratio limit gives
$P_{i\to f}(t)\to|V_{fi}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$ (quadratic growth); for
$\omega_{fi}\neq0$, $P_{i\to f}(t)$ oscillates, bounded by
$4|V_{fi}|^2/\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2$.

### An exact comparison

Because this is only a two-level system, the *exact* (non-perturbative)
answer is also directly computable: numerically integrate
$i\hbar\dot{\vec c}=H\vec c$ with
$H=\begin{pmatrix}E_i&V\\V&E_f\end{pmatrix}$ via fourth-order
Runge-Kutta, a general-purpose small-step ODE method that knows nothing
about perturbation theory. It makes no assumption that $V$ is small,
which is what makes it a usable check instead of a restatement: the two
calculations can disagree, and where they do is informative.

## Reading the formula factor by factor

Read the formula factor by factor. $|V_{fi}|^2$ sets the overall
strength: with no coupling there is no transition, whatever else is true.
The $\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)$ makes the probability oscillate rather than
climb toward a plateau, so a system can cross to $|f\rangle$ and then
cross back. That is required by <Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term> evolution, not an oddity: nothing
is lost, so nothing accumulates.

The $1/\omega_{fi}^2$ out front is the piece worth dwelling on. It says
the transition is easiest between levels that are close in energy, and it
is why the formula's ceiling $4|V_{fi}|^2/\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2$ blows up
as the levels approach degeneracy. A ceiling that exceeds $1$ is not a
prediction; it is the approximation reporting that it has left its
domain, and the worked example below walks straight into that.

## Weak coupling against strong, side by side

**Compare perturbative and exact transition probabilities for weak vs.
strong coupling ($E_i=0,E_f=1,\hbar=1$).**

| $V$ | $t$ | Perturbative | Exact | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 1 | 0.000092 | 0.000092 | 1.0000 |
| 0.01 | 3 | 0.000398 | 0.000398 | 1.0004 |
| 0.1 | 3 | 0.039800 | 0.038397 | 1.037 |
| 0.5 | 3 | 0.994996 | 0.363165 | 2.740 |

At $V=0.01$ the two agree to four decimal places. At $V=0.5$ and $t=3$
the perturbative formula predicts $0.995$, a near-certain transition,
while the exact calculation returns $0.363$.

The disagreement is not a matter of accuracy. The exact system is
<Term id="rabi-oscillation">Rabi-oscillating</Term>: its transition probability rises, falls back, and
repeats. The first-order formula captures the first rise and then keeps
extrapolating it, so the further past the first peak you ask, the more
confidently wrong the answer becomes. Longer times do not average the
error out; they compound it.

Watching both curves over time, at several coupling strengths, makes the
breakdown visible directly rather than only from the table's four rows.
The strongest frame is the one to think about first. With
$\hbar=\omega_{fi}=1$ the perturbative ceiling $4|V_{fi}|^2$ comes out to
exactly $1$ at $V=0.5$, which is also the hard cap unitarity puts on the
exact answer, so on that frame the two calculations are at least allowed
to agree.

Increase $V$ from $0.01$ toward $0.5$. At weak coupling the two curves
lie on top of one another. Past roughly $V\approx0.1$ the perturbative
curve climbs through the exact curve's first peak and keeps going, while
the exact curve turns over and comes back down, then repeats: the Rabi
oscillation. The last frame stops just short of the dashed line, at
$0.995$; hold $V=0.5$ and the perturbative curve rises to touch it exactly
at $t=\pi$, and nudge $V$ any higher and it reports a probability above
$1$. The arithmetic is
not wrong. The formula is telling you it has been asked something outside
its range, which is the most useful thing a leading-order approximation
can do.

Notice where they first separate. It is not at some fixed time but at
the exact curve's first turning point, which arrives sooner as $V$ grows.
That is the practical statement of the validity condition
$|V_{fi}|\ll\hbar|\omega_{fi}|$: first order is trustworthy for as long as
the system has not yet had time to come back.

The frames above sample a handful of coupling strengths at one fixed
level splitting, $\omega_{fi}=1$, and that splitting is a detuning: how
far the two levels sit from degeneracy. It is the term in
$4V^2/(\omega_{fi}^2+4V^2)$ that holds the exact curve below $1$, and the

## Where a factor of 2.7 appears

<Callout type="warning">
**Trusting the perturbative formula for strong coupling or long times.**
The worked example's $V=0.5$ case shows a factor-of-2.7 disagreement.
First-order perturbation theory is a leading-order approximation, valid
specifically when $|V_{fi}|\ll\hbar|\omega_{fi}|$ (weak coupling relative
to the energy splitting). Universal accuracy was never on offer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Interpreting $P_{i\to f}(t)$ growing past its perturbative bound as
physical.** The exact probability is always $\leq1$ by unitarity, no
matter how strong $V$ is; if a perturbative estimate exceeds what's
physically sensible, that's itself a signal the perturbative
approximation has broken down, and should be read that way.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. In the limit $\omega_{fi}\to0$ (degenerate $E_i,E_f$), what happens to
   $P_{i\to f}(t)$ as $t$ grows? Does it stay bounded, or grow without
   limit? Is this consistent with $P\leq1$ for large enough $t$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It grows without limit, as $t^2$, and it is flatly inconsistent with
   $P\le1$ past a certain time. That inconsistency is a fact about the
   approximation, not about the physics.

   Taking $\omega_{fi}\to0$ with $\sin^2(x)\to x^2$ for small argument:

   $$
   P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\omega_{fi}t}{2}\right) \longrightarrow \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{fi}^2t^2}{4} = \frac{|V_{fi}|^2t^2}{\hbar^2}
   $$

   which passes $1$ at $t=\hbar/|V_{fi}|$ and keeps climbing. A probability
   of $4$ is not a prediction anyone should carry away.

   What actually happens is available in closed form, because at zero
   detuning the exact two-level solution is the resonant Rabi formula:

   $$
   P^{\text{exact}}_{i\to f}(t) = \sin^2\!\left(\frac{|V_{fi}|t}{\hbar}\right)
   $$

   which oscillates between $0$ and $1$ forever. Expanding it for small
   argument gives $|V_{fi}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$, the perturbative answer exactly.
   So first order is not wrong; it is the leading term of the truth, and
   it stays good as long as the argument of that sine is small.

   The lesson to extract is about which parameter has to be small. It is
   not $|V_{fi}|$ on its own. It is the dimensionless combination
   $|V_{fi}|t/\hbar$, so *any* coupling, however weak, eventually breaks
   the formula if you wait long enough. Time-dependent perturbation theory
   is a short-time approximation, and the practical test is the one the
   formula fails here: if it reports $P$ approaching $1$, it has already
   stopped being trustworthy, because its own validity required $P\ll1$.

   </details>

2. Using the table above, at what rough combination of $V$ and $t$ does
   the perturbative and exact answer start to visibly diverge (say, by
   more than 5%)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Somewhere just past $V\approx0.1$ at these times. The table's four rows
   read $0.00\%$, $0.04\%$, $3.7\%$ and $174\%$ error, so the $5\%$ line
   falls between the $V=0.1$ and $V=0.5$ rows, much nearer the first.

   The rows do better than bracket the answer, though, because they reveal
   the parameter that controls it. Compare the two ceilings. First order
   caps out at $4V^2/\omega_{fi}^2$; the exact solution caps out at
   $4V^2/(\omega_{fi}^2+4V^2)$. Their ratio is

   $$
   \frac{4V^2/\omega_{fi}^2}{4V^2/(\omega_{fi}^2+4V^2)} = 1 + \frac{4V^2}{\omega_{fi}^2}
   $$

   With $\omega_{fi}=1$ that predicts errors of $0.04\%$ at $V=0.01$ and
   $4\%$ at $V=0.1$, which is what the table's second and third rows
   report. Setting $4V^2/\omega_{fi}^2=0.05$ puts the $5\%$ threshold at
   $V\approx0.11$.

   The $V=0.5$ row does not follow the rule, and the discrepancy is
   informative rather than a failure of the estimate. The ratio formula
   compares peak heights; at $V=0.5$ and $t=3$ the exact curve has already
   passed its peak and fallen back to $0.363$ while the perturbative curve
   is still climbing toward $0.995$. So a second error has appeared on top
   of the amplitude error: the perturbative curve has the wrong *period*
   as well as the wrong height, and once the two are out of step the
   disagreement depends on when you look.

   That is the honest way to state the validity condition. Not "$V$ small"
   but $|V_{fi}|\ll\hbar|\omega_{fi}|$ *and* a time short enough that the
   exact system has not yet turned around. Once it has, the error stops
   being a percentage and becomes a matter of comparing two curves that no
   longer have anything to do with each other.

   </details>

3. Explain physically why $P_{i\to f}(t)$ oscillating in time (rather
   than monotonically increasing) is required by unitarity, beyond being a
   mathematical curiosity of the formula.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because there is nowhere for the probability to go. With only two
   states, $P_i(t)+P_f(t)=1$ at all times, so every bit of population that
   reaches $|f\rangle$ came out of $|i\rangle$ and is still available to
   come back. Nothing about the coupling distinguishes the two directions.

   The stronger version of the argument does not even need the count to be
   two. For a closed system with a time-independent $H$ and finitely many
   levels, every amplitude is a finite sum $\sum_k a_k e^{-iE_kt/\hbar}$
   of pure phases. A finite sum of phases cannot settle down: it is
   quasi-periodic and returns arbitrarily close to its starting value,
   again and again. Monotone approach to a limit is simply not among the
   behaviours available. Here, with exactly two eigenvalues, the return is
   not merely approximate but exactly periodic, which is the Rabi
   oscillation the numerical curve shows.

   The point that makes this worth understanding rather than reciting is
   that real atoms *do* decay monotonically, and that is not a violation.
   An excited atom in free space empties into a continuum of photon modes,
   with infinitely many final states rather than one. Each individual mode
   would still like to give the population back, but they return it at
   incommensurate frequencies and the sum never reassembles, so the
   recurrence time goes to infinity and exponential decay is the correct
   description. This is where Fermi's golden rule comes from: a constant
   transition *rate*, linear rather than oscillating growth, obtained by
   summing this same $\sin^2$ formula over a dense band of final states.

   So the correct statement is that irreversibility comes from the size of
   the final-state space, never from the dynamics being non-unitary. Two
   levels oscillate. A continuum decays. Both evolve unitarily.

   </details>

4. Why does this lesson use direct Runge-Kutta integration as the
   "exact" check, rather than another perturbative formula at a higher
   order?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a check has to be able to fail independently, and a
   higher-order term in the same series cannot. It shares the assumption
   under test.

   The whole question is whether treating $V$ as small is legitimate at
   $V=0.5$. Second order is still an expansion in $V$, still built from
   the same starting point, and still inherits whatever goes wrong when
   the expansion parameter stops being small. It would give a different
   number, and no way to tell whether the difference was progress. Worse,
   the perturbation series is generally asymptotic rather than convergent,
   so past a certain order the terms start growing again and "higher
   order" is not reliably "more accurate."

   Runge-Kutta assumes nothing about $V$. It integrates
   $i\hbar\dot{\vec c}=H\vec c$ as written, and its error comes from a
   completely different source: the finite step size, which can be shrunk
   until the answer stops moving, with no reference to how large $V$ is.
   Two methods whose errors have unrelated causes can disagree
   informatively. Two methods sharing an error source can only agree
   uninformatively.

   The choice is affordable here for a specific reason worth noticing.
   The system is two-dimensional, so "just solve it" costs almost nothing.
   That is exactly the situation where a validity check should be built,
   and it is why this lesson's example is a system nobody needs
   perturbation theory for: calibrating a tool against a known answer
   requires a known answer. The finding then transfers to the systems
   where the exact route is unavailable and the first-order formula is all
   there is.

   As a further check, this particular problem also has a closed-form
   solution, the Rabi formula with its $4V^2/(\omega_{fi}^2+4V^2)$ ceiling,
   so the numerical integration is itself verifiable rather than being
   trusted on reputation.

   </details>

## Summary

- First-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives
  $P_{i\to f}(t)=\dfrac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)$
  for a constant perturbation switched on at $t=0$.
- Checked directly against exact 2-level Runge-Kutta integration: nearly
  exact for weak coupling, badly wrong (off by a factor of $\sim2.7$ in
  this course's worked example) for strong coupling and long times.
- A perturbative ceiling above $P=1$ is the formula flagging its own
  failure, which makes it a usable diagnostic instead of a hidden trap.
- First order is reliable until the exact solution reaches its first
  turning point, and that arrives sooner as the coupling grows.
