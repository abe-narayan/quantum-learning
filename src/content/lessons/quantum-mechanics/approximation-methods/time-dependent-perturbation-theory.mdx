## Motivation

Every previous lesson in this course asked "what is the corrected
*energy*?" This lesson asks a different question: given a system sitting
in state $|i\rangle$, with a perturbation $V$ switched on at $t=0$
coupling it to a nearby state $|f\rangle$, what's the probability of
finding it in $|f\rangle$ at time $t$?

## Mathematical Development

### The first-order transition probability

For $H=H_0+V$ with $V$ constant for $t>0$, coupling only $|i\rangle$ and
$|f\rangle$ (matrix element $V_{fi}$), separated by
$\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar$, first-order time-dependent perturbation
theory gives:

$$
P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\omega_{fi}t}{2}\right)
$$

This is derived by integrating $i\hbar\dot c_f=V_{fi}e^{i\omega_{fi}t}$ to
first order in $V$, starting from $c_f(0)=0,c_i(0)=1$ (a standard,
if lengthy, integration this lesson states rather than re-derives step
by step). Two limits worth noting: as $\omega_{fi}\to0$ (degenerate
levels), the formula's $\sin^2$ ratio limit gives
$P_{i\to f}(t)\to|V_{fi}|^2t^2/\hbar^2$ (quadratic growth); for
$\omega_{fi}\neq0$, $P_{i\to f}(t)$ oscillates, bounded by
$4|V_{fi}|^2/\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2$.

### An exact comparison

Because this is only a two-level system, the *exact* (non-perturbative)
answer is also directly computable: numerically integrate
$i\hbar\dot{\vec c}=H\vec c$ with
$H=\begin{pmatrix}E_i&V\\V&E_f\end{pmatrix}$ via 4th-order Runge-Kutta
(a genuine, general-purpose small-step ODE integration, not a
perturbative shortcut). This gives a completely independent check of
where the first-order formula is trustworthy.

## Physical Interpretation

$|V_{fi}|^2$ sets the overall transition strength: no coupling, no
transition, regardless of anything else. The $\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)$
factor makes the probability genuinely oscillate in time (it is *not* a
monotonically growing quantity in general). A system can transition to
$|f\rangle$ and then transition back, entirely consistent with unitary
(probability-conserving) time evolution.

## Worked Example

**Compare perturbative and exact transition probabilities for weak vs.
strong coupling ($E_i=0,E_f=1,\hbar=1$).**

| $V$ | $t$ | Perturbative | Exact | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 1 | 0.000092 | 0.000092 | 1.0000 |
| 0.01 | 3 | 0.000398 | 0.000398 | 1.0004 |
| 0.1 | 3 | 0.039800 | 0.038397 | 1.037 |
| 0.5 | 3 | 0.994996 | 0.363165 | 2.740 |

For weak coupling ($V=0.01$), perturbative and exact agree to 4 decimal
places. For strong coupling ($V=0.5$) at $t=3$, the perturbative formula
predicts a probability near 1 (nearly certain transition) while the
exact calculation gives $0.363$. The true system is genuinely
Rabi-oscillating (its exact transition probability rises and falls
periodically), a behavior the first-order formula, which only captures
the *initial* rise, cannot see at all.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Trusting the perturbative formula for strong coupling or long times.**
The worked example's $V=0.5$ case shows a factor-of-2.7 disagreement.
First-order perturbation theory is a leading-order approximation, valid
specifically when $|V_{fi}|\ll\hbar|\omega_{fi}|$ (weak coupling relative
to the energy splitting), not a universally accurate formula.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Interpreting $P_{i\to f}(t)$ growing past its perturbative bound as
physical.** The exact probability is always $\leq1$ by unitarity, no
matter how strong $V$ is; if a perturbative estimate exceeds what's
physically sensible, that's itself a signal the perturbative
approximation has broken down, not a real prediction.
</Callout>

## Summary

- First-order time-dependent perturbation theory gives
  $P_{i\to f}(t)=\dfrac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t/2)$
  for a constant perturbation switched on at $t=0$.
- Checked directly against exact 2-level Runge-Kutta integration: nearly
  exact for weak coupling, badly wrong (off by a factor of $\sim2.7$ in
  this course's worked example) for strong coupling and long times.
- The breakdown is a genuine numerical fact about this specific
  comparison, not a general claim: it demonstrates exactly what "first
  order" and "small perturbation" mean in practice.
