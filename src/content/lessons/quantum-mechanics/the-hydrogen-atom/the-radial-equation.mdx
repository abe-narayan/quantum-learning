## Motivation

Last lesson proved the angular part is already solved. This lesson does
the actual reduction: substitute that fact into the full 3D Schrödinger
equation and watch a genuinely 3D partial differential equation collapse
into a single ordinary differential equation in $r$ alone, one this
platform can then solve using the exact same machinery One-Dimensional
Systems already built.

## Mathematical Development

### The separated ansatz

Write $\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$. The
time-independent Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates,
$-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V(r)\psi=E\psi$, uses the spherical
Laplacian, which separates cleanly into a radial piece and exactly
$\vec L^{\,2}/\hbar^2r^2$ for the angular piece (a standard identity of
spherical coordinates). Since $Y_l^m$ is already an $\vec L^{\,2}$
eigenfunction with eigenvalue $l(l+1)\hbar^2$ (Angular Momentum & Spin),
substituting it in makes the *entire* angular dependence collapse to a
single number, leaving:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \left[V(r)+\frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]R = ER
$$

This is one ordinary differential equation in $r$ alone, for each fixed $l$.

### The centrifugal term, interpreted

The extra piece, $\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}$, is the **centrifugal
term**: it adds to $V(r)$ exactly like a repulsive potential barrier
near $r=0$, growing without bound as $r\to0$ for any $l>0$. Physically
this is the same effect as classical angular momentum: an orbiting
particle with nonzero $L$ can never reach $r=0$, since that would require
infinite angular velocity. This is precisely the "effective potential"
concept One-Dimensional Systems used for scattering; here it isn't
introduced as a separate trick, it falls directly out of the angular
momentum substitution.

### Standard form via u(r) = rR(r)

Substituting $u(r)=rR(r)$ and simplifying the derivative term (a
standard, mechanical substitution that removes the awkward
$\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}r^2\frac{d}{dr}$ structure) gives:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left[V(r)+\frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]u = Eu
$$

This is **exactly** the 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation from Wave
Mechanics, with an effective potential
$V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}$ and the boundary
condition $u(0)=0$ (so that $R(r)=u(r)/r$ stays finite at the origin).
Every tool this platform built for 1D problems (the general solving
strategy, boundary-condition matching) applies directly; only the
specific $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ for the Coulomb potential is new.

## Physical Interpretation

Turning a 3D problem into a 1D one isn't a mathematical convenience
specific to hydrogen: it's the direct payoff of Lesson 1's
$[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=[H,L_z]=0$ result, applied mechanically. Every central
potential reduces exactly this way; hydrogen's Coulomb $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$
is simply the specific effective potential the next lesson solves.

This same radial equation is why hydrogen's emission lines are so
precisely predictable that astronomers use them as a cosmic ruler: the
Lyman-alpha line, from a transition this effective potential's bound
states set the exact energy of, is routinely used to identify hydrogen
gas in galaxies billions of light-years away, with its wavelength shift
directly giving that galaxy's redshift. The same exactly-solvable
structure also underlies the hydrogen maser, an atomic frequency
reference stable enough to serve as a timekeeping standard in radio
astronomy, both real applications resting on the same $u(0)=0$
boundary-value problem solved here.

## Worked Example

**Write out $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ explicitly for hydrogen's $l=1$ states.**

$$
V_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} + \frac{2\hbar^2}{2mr^2} = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} + \frac{\hbar^2}{mr^2}
$$

using $l(l+1)=1(2)=2$. Near $r=0$, the $1/r^2$ centrifugal term
dominates (grows faster than the $1/r$ Coulomb attraction), creating an
effective repulsive barrier, consistent with $l=1$ states (unlike
$l=0$) having zero probability density exactly at the nucleus, previewed
here and confirmed directly in the next lesson's explicit radial
wavefunctions.

directly visible (atomic units, $V(r)=-1/r$):

$l=0$: no centrifugal term at all, so $V_\text{eff}=V(r)$ falls straight

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the centrifugal term for l>0 states.** Only $l=0$ ($s$
states) have $V_{\text{eff}}=V(r)$ exactly; every $l>0$ state solves a
*different* effective 1D problem, with its own centrifugal barrier.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing $R(r)$ and $u(r)=rR(r)$.** The boundary condition $u(0)=0$
does *not* mean $R(0)=0$: for $l=0$, $R(0)$ can be (and is) nonzero,
since $R(r)=u(r)/r$ can stay finite as $r\to0$ if $u(r)\to0$
proportionally to $r$, which is exactly the $l=0$ radial wavefunction's
behavior.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Substituting $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m$ into the 3D Schrödinger equation and
  using $Y_l^m$'s known $\vec L^{\,2}$ eigenvalue collapses the angular
  dependence entirely, leaving a radial ODE.
- The centrifugal term $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ emerges directly from this
  substitution (not introduced separately) and acts as a repulsive
  effective potential for $l>0$.
- $u(r)=rR(r)$ puts the radial equation into exactly the 1D Schrödinger
  equation's form, with $V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ and
  boundary condition $u(0)=0$.
