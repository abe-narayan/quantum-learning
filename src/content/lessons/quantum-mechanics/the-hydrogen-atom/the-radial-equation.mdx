## The angular part was solved before we started

Two variables disappear in this lesson, and the reason they can is worth
holding onto: the angular dependence of $\psi$ is not solved here so much
as *already known*, fixed by the commutators of the last lesson before any
calculation about hydrogen begins. Substituting a known $Y_l^m$ into the
3D equation turns the entire angular part into a single number,
$l(l+1)\hbar^2$, and what is left is an ordinary differential equation in
$r$ alone.

That leftover equation is worth more than its brevity suggests. A change
of variable puts it into the exact shape of the 1D Schrödinger equation
from Wave Mechanics, which means the boundary-condition machinery, the
bound-state reasoning, and the effective-potential picture already built
for one-dimensional problems all transfer to hydrogen without
modification. Three dimensions, solved with one-dimensional tools.

## Substituting the ansatz and isolating r

### The separated ansatz

Write $\psi(r,\theta,\varphi)=R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$. The
time-independent <Term id="schrodinger-equation">Schrödinger equation</Term> in spherical coordinates,
$-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi+V(r)\psi=E\psi$, uses the spherical
Laplacian, which separates cleanly into a radial piece and exactly
$\vec L^{\,2}/\hbar^2r^2$ for the angular piece (a standard identity of
spherical coordinates). Since $Y_l^m$ is already an $\vec L^{\,2}$
eigenfunction with <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalue</Term> $l(l+1)\hbar^2$ (Angular Momentum & Spin),
substituting it in makes the *entire* angular dependence collapse to a
single number, leaving:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \left[V(r)+\frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]R = ER
$$

This is one ordinary differential equation in $r$ alone, for each fixed $l$.

### The centrifugal term, interpreted

The extra piece, $\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}$, is the **centrifugal
term**: it adds to $V(r)$ the way a repulsive potential barrier near
$r=0$ would, growing without bound as $r\to0$ for any $l>0$. Physically
this is the same effect as classical angular momentum: an orbiting
particle with nonzero $L$ can never reach $r=0$, since that would require
infinite angular velocity. It is the "effective potential" concept
One-Dimensional Systems used for scattering, arriving here not as a trick
introduced for convenience but as a term that falls out of the angular
momentum substitution on its own.

### Standard form via u(r) = rR(r)

Substituting $u(r)=rR(r)$ and simplifying the derivative term (a
standard, mechanical substitution that removes the awkward
$\frac1{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}r^2\frac{d}{dr}$ structure) gives:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left[V(r)+\frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]u = Eu
$$

Compare that to the 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation from Wave
Mechanics and the two are the same equation, symbol for symbol, with an
effective potential
$V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}$ and the boundary
condition $u(0)=0$. Every tool this platform built for 1D problems (the
general solving strategy, boundary-condition matching) applies directly;
only the specific $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ for the Coulomb potential is new.

That boundary condition is a statement about $u$, and it is worth being
careful about what it does and does not say about $R$.

## The payoff of last lesson's commutators

Turning a 3D problem into a 1D one is not a convenience specific to
hydrogen. It is the payoff of Lesson 1's $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=[H,L_z]=0$
result, applied mechanically. Every central potential reduces this way,
and hydrogen's Coulomb $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$ is the one effective
potential the next lesson solves.

Because this equation is exactly solvable, hydrogen's emission lines can
be predicted to many decimal places, which is what makes them usable as a
cosmic ruler. The Lyman-alpha line, whose energy the bound states of this
effective potential fix, identifies hydrogen gas in galaxies billions of
light-years away, and how far the observed wavelength has slid from the
predicted one gives that galaxy's redshift directly. The same solvability
underlies the hydrogen maser pictured above. Both applications rest on the
$u(0)=0$ boundary-value problem set up here.

## The effective potential at l = 1

**Write out $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ explicitly for hydrogen's $l=1$ states.**

$$
V_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} + \frac{2\hbar^2}{2mr^2} = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r} + \frac{\hbar^2}{mr^2}
$$

using $l(l+1)=1(2)=2$. Near $r=0$, the $1/r^2$ centrifugal term
dominates (grows faster than the $1/r$ Coulomb attraction), creating an
effective repulsive barrier, consistent with $l=1$ states (unlike $l=0$)
having zero probability density at the nucleus itself. The next lesson's
explicit radial wavefunctions confirm it.

Plotting $V_\text{eff}(r)$ for each $l$ makes the growing barrier
directly visible (atomic units, $V(r)=-1/r$):

It never turns back up and has no minimum: this is the bare -1/r Coulomb well, falling without limit as the origin is approached." />

$l=0$: no centrifugal term at all, so $V_\text{eff}=V(r)$ falls straight
into the origin, unbounded below. This is the bare Coulomb attraction and
nothing else.

$l=1$ and $l=2$: the curve turns sharply *upward* near $r=0$ instead of
falling to $-\infty$, and the turn gets steeper as $l$ grows. That upturn
is the repulsive barrier keeping higher-$l$ electrons away from the
nucleus.

## The centrifugal term does not vanish for l > 0

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the centrifugal term for l>0 states.** Only $l=0$ ($s$
states) have $V_{\text{eff}}=V(r)$ exactly; every $l>0$ state solves a
*different* effective 1D problem, with its own centrifugal barrier.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing $R(r)$ and $u(r)=rR(r)$.** The boundary condition $u(0)=0$
does *not* mean $R(0)=0$: for $l=0$, $R(0)$ can be (and is) nonzero,
since $R(r)=u(r)/r$ can stay finite as $r\to0$ if $u(r)\to0$
proportionally to $r$, which is how the $l=0$ radial wavefunction
behaves.
</Callout>

The actual $1s$ ($l=0$) and $2p$ ($l=1$) radial wavefunctions make this
concrete right at the origin (atomic units, $r$ from 0 to 0.5). $l=0$
and $l=1$ get separate plots below, since $R(r)$ near the origin is
over an order of magnitude larger for $l=0$ than for $l=1$; on one shared
axis the $l=1$ curves would flatten into an invisible line.

<ParametricCurve
  frames={uAndRNearOriginFramesL0}

For $l=0$, $u(r)\to0$ *linearly* as $r\to0$, so $R(r)=u(r)/r$ approaches
the finite, nonzero value $R(0)=2$. The $u(0)=0$ boundary condition and a
nonzero probability density at the nucleus coexist without conflict.

  frames={uAndRNearOriginFramesL1}

/>

For $l=1$, $u(r)\to0$ even faster (quadratically, since the centrifugal
barrier forces $R(r)\propto r^l=r$ itself), so $R(r)$ vanishes at the
origin too. Same boundary condition on $u$, opposite conclusion about
$R$: nonzero ($R(0)=2$) for $l=0$, zero for $l=1$.

## Practice Questions

1. Write $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$ explicitly for hydrogen's $l=0$ states, and
   confirm it equals the bare Coulomb potential (no centrifugal term).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At $l=0$ the centrifugal coefficient is $l(l+1)=0\cdot1=0$, so the
   whole term disappears:

   $$
   V_{\text{eff}}(r) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} + \frac{0\cdot\hbar^2}{2mr^2} = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}
   $$

   $s$ states are the only ones that solve the bare Coulomb problem. Every
   $l>0$ state solves a different one-dimensional problem with its own
   barrier, which is the first plot in the sequence above: monotonically
   rising toward zero, with no upturn near the origin and no minimum.

   The worry that ought to surface here is worth answering. With no
   barrier, $V_{\text{eff}}\to-\infty$ as $r\to0$, so what stops the
   electron collapsing onto the proton and releasing unbounded energy?
   Nothing in the potential does. The kinetic term does. Confining the
   electron to a region of size $r$ costs kinetic energy growing like
   $\hbar^2/2mr^2$, by the uncertainty principle, and that beats the
   potential's $-1/r$ gain in the limit. The competition between a
   $-1/r$ attraction and a $+1/r^2$ confinement cost settles at a finite
   radius, and that radius is the Bohr radius. The centrifugal term the
   $l=0$ state lacks has exactly the $1/r^2$ form of the cost that saves
   it anyway.

   </details>

2. Explain why the boundary condition is stated as $u(0)=0$ rather than
   $R(0)=0$, using the relationship $u(r)=rR(r)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $u(0)=0$ is the true condition and $R(0)=0$ is a stronger claim
   that is false for exactly the states chemistry cares about most.

   The physical requirement is that $R(r)$ stay *finite* at the origin: a
   radial function blowing up like $1/r$ is not an acceptable solution of
   the Schrödinger equation there. Feed that requirement through
   $u=rR$ and you get $u(0)=\lim_{r\to0}rR(r)=0$, since $r\to0$ times
   something finite is zero. The implication runs one way only. $u(0)=0$
   can be satisfied by the factor of $r$ alone, with $R$ perfectly happy
   and nonzero at the origin.

   The $1s$ state is the case in point, and it is the case that matters.
   Its $u(r)$ leaves the origin linearly, so $R(0)=u'(0)$ is finite and
   nonzero, $R(0)=2$ in atomic units. Impose $R(0)=0$ instead and you
   delete the ground state of hydrogen. You also delete every consequence
   of $s$ electrons having density at the nucleus: hyperfine structure,
   the 21 cm line radio astronomers map galaxies with, the Fermi contact
   term in NMR chemical shifts. The distinction between the two boundary
   conditions is the difference between a correct atom and no atom.

   There is a second reason the condition is written on $u$, and it is the
   reason the whole substitution was made. In the $u$ equation the problem
   reads as a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation on the half-line
   $r\ge0$, and $u(0)=0$ is the same boundary condition an infinite wall
   imposes in the infinite square well from Wave Mechanics. Written that
   way, every technique built there transfers without translation. Written
   as a condition on $R$, it would not even be true.

   </details>

3. For a fixed $r$, which grows faster as $l$ increases: the centrifugal
   term or the Coulomb term? What does this suggest about how a higher-$l$
   electron's wavefunction behaves near the nucleus, compared to a
   lower-$l$ one at the same energy?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The centrifugal term, and not by a narrow margin: the Coulomb term does
   not grow at all. There is no $l$ anywhere in $-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$, so
   at fixed $r$ it is a constant while $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ climbs
   quadratically in $l$. The barrier wins at every $l>0$ once $r$ is small
   enough, since $1/r^2$ outruns $1/r$ as $r\to0$ regardless of
   coefficients.

   The consequence for the wavefunction can be read straight off the
   equation. Near the origin the centrifugal term dominates everything
   else, so the radial equation reduces to

   $$
   -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \frac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}u = 0
   $$

   whose solutions are $u\propto r^{l+1}$ and $u\propto r^{-l}$. The
   second is discarded as singular, leaving $u\propto r^{l+1}$ and
   therefore

   $$
   R(r) \propto r^{\,l} \quad\text{as } r\to0
   $$

   So each extra unit of $l$ adds another power of $r$ suppressing the
   wavefunction near the nucleus. At $l=0$, $R$ is flat and finite there;
   at $l=1$ it vanishes linearly; at $l=2$ quadratically. The three
   states $3s$, $3p$, $3d$ all sit at the same hydrogen energy $E_3$ and
   are nevertheless completely different in how close they get to the
   proton.

   That difference is not a curiosity, and it is where this question pays
   off. In any atom past hydrogen, the inner electrons screen the nuclear
   charge, so an outer electron that *penetrates* the core sees more of
   the bare nucleus and is bound more tightly. Low $l$ penetrates, high
   $l$ does not, so the $l$-degeneracy of hydrogen breaks with $s$ below
   $p$ below $d$. That ordering is why $4s$ fills before $3d$ and why the
   periodic table has the shape it has. It follows from the $r^{\,l}$
   behaviour derived in three lines above.

   </details>

4. This lesson's reduction assumed a general central $V(r)$, then
   specialized to Coulomb only in the worked example. What is the ONE
   piece of the derivation that's specific to hydrogen, versus what
   applies to any central potential?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   One substitution: writing $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$. Everything else
   holds for every central potential there is.

   Worth listing what "everything else" covers, because several of these
   feel hydrogen-flavoured and are not:

   The separation $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m$ itself, which came from
   $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=[H,L_z]=0$ and used nothing about the radial shape.

   The angular factors $Y_l^m$, fixed by the angular momentum algebra
   before hydrogen was mentioned.

   The centrifugal term $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$. Its coefficient is the
   $\vec L^{\,2}$ eigenvalue, so it is the same term in a spherical well,
   a three-dimensional oscillator, and a quantum dot.

   The substitution $u=rR$, the resulting one-dimensional form, and the
   boundary condition $u(0)=0$.

   The $R\propto r^{\,l}$ behaviour near the origin, since that came from
   the centrifugal term dominating, which happens for any $V$ less
   singular than $1/r^2$.

   So the entire machinery of this lesson is on loan, and hydrogen
   supplies one function. What hydrogen then buys with that function
   belongs to the next lesson: the Coulomb potential is one of only two
   central potentials whose bound-state energies come out independent of
   $l$, and that extra degeneracy is genuinely special rather than
   general.

   One refinement, in the interest of precision rather than correction:
   the mass in these equations is properly the electron-proton reduced
   mass $\mu=m_em_p/(m_e+m_p)$, which differs from $m_e$ by about one part
   in two thousand and is measurable in the spectrum, most visibly in the
   isotope shift between hydrogen and deuterium. That replacement is also
   general, applying to any two-body central-force problem, so it does not
   change the answer above.

   </details>

## Summary

- Substituting $\psi=R(r)Y_l^m$ into the 3D Schrödinger equation and
  using $Y_l^m$'s known $\vec L^{\,2}$ eigenvalue collapses the angular
  dependence entirely, leaving a radial ODE.
- The centrifugal term $l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ emerges directly from this
  substitution (not introduced separately) and acts as a repulsive
  effective potential for $l>0$.
- $u(r)=rR(r)$ puts the radial equation into exactly the 1D Schrödinger
  equation's form, with $V_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+l(l+1)\hbar^2/2mr^2$ and
  boundary condition $u(0)=0$.
