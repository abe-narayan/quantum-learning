## Motivation

Heat hydrogen gas and it doesn't glow across a smooth rainbow. It emits
at a handful of exact, discrete wavelengths, cataloged by Balmer in 1885
as pure empirical numerology, a formula that fit the data perfectly with
no physical justification behind it for decades. This lesson derives the
number underneath those lines from nothing but the Schrödinger equation
and a boundary condition. Solving the radial equation from the previous
lesson for hydrogen's specific Coulomb potential
$V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$ (subject to the bound-state requirement that
$u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$) turns out to force $E$ to only a discrete,
countable set of values, exactly the discreteness Balmer saw and couldn't
explain. This lesson states that result and unpacks what it means
physically; the next lesson uses the actual radial wavefunctions (already
built and verified in this course's engine) to see the states themselves.

## Intuition

The infinite square well forced only certain wavelengths to fit between
two walls; here, the walls are gone, but the requirement is just as
rigid. A generic solution to the radial equation blows up as $r\to\infty$,
which isn't normalizable and can't describe a bound electron. Only for
special values of $E$ does the blow-up cancel and the wavefunction
actually decay to zero at infinity instead. That cancellation is a
knife-edge condition, satisfied only at a discrete ladder of energies, not
a continuum, for exactly the same reason a guitar string can only vibrate
at certain frequencies: a boundary condition that must be satisfied
exactly, not approximately, leaves only a countable set of solutions.

## Mathematical Development

### The quantization condition

Solving $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2u}{dr^2}+\left[-\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}+\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]u=Eu$
with $u(0)=0$ and $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ is a standard (if lengthy)
bound-state ODE calculation: the same *category* of problem as the
finite square well in Wave Mechanics, just with a different
$V_{\text{eff}}(r)$. The result is that normalizable solutions exist only
for a specific discrete set of energies:

$$
E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0^2\hbar^2}\cdot\frac1{n^2} = -\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots
$$

where $n$ (the **principal quantum number**) is a positive integer that
emerges from the same kind of boundary-condition counting argument the
harmonic oscillator and finite well used, except here the messy
combination of physical constants collapses to the clean number
$13.6\text{ eV}$, called **one Rydberg** ($\text{Ry}$).

### Why the energy doesn't depend on l or m

A striking feature specific to the pure $1/r$ Coulomb potential: $E_n$
depends *only* on $n$, not on $l$ (despite $l$ appearing explicitly in
the effective potential solved above) or $m$. This is a special
"accidental" degeneracy of the exact Coulomb form: for $n\geq2$,
states with different $l$ (and hence different-shaped radial
wavefunctions) share exactly the same energy. Combined with the $2l+1$
degeneracy in $m$ from Angular Momentum & Spin, the total degeneracy of
level $n$ is $n^2$ (summing $2l+1$ over $l=0,\ldots,n-1$, a direct
algebra exercise, not a new physical input).

## Physical Interpretation

$-13.6\text{ eV}$ is the ionization energy of hydrogen: exactly the
energy needed to free a ground-state electron completely (to $E=0$, the
threshold of an unbound state). As $n\to\infty$, $E_n\to0^-$: the
levels crowd together approaching the ionization threshold, exactly the
"Rydberg series" pattern seen in hydrogen's real emission spectrum.
`hydrogenEnergyLevel(n)` in this platform's engine computes exactly this
formula and was verified against $E_1=-13.6$, $E_2=-3.4$, $E_4=-0.85$ eV.

## Worked Example

**What photon energy is released when an electron falls from $n=3$ to
$n=2$? (This is the first line of the hydrogen Balmer series.)**

$$
E_3 = -\frac{13.6}{9} \approx -1.51\text{ eV}, \qquad E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{4} = -3.40\text{ eV}
$$

$$
\Delta E = E_3 - E_2 \approx -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\text{ eV}
$$

confirmed directly: `hydrogenEnergyLevel(3) - hydrogenEnergyLevel(2)`
$\approx1.89\text{ eV}$, matching the well-known Balmer-alpha transition.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the sign.** $E_n$ is negative for every bound state: the
electron is bound (has less energy than a free electron at rest at
infinity). A "higher" energy level (larger $n$) means a *less negative*
number, not a larger positive one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming $E$ depends on $l$.** This would be true for almost any other
central potential (a finite spherical well, for instance); the
$l$-independence is a special feature of the *exact* $1/r$ Coulomb form,
not a general rule this course's earlier central-potential argument
implies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Solving the radial equation for the Coulomb potential, with bound-state
  boundary conditions, quantizes energy: $E_n=-13.6\text{ eV}/n^2$.
- This $13.6\text{ eV}$ (one Rydberg) is hydrogen's ground-state
  ionization energy.
- $E_n$ depends only on $n$ (not $l$ or $m$), a special feature of the
  exact $1/r$ potential, giving each level an $n^2$-fold degeneracy.
