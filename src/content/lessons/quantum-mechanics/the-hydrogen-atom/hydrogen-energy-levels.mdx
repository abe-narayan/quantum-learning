<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
Heat hydrogen gas and it doesn't glow across a smooth rainbow. It emits at a handful of exact, discrete wavelengths, cataloged by Balmer in 1885 as pure empirical numerology, a formula that fit the data perfectly with no physical justification behind it for decades.
</LessonHook>

Balmer's numerology waited nearly three decades for an explanation. This
lesson supplies it, deriving the number underneath those lines from
nothing but the <Term id="schrodinger-equation">Schrödinger equation</Term> and a boundary condition: solving
the radial equation from the previous lesson for hydrogen's specific
Coulomb potential $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$ (subject to the bound-state
requirement that $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$) forces $E$ to only a
discrete, countable set of values, the discreteness Balmer saw
and couldn't explain. This lesson states that result and unpacks what it
means physically; the next lesson uses the actual radial wavefunctions
(already built and verified in this course's engine) to see the states
themselves.

## Intuition

The infinite square well forced only certain wavelengths to fit between
two walls; here, the walls are gone, but the requirement is just as
rigid. A generic solution to the radial equation blows up as $r\to\infty$,
which isn't <Term id="normalization">normalizable</Term> and can't describe a bound electron. Only for
special values of $E$ does the blow-up cancel and the wavefunction decay
to zero at infinity instead. That cancellation is a
knife-edge condition, satisfied only at a discrete ladder of energies, not
a continuum, for the same reason a guitar string can only vibrate
at certain frequencies: a boundary condition that must be satisfied
exactly, not approximately, leaves only a countable set of solutions.

## Mathematical Development

### The quantization condition

Solving $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{d^2u}{dr^2}+\left[-\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}+\dfrac{l(l+1)\hbar^2}{2mr^2}\right]u=Eu$
with $u(0)=0$ and $u(r)\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ is the same *category* of
problem as the finite square well in Wave Mechanics, just with a
different $V_{\text{eff}}(r)$. The full calculation is lengthy, but the
logic that forces quantization fits in a sketch. Here is that logic,
with the boundary conditions doing all the work:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Bound states have E < 0. Package the energy as a decay constant κ and switch to the dimensionless radius ρ.">
    $$
    \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar}, \qquad \rho = \kappa r
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Far from the proton both potential terms die off and the equation collapses. The growing exponential solution is discarded: it cannot be normalized.">
    $$
    \frac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} \approx u \quad\Rightarrow\quad u \sim e^{-\rho} \ \text{ as } \rho\to\infty
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Near the origin the centrifugal term dominates instead. The other solution behaves like ρ to the power −l, blows up at r = 0, and is discarded too.">
    $$
    \frac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} \approx \frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}\,u \quad\Rightarrow\quad u \sim \rho^{\,l+1} \ \text{ as } \rho\to0
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Factor out both required behaviors. Whatever is left over is carried by v(ρ).">
    $$
    u(\rho) = \rho^{\,l+1}\,e^{-\rho}\,v(\rho)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Try a power series for v. Substituting it into the radial equation turns the ODE into a recursion: each coefficient follows from the one before it.">
    $$
    v(\rho) = \sum_{j=0}^{\infty} c_j\,\rho^j
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The recursion. ρ₀ packages the Coulomb strength together with the energy (through κ); everything follows from c₀.">
    $$
    c_{j+1} = \frac{2(j+l+1)-\rho_0}{(j+1)(j+2l+2)}\,c_j, \qquad \rho_0 \equiv \frac{me^2}{2\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2\kappa}
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The generic disaster: at large j this is the coefficient ratio of the series for e raised to 2ρ. A never-ending series rebuilds the growing exponential step 2 threw out, and u is not normalizable after all.">
    $$
    \frac{c_{j+1}}{c_j} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{2}{j} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v(\rho) \sim e^{2\rho}
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The only escape: the numerator must vanish at some finite j, cutting the series off into a polynomial. That forces ρ₀ to be an even integer and defines the integer n.">
    $$
    \rho_0 = 2(j_{\max}+l+1) \equiv 2n, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Unpack ρ₀ = 2n through the definitions of ρ₀ and κ. Nothing chose n except the demand that the series terminate.">
    $$
    \kappa = \frac{me^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2\,n} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m} = -\frac{me^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0^2\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

That sketch is the complete reason the energies are discrete. What it
defers is the rest of the solution: the terminating polynomials $v(\rho)$
are the associated Laguerre polynomials, and assembling them into
normalized radial wavefunctions $R_{nl}(r)$ is real work this course does
not repeat. The next lesson imports the pre-verified
`radial1s`/`radial2s`/`radial2p` functions instead. The result:
normalizable solutions exist only for a specific discrete set of
energies,

$$
E_n = -\frac{m e^4}{32\pi^2\epsilon_0^2\hbar^2}\cdot\frac1{n^2} = -\frac{13.6\text{ eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots
$$

where $n$ (the **principal quantum number**) is the integer the
series-termination condition manufactured in step 8, the same kind of
boundary-condition counting argument the
<Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term> and finite well used. Here the messy
combination of physical constants collapses to the clean number
$13.6\text{ eV}$, called **one Rydberg** ($\text{Ry}$).

### Why the energy doesn't depend on l or m

Look back at the derivation and something should be nagging. The
parameter $l$ appeared explicitly in the effective potential, sat inside
the recursion relation, and helped define $n = j_{\max}+l+1$. Yet the
final energy contains no $l$ at all. States with different $l$, and
therefore genuinely different-shaped radial wavefunctions, land on the
same energy for every $n\geq2$. Nothing in the argument required that;
it is a property of the exact $1/r$ form, traditionally called an
"accidental" degeneracy. Combined with the $2l+1$
degeneracy in $m$ from Angular Momentum & Spin, the total degeneracy of
level $n$ is $n^2$ (summing $2l+1$ over $l=0,\ldots,n-1$, a direct
algebra exercise, not a new physical input).

## Physical Interpretation

$E_1=-13.6\text{ eV}$ fixes the ionization energy of hydrogen, which is
its magnitude $+13.6\text{ eV}$: the energy that must be *supplied* to
free a ground-state electron completely, lifting it from $E_1$ up to
$E=0$, the threshold of an unbound state. As $n\to\infty$, $E_n\to0^-$: the
levels crowd together approaching the ionization threshold, the
"Rydberg series" pattern seen in hydrogen's real emission spectrum.
`hydrogenEnergyLevel(n)` in the course engine computes this
formula and was verified against $E_1=-13.6$, $E_2=-3.4$, $E_4=-0.85$ eV.

## Worked Example

**What photon energy is released when an electron falls from $n=3$ to
$n=2$? (This is the first line of the hydrogen Balmer series.)**

$$
E_3 = -\frac{13.6}{9} \approx -1.51\text{ eV}, \qquad E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{4} = -3.40\text{ eV}
$$

$$
\Delta E = E_3 - E_2 \approx -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\text{ eV}
$$

confirmed directly: `hydrogenEnergyLevel(3) - hydrogenEnergyLevel(2)`
$\approx1.89\text{ eV}$, matching the well-known Balmer-alpha transition.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the sign.** $E_n$ is negative for every bound state: the
electron is bound (has less energy than a free electron at rest at
infinity). A "higher" energy level (larger $n$) means a *less negative*
number, not a larger positive one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming $E$ depends on $l$.** This would be true for almost any other
central potential (a finite spherical well, for instance); the
$l$-independence is a special feature of the *exact* $1/r$ Coulomb form,
not a general rule this course's earlier central-potential argument
implies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Solving the radial equation for the Coulomb potential, with bound-state
  boundary conditions, quantizes energy: $E_n=-13.6\text{ eV}/n^2$.
- This $13.6\text{ eV}$ (one Rydberg) is hydrogen's ground-state
  ionization energy.
- $E_n$ depends only on $n$ (not $l$ or $m$), a special feature of the
  exact $1/r$ potential, giving each level an $n^2$-fold degeneracy.
