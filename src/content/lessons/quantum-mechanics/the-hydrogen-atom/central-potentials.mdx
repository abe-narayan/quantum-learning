## Different potentials, the same angular functions

Hydrogen, the finite spherical well, and the 3D <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic oscillator</Term> look
nothing alike as potentials, yet every one of them factors into a radial
piece times an angular piece, and the angular piece is always one of the
same functions $Y_l^m$, whichever $V(r)$ you picked. Three unrelated
potentials do not agree by accident, so there should be a reason, and the
reason turns out to have nothing to do with hydrogen. It is about
rotations.

Angular Momentum & Spin's capstone gave the punchline, that hydrogen's
wavefunction separates as $R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ with the angular
part already solved. What that lesson did not establish is why the
separation is legitimate. This one does, and the whole argument fits in
a commutator.

## Kepler's second law, and why it holds

A planet orbiting the sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times, Kepler's
second law, and the textbook reason is that gravity, like the Coulomb
force, pulls straight along the line connecting the two bodies: a force
with no sideways component exerts no torque, so classical angular
momentum $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ never changes. The quantum argument
below is the same idea in operator language. A potential that only cares
about distance from the origin, never direction, can't tell the
difference between "before" and "after" you rotate the whole system
around it. Rotating the electron's position doesn't change how far it is
from the proton, so it can't change its potential energy either. That's
the entire argument in one sentence: rotating everything is a symmetry
of $V(r)$, and whenever some operation is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian,
the operator that *generates* that operation, here, angular momentum,
has to commute with $H$. Nothing more exotic than that is needed.

## What depends on angle, and what does not

### Central potentials

A potential is **central** if it depends only on the distance from the
origin, $V(r)$, never on direction. Hydrogen's electron-proton Coulomb
attraction, $V(r)=-\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$, has this form: it knows
how far the electron is from the nucleus and nothing about which way.

### Why H commutes with L² and Lz

The Hamiltonian is $H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}+V(r)$. $L_z=xp_y-yp_x$ (Angular
Momentum & Spin, Lesson 1) is built entirely from position and momentum
components. The load-bearing step is that $V(r)$, depending only on the
radial distance $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$, commutes with $L_z$: rotating
about the $z$-axis leaves $r$ untouched, since a rotation preserves
distance from the origin by definition, and $L_z$ is the generator of
that rotation. That last clause is imported rather than re-derived here;
Angular Momentum & Spin established it.

Anyone who would rather see the cancellation than trust the symmetry can
have it in two lines, using only $[p_x,x]=[p_y,y]=-i\hbar$ and the fact
that a momentum commutes with every coordinate along a different axis:

$$
[L_z, r^2] = [xp_y, y^2] - [yp_x, x^2] = x(-2i\hbar y) - y(-2i\hbar x) = 0
$$

since $xp_y$ commutes with $x^2$ and $z^2$, and $yp_x$ with $y^2$ and
$z^2$. The two surviving terms are the same quantity with opposite signs.
Notice that the cancellation turned on $x$ and $y$ entering $r^2$ with
equal weight, which is the algebra's version of "no direction is
special."

The kinetic term $p^2/2m$ commutes with $L_z$ too, for the same kind of
reason: $L_z$ generates rotations, and rotations don't change how "spread
out" a wavefunction's momentum is, only its direction. Both pieces of $H$
separately commute with $L_z$, so $[H,L_z]=0$. The identical argument
(rotations about any axis preserve $r$) gives $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ too.

### Why this matters: simultaneous eigenstates

Operators, Observables & Measurement proved that commuting <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term>
operators share a common eigenbasis. Since $H$, $\vec L^{\,2}$, and $L_z$
all mutually commute, energy <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstates</Term> can be *chosen* to simultaneously
satisfy $\vec L^{\,2}\psi=l(l+1)\hbar^2\psi$ and $L_z\psi=m\hbar\psi$,
which means the angular part of any such $\psi$ has to be one of Angular
Momentum & Spin's $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ functions, already derived and
verified there, with no new angular calculation needed here.

## Nothing here is special to hydrogen

None of this is special to hydrogen. *Any* central potential, a 3D
harmonic oscillator or a finite spherical well included, separates the
same way and for the same reason: rotational symmetry of $V(r)$ guarantees
$H$ commutes with the generators of rotation. Hydrogen is the one case
this course solves in full, at $V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$.

## Commuting p-squared with Lz, without expanding

**Confirm $[p^2, L_z]=0$ using only that $p^2=p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2$ is
rotationally symmetric (built the same way from $p_x,p_y,p_z$ as $r^2$
is from $x,y,z$), without expanding every term explicitly.**

$L_z$ generates rotation about $z$ acting identically on any vector
built symmetrically from $(x,y,z)$ or $(p_x,p_y,p_z)$: $r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$
and $p^2=p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2$ have the *same* rotational structure (a sum
of squares of all three Cartesian components), so whatever argument
shows $[L_z,r^2]=0$ (rotation preserves distance) applies identically to
$[L_z,p^2]=0$ (rotation preserves total momentum magnitude); both are
rotationally invariant scalars, and $L_z$, generating rotations, commutes
with any rotationally invariant quantity by definition.

## Which potentials fail the central test

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming this separation works for *any* potential.** It specifically
requires $V$ to depend only on $r$: a potential like $V(x,y,z)=xyz$ or
even $V=x^2$ alone (not $x^2+y^2+z^2$) is *not* central, and $H$ would
not commute with $L_z$ or $\vec L^{\,2}$ for such a potential.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking $[H,L_z]=0$ means $H$ and $L_z$ are the same operator, or that
energy determines $m$.** It means they *can* be simultaneously
diagonalized: energy eigenstates are highly degenerate in $m$ because
$H$ doesn't depend on $m$ at all (only on $n$, and, before fine
structure, not even on $l$) for the Coulomb potential specifically.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is $V(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2$ (the 3D harmonic oscillator) a central
   potential? Justify using the definition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. The definition asks whether $V$ can be written as a function of
   $r$ alone, and this one is $r^2$ on the nose:

   $$
   x^2+y^2+z^2 = \left(\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\right)^2 = r^2
   $$

   Two points at the same distance from the origin get the same potential
   energy, whichever directions they lie in, so the commutator argument
   applies unchanged and this oscillator's energy eigenstates carry the
   same $Y_l^m$ angular factors hydrogen does.

   The word carrying the weight is *isotropic*, and it is easy to lose.
   The physical oscillator is $V=\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2$, one frequency for
   all three directions. Give the three axes different stiffnesses,

   $$
   V = \tfrac12 m\left(\omega_x^2x^2 + \omega_y^2y^2 + \omega_z^2z^2\right)
   $$

   and it is still a sum of squares, still perfectly solvable, and no
   longer central unless the three frequencies are equal. A point on the
   $x$ axis and a point at the same distance along $y$ now sit at
   different energies, so no function of $r$ alone reproduces it,
   $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]\neq0$, and the wavefunctions are products of
   one-dimensional Hermite functions rather than anything angular. "Sum of
   squares" is not the test. "Depends on $r$ and nothing else" is.

   </details>

2. Is $V(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2$ (no $z$-dependence, but not full 3D symmetry) a
   central potential? Why or why not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. It fails the definition at a single pair of points. Take
   $(0,0,5)$ and $(5,0,0)$: both sit at $r=5$, yet the first has $V=0$ and
   the second has $V=25$. A function of $r$ alone cannot assign two
   different values to the same $r$.

   What it is instead is *axially* symmetric: $x^2+y^2=\rho^2$ is the
   squared distance from the $z$ axis, not from the origin, so $V$ is
   unchanged by rotations about $z$ and changed by rotations about $x$ or
   $y$. That partial symmetry has a precise consequence worth tracing,
   because it is not all-or-nothing.

   $[H,L_z]=0$ still holds, since $L_z$ generates exactly the rotations
   this potential does respect. So $m$ survives as a good quantum number
   and energy eigenstates can still be chosen with definite $L_z$.

   $[H,L_x]$ and $[H,L_y]$ do not vanish, so
   $\vec L^{\,2}=L_x^2+L_y^2+L_z^2$ does not commute with $H$ either, and
   $l$ stops being a good quantum number. Energy eigenstates of this
   potential are generally superpositions of different $l$ at fixed $m$,
   and the wavefunction does not separate as $R(r)Y_l^m$. Cylindrical
   coordinates work; spherical ones do not.

   This is the useful way to hold the whole topic: how much symmetry $V$
   has decides how many labels the states get. Full rotational symmetry
   buys both $l$ and $m$; axial symmetry buys $m$ only; no symmetry buys
   neither.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why $[H,L_z]=0$ is necessary (though not
   sufficient on its own) for the energy eigenstates to be labeled by a
   definite $m$ quantum number.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because commuting is what makes a shared eigenbasis *available*, so
   without it no energy eigenstate could carry a definite $m$ at all,
   while with it you still have to go and choose that basis, since a
   degenerate energy level also contains superpositions of different $m$
   that are perfectly good energy eigenstates with no definite $m$
   whatsoever.

   The gap is worth seeing concretely. Hydrogen's $n=2$ level holds four
   degenerate states, among them $\psi_{2,1,+1}$ and $\psi_{2,1,-1}$. Any
   combination $a\psi_{2,1,+1}+b\psi_{2,1,-1}$ is still an eigenstate of
   $H$ with the same energy, because $H$ acts as one number on the whole
   degenerate subspace. It is not an eigenstate of $L_z$: measuring $L_z$
   on it returns $+\hbar$ or $-\hbar$ at random. The real $p_x$ and $p_y$
   orbitals that chemistry draws are precisely such combinations, and they
   have no definite $m$.

   So the correct statement of the theorem is "the eigenbasis can be
   chosen simultaneous," not "every energy eigenstate is automatically an
   $L_z$ eigenstate." Reading the second into the first is the standard
   error, and degeneracy is where it bites. When the spectrum is
   nondegenerate the two statements coincide, which is why the difference
   is easy to go years without noticing.

   </details>

4. If $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ but a specific potential's energy levels
   *did* depend on $l$ (unlike hydrogen's, which don't), would that
   contradict $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$? Explain why or why not.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No contradiction. A commutator constrains the eigen*vectors*, not the
   eigen*values*: it says $H$ and $\vec L^{\,2}$ can be diagonalized in
   one basis, and says nothing about how $H$'s numbers vary across that
   basis.

   In fact $l$-dependent energies are the normal case and hydrogen is the
   exception. Any central potential that is not $1/r$ (or the isotropic
   oscillator) gives $E_{n,l}$ depending on both labels, while the
   separation into $R_{n,l}(r)Y_l^m$ still holds perfectly. You can see it
   without leaving the periodic table: in every atom past hydrogen, the
   inner electrons screen the nucleus, the outer electron feels something
   that is central but no longer pure $1/r$, and $2s$ and $2p$ split apart
   in energy. That splitting is why lithium's chemistry is not helium's,
   and nothing about it disturbs $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$, which still holds
   for the screened central potential.

   The converse case makes the same point from the other side and is
   worth having. Put a hydrogen atom in a magnetic field along $z$. The
   Hamiltonian gains a term proportional to $L_z$, which still commutes
   with $L_z$, so $[H,L_z]=0$ survives and $m$ remains a good quantum
   number. The energies now depend on $m$ and the level splits into
   $2l+1$ lines. That is the Zeeman effect, and it is a commuting pair
   with eigenvalue dependence, in plain sight.

   What *would* contradict $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ is a state with definite
   $l$ evolving into a mixture of different $l$, or a level whose
   eigenstates could not be organized by $l$ at all. Degeneracy patterns
   are a separate question, and answering it requires knowing the actual
   $V(r)$, not just its symmetry.

   </details>

## Summary

- A central potential $V(r)$ depends only on distance from the origin,
  never direction; hydrogen's Coulomb potential is one.
- $[H,L_z]=[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ for any central potential, since rotations
  preserve $r$ and hence preserve $V(r)$ and the rotationally-symmetric
  kinetic term.
- This lets energy eigenstates be chosen as simultaneous $H$, $\vec
  L^{\,2}$, $L_z$ eigenstates, forcing the angular part to be one of
  Angular Momentum & Spin's already-derived $Y_l^m$ functions.
