## Motivation

Hydrogen, the finite spherical well, and the 3D harmonic oscillator look
nothing alike as potentials, yet every one of them factors into a radial
piece times an angular piece, and the angular piece is always built from
exactly the same handful of functions, $Y_l^m$, no matter which of the
three $V(r)$ actually is. That's a strange thing for three unrelated
potentials to have in common, unless there's a shared reason it has to be
true. There is, and it has almost nothing to do with hydrogen
specifically: it's about rotations. Angular Momentum & Spin's capstone
previewed the punchline, that hydrogen's wavefunction separates into
$R(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ with the angular part already fully solved.
This lesson proves *why* that separation is valid at all: the answer is a
commutator, not a coincidence.

## Intuition

A potential that only cares about distance from the origin, never
direction, can't tell the difference between "before" and "after" you
rotate the whole system around it. Rotating the electron's position
doesn't change how far it is from the proton, so it can't change its
potential energy either. That's the entire argument in one sentence:
rotating everything is a symmetry of $V(r)$, and whenever some operation
is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, the operator that *generates* that
operation, here, angular momentum, has to commute with $H$. Nothing more
exotic than that is needed.

## Mathematical Development

### Central potentials

A potential is **central** if it depends only on the distance from the
origin, $V(r)$, never on direction. Hydrogen's electron-proton Coulomb
attraction, $V(r)=-\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}$, is exactly this form:
it only knows how far the electron is from the nucleus, not which way.

### Why H commutes with L² and Lz

The Hamiltonian is $H=\dfrac{p^2}{2m}+V(r)$. $L_z=xp_y-yp_x$ (Angular
Momentum & Spin, Lesson 1) is built entirely from position and momentum
components; crucially, $V(r)$ (depending only on the radial distance
$r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$) commutes with $L_z$, because rotating about the
$z$-axis doesn't change $r$ at all (a rotation preserves distance from
the origin by definition), and $L_z$ *generates* exactly that rotation.

The kinetic term $p^2/2m$ commutes with $L_z$ too, for the same kind of
reason: $L_z$ generates rotations, and rotations don't change how "spread
out" a wavefunction's momentum is, only its direction. Both pieces of $H$
separately commute with $L_z$, so $[H,L_z]=0$. The identical argument
(rotations about any axis preserve $r$) gives $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ too.

### Why this matters: simultaneous eigenstates

Operators, Observables & Measurement proved that commuting Hermitian
operators share a common eigenbasis. Since $H$, $\vec L^{\,2}$, and $L_z$
all mutually commute, energy eigenstates can be *chosen* to simultaneously
satisfy $\vec L^{\,2}\psi=l(l+1)\hbar^2\psi$ and $L_z\psi=m\hbar\psi$,
which means the angular part of any such $\psi$ is forced to be exactly
one of Angular Momentum & Spin's $Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ functions,
already fully derived and verified there, with zero new angular
calculation needed here. This is exactly why the shapes below are the
*only* angular shapes any central potential's energy eigenstates can

## Physical Interpretation

This isn't special to hydrogen: *any* central potential (a 3D harmonic
oscillator, a finite spherical well, hydrogen) separates the same way,
for the same reason: rotational symmetry of $V(r)$ guarantees $H$
commutes with the generators of rotation. Hydrogen is simply the specific
$V(r)=-e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0r$ case this course solves completely.

## Worked Example

**Confirm $[p^2, L_z]=0$ using only that $p^2=p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2$ is
rotationally symmetric (built the same way from $p_x,p_y,p_z$ as $r^2$
is from $x,y,z$), without expanding every term explicitly.**

$L_z$ generates rotation about $z$ acting identically on any vector
built symmetrically from $(x,y,z)$ or $(p_x,p_y,p_z)$: $r^2=x^2+y^2+z^2$
and $p^2=p_x^2+p_y^2+p_z^2$ have the *same* rotational structure (a sum
of squares of all three Cartesian components), so whatever argument
shows $[L_z,r^2]=0$ (rotation preserves distance) applies identically to
$[L_z,p^2]=0$ (rotation preserves total momentum magnitude); both are
rotationally invariant scalars, and $L_z$, generating rotations, commutes
with any rotationally invariant quantity by definition.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming this separation works for *any* potential.** It specifically
requires $V$ to depend only on $r$: a potential like $V(x,y,z)=xyz$ or
even $V=x^2$ alone (not $x^2+y^2+z^2$) is *not* central, and $H$ would
not commute with $L_z$ or $\vec L^{\,2}$ for such a potential.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking $[H,L_z]=0$ means $H$ and $L_z$ are the same operator, or that
energy determines $m$.** It means they *can* be simultaneously
diagonalized: energy eigenstates are highly degenerate in $m$ precisely
because $H$ doesn't depend on $m$ at all (only on $n$, and, before fine
structure, not even on $l$) for the Coulomb potential specifically.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A central potential $V(r)$ depends only on distance from the origin,
  never direction; hydrogen's Coulomb potential is exactly this form.
- $[H,L_z]=[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$ for any central potential, since rotations
  preserve $r$ and hence preserve $V(r)$ and the rotationally-symmetric
  kinetic term.
- This lets energy eigenstates be chosen as simultaneous $H$, $\vec
  L^{\,2}$, $L_z$ eigenstates, forcing the angular part to be exactly
  one of Angular Momentum & Spin's already-derived $Y_l^m$ functions.
