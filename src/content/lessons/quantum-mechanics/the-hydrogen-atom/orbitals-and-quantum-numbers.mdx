## Assembly work, worth doing carefully

Nothing new needs deriving here. This lesson is assembly work: multiply
the previous lesson's radial functions by Angular Momentum & Spin's
spherical harmonics and read off what the resulting
<Term id="wavefunction">wavefunctions</Term> look like.

Assembly is worth a lesson of its own because the pieces have been apart
for a long time. The angular factors were settled two courses ago from
commutation relations, with no atom in sight; the radial factors came out
of a Coulomb boundary-value problem last lesson, with no angles in sight.
Neither half is a picture of anything. Multiplied together, they are the
electron cloud that every chemistry textbook draws, and the labels $s$,
$p$, $d$ stop being nomenclature and become values of $l$.

## Three integers, and the function they fix

### The three quantum numbers

$$
\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\varphi) = R_{nl}(r)\, Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)
$$

- $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ (principal quantum number): sets the energy, $E_n=-13.6\text{ eV}/n^2$.
- $l=0,1,\ldots,n-1$ (orbital angular momentum quantum number): sets $\vec L^{\,2}=l(l+1)\hbar^2$; chemistry labels $l=0,1,2,3$ as $s,p,d,f$.
- $m=-l,\ldots,l$ (magnetic quantum number): sets $L_z=m\hbar$.

Every allowed $(n,l,m)$ triple names one orbital. "2p" means $n=2,l=1$
(with $m=-1,0,1$ giving three degenerate 2p orbitals); "1s" means
$n=1,l=0,m=0$, the only orbital allowed at $n=1$.

### Assembling ψ from already-verified pieces

This platform never had to derive a new radial-times-angular product
formula: it's already built, in two separately-verified pieces:
`radial1s`/`radial2s`/`radial2p` (verified <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term>, previous lessons)
and `sphericalHarmonic({l,m},θ,φ)` (verified normalized and orthogonal,
Angular Momentum & Spin). The full 3D <Term id="probability-density">probability density</Term> is:

$$
|\psi_{nlm}(r,\theta,\varphi)|^2 = |R_{nl}(r)|^2\,|Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)|^2
$$

Because that is a product of two independently normalized pieces, the
combined 3D integral
$\int|\psi_{nlm}|^2\,r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi$ comes out to
$1$ with no new normalization work: each factor already integrates to $1$
over its own variables.

Both factors so far were only ever shown separately. Here is the actual
product, $|R_{nl}(r)|^2|Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)|^2$, plotted as a
cross-section through the $(r,\theta)$ half-plane, the standard textbook
electron-cloud picture, for the three $m=0$ states 1s, 2s, and 2p$_0$:

### The 1s orbital's radial shape

The previous course's `mostProbableRadius1s()` function directly
maximizes $r^2|R_{10}(r)|^2$ (not $|R_{10}(r)|^2$ alone: the $r^2$
comes from the spherical volume element, and it matters: the *density*
$|R_{10}|^2$ is largest at $r=0$, but the *probability of finding the
electron in a thin shell at radius r* is largest away from the origin,
weighted by the shell's growing surface area $4\pi r^2$). The verified
result is $r=1$, in units where the Bohr radius $a_0=1$: numerically the
Bohr model's ground-state radius, reached by maximizing a probability
density rather than by assuming a circular orbit.

The three radial functions look distinctly different once plotted, not
just algebraically different:

Compare 1s and 2p directly: 1s peaks once, close to the origin (at $r=1$,
computed above); 2p peaks once too, but farther out and only a third as
tall, since the $r$ in `radial2p`'s explicit $r\,e^{-r/2}$ makes the
plotted $r^2|R|^2$ start off as $r^4$ near the origin rather than $r^2$,
pushing its weight away from the nucleus in a way 1s's is not.

2s shows something neither does: a **node**, a radius where the
distribution touches zero before rising again, from the $(2-r)$ factor in
`radial2s` changing sign at $r=2$. It leaves 2s with two humps, a small
one inside the node and a larger one outside. The count generalizes:
$R_{nl}$ has $n-l-1$ radial nodes, which gives $0$ for 1s, $0$ for 2p,
and $1$ for 2s, and is the reason 2s and 2p sit at the same energy while
looking nothing alike.

## Why s orbitals are round and p orbitals point

The 1s and 2s orbitals ($l=0$) are spherically symmetric: $Y_0^0$ is a
constant, so all angular dependence vanishes, and the "shape" is purely
radial. The 2p orbitals ($l=1$) point in specific directions, following
$Y_1^{-1},Y_1^0,Y_1^1$'s angular dependence, and it is that
directionality that sets molecular bond geometry in chemistry (an
application well beyond this course's scope). The angular part
alone, independent of which radial function it's paired with, looks like
this:

## Normalization, from two already-normalized factors

**Confirm that $\psi_{100}$ (the 1s orbital) is properly normalized,
using the fact that both factors are independently normalized.**

$$
\int|\psi_{100}|^2\,r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\varphi = \left(\int|R_{10}(r)|^2r^2\,dr\right)\left(\int|Y_0^0(\theta,\varphi)|^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi\right) = (1)(1) = 1
$$

using `radialNormSquared(radial1s)`$\approx1$ (verified, previous
lesson) and `sphericalHarmonicNormSquared({l:0,m:0})`$\approx1$
(verified, Angular Momentum & Spin), no new integral needed.

## Radial density against radial distribution

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing $|R_{nl}(r)|^2$ (radial probability density) with $r^2|R_{nl}(r)|^2$
(radial distribution, probability per unit r).** The 1s density
$|R_{10}(r)|^2$ peaks at $r=0$; only the $r^2$-weighted version
peaks at $r=1$ (the Bohr radius). Both are used, for different
questions: "how dense is it right at a point" vs. "how likely is a
measurement to land in this shell."
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Reading "most probable radius equals the Bohr radius" as "the electron
orbits at that radius."** Unlike the Bohr model's fixed circular orbit,
the hydrogen ground state has a nonzero probability of being measured at
*any* $r>0$: $r=1$ is only the single most likely value, from a broad
continuous distribution, not a trajectory.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. List all $(n,l,m)$ triples for $n=3$, grouped by $l$, and state the
   chemistry shorthand name (3s, 3p, 3d) for each group.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $l$ running from $0$ to $n-1=2$, and $m$ from $-l$ to $+l$:

   | $l$ | $(n,l,m)$ triples | Name | Count |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | $0$ | $(3,0,0)$ | 3s | 1 |
   | $1$ | $(3,1,-1),(3,1,0),(3,1,+1)$ | 3p | 3 |
   | $2$ | $(3,2,-2),(3,2,-1),(3,2,0),(3,2,+1),(3,2,+2)$ | 3d | 5 |

   Nine in total, which is $n^2$ at $n=3$, and $18$ once spin is counted.
   All nine sit at $E_3=-1.51\text{ eV}$ in pure hydrogen.

   Note the constraint $l\le n-1$ is doing real work here: there is no
   "3f." The reason is in the last lesson's derivation, where $n$ was
   *defined* as $j_{\max}+l+1$ with $j_{\max}\ge0$ counting the terms of
   the terminating polynomial, so $l$ can never reach $n$. Equivalently,
   $n-l-1$ is the number of radial nodes and cannot be negative. The rule
   is not a separate postulate; it is the series-termination condition
   restated.

   </details>

2. Why is $|\psi_{1s}|^2$ spherically symmetric while $|\psi_{2p_0}|^2$
   is not? Trace this to a specific property of $Y_0^0$ vs. $Y_1^0$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $Y_0^0=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}$ is a constant while
   $Y_1^0=\sqrt{\dfrac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta$ is not. The angular factor is
   the only place direction can enter, and at $l=0$ there is nothing
   there.

   Take the densities apart:

   $$
   |\psi_{1s}|^2 = |R_{10}(r)|^2\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi}, \qquad |\psi_{2p_0}|^2 = |R_{21}(r)|^2\cdot\frac{3}{4\pi}\cos^2\theta
   $$

   The first depends on $r$ alone, so every direction from the nucleus
   looks the same. The second carries $\cos^2\theta$, which is maximal
   along $\pm z$ and vanishes at $\theta=\pi/2$, producing the two lobes
   and the nodal plane through the equator.

   The step worth making explicit is that the radial factor is
   *incapable* of breaking spherical symmetry, whatever it looks like.
   $R_{nl}(r)$ is a function of distance only, by construction. $2s$ has a
   node, two humps and a shape nothing like $1s$'s, and it is still
   perfectly spherical, because a node in $r$ is a spherical shell. So
   the question "is this orbital round" is answered by $l$ alone, and
   never by $n$: every $s$ orbital is round, no $p$ or $d$ orbital is.

   One refinement on "not symmetric." $2p_0$ has $m=0$, so its
   $e^{im\varphi}$ factor is $1$ and the density has no $\varphi$
   dependence at all: it is symmetric about the $z$ axis, just not about
   every axis. That partial symmetry is the visible trace of $m$ being a
   good quantum number about $z$ specifically.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why $\int|\psi_{nlm}|^2\,d^3r=1$ follows
   automatically from the radial and angular pieces each separately
   normalizing to 1, without a new 3D integral.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because in spherical coordinates both the integrand and the volume
   element factor into a purely radial part and a purely angular part, so
   the triple integral separates into a product of two integrals that were
   each already computed and each already equal to $1$.

   Written out, with $d^3r=r^2\,dr\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\varphi$:

   $$
   \int|R_{nl}|^2|Y_l^m|^2\,d^3r = \left(\int_0^\infty |R_{nl}(r)|^2r^2\,dr\right)\left(\int|Y_l^m|^2\,d\Omega\right) = (1)(1) = 1
   $$

   Two conditions make that split legal, and both happen to hold here
   rather than being automatic. The integrand has to factor, which it does
   because $\psi$ was separable to begin with. And the region has to be a
   product region: $r$ ranges over $[0,\infty)$ independently of the
   angles, and the angles over the full sphere independently of $r$. Over
   a region where the limits on one variable depended on another, the
   integral would not separate no matter how cleanly the integrand did.

   The $r^2$ has to travel with the radial factor, and that is the part
   people drop. It belongs to the volume element rather than to the
   wavefunction, which is why the platform's `radialNormSquared` includes
   it and why the radial distribution plotted above is $r^2|R|^2$ rather
   than $|R|^2$.

   </details>

4. Is the most-probable-radius result ($r=1$ for 1s) evidence that the
   Bohr model was secretly correct about orbits? Explain what it does
   and doesn't establish.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. It establishes that the two treatments agree on a length scale, and
   nothing whatever about orbits.

   What the agreement does mean is worth stating fairly, because it is not
   empty. Both descriptions produce the same characteristic size for the
   ground state, and that size is not a fitted parameter in either one. It
   is also, on reflection, not much of a coincidence: $a_0=4\pi\epsilon_0
   \hbar^2/me^2$ is the only length that can be assembled from the
   constants in the problem, so any correct account of hydrogen has to
   land on it up to a factor of order one.

   What it does not establish is a trajectory, and the clearest way to see
   that is to notice where the two models flatly contradict each other
   rather than merely differ in emphasis. Bohr's $n=1$ orbit has orbital
   angular momentum $|\vec L|=\hbar$, by construction: quantized circular
   motion was the whole postulate. The true $1s$ state has $l=0$, so
   $\vec L^{\,2}=0$ and every component of angular momentum is zero. The
   real ground state has no orbital motion at all. Bohr got the right
   energy from a picture whose central quantity has the wrong value.

   Two more things the number hides. The distribution is broad: $r=a_0$ is
   its mode, while its mean is $\langle r\rangle=\tfrac32a_0$, and the
   probability of finding the electron at any $r>0$ is nonzero. A single
   radius is a summary statistic of a spread-out cloud, not a location.
   And the mode only appears at $a_0$ once the $r^2$ volume weighting is
   included: the density $|R_{10}|^2$ itself is largest at the nucleus and
   falls monotonically, with no special feature at $a_0$ at all.

   The broader test is what each model predicts beyond this one number.
   Bohr gives no orbital shapes, no $l$ or $m$ labels, no degeneracies, no
   selection rules, and fails outright for helium. The Schrödinger
   treatment gives all of them. Agreement on one length is what you should
   expect from a wrong model that got the scale right, not evidence that
   it was secretly correct.

   </details>

## Summary

- Three quantum numbers $(n,l,m)$ label every hydrogen orbital; $n$
  sets energy, $l$ sets $\vec L^{\,2}$, $m$ sets $L_z$.
- $\psi_{nlm}=R_{nl}(r)Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi)$ assembles directly from
  this course's verified radial functions and Angular Momentum & Spin's
  verified spherical harmonics; normalization comes for free.
- The 1s state's most probable radius, found by direct numerical
  maximization, equals the Bohr radius: a checkable numerical agreement
  between the full quantum treatment and the older Bohr model, reached by
  two unrelated arguments.
