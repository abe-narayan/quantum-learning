<LessonHook eyebrow="Real hydrogen has neither property">
Every energy value this course computed, Eₙ=−13.6 eV/n², independent of l and m, came from solving the non-relativistic Schrödinger equation with a spin-free electron. Real hydrogen has neither property exactly.
</LessonHook>

## Small, real, and worth naming

The gap between the model and the real atom is small but real. The
electron carries spin, which the Schrödinger treatment ignored, and it
moves fast enough ($v/c\sim\alpha\approx1/137$) that relativistic
corrections enter at the $\alpha^2$ level. This closing lesson is honest
about that gap instead of pretending it doesn't exist, and previews
(without deriving) what the next level of approximation adds.

## Two corrections of about the same size

### Two corrections, roughly the same size

**Spin-orbit coupling.** The orbiting electron sees the proton
circulating around *it* (in the electron's own rest frame), a moving
charge, which is a current, which generates a magnetic field. The
electron's own spin magnetic moment then couples to this field, adding
an energy term $\propto\vec L\cdot\vec S$. This term explicitly breaks
the clean separation this course used between $\vec L$ (orbital) and
$\vec S$ (spin, from Angular Momentum & Spin); the good quantum numbers
become total angular momentum $\vec J=\vec L+\vec S$ <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstates</Term> (a
direct application of Addition of Angular Momentum), not separate $L_z$
and $S_z$ eigenstates.

**Relativistic kinetic correction.** This course used the
non-relativistic $p^2/2m$ throughout. The next-order relativistic
correction (from expanding $\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2$ instead) shifts
energies by an amount that depends on both $n$ and $l$.

### What breaks, specifically

Both corrections scale as roughly $\alpha^2\times13.6\text{ eV}/n^3$
(times an order-one factor that depends on $j$), where
$\alpha\approx1/137$ is the fine-structure constant, so
$\alpha^2\approx5.3\times10^{-5}$. That suppression is small enough
that this course's $E_n=-13.6/n^2$ is an excellent approximation for most
purposes, but large enough to measure. Fine structure was discovered
spectroscopically, as a splitting of lines the Bohr model predicted to be
single.

## What this course got right, and where it stops

This course derived the leading-order, non-relativistic, spin-free
hydrogen atom completely and correctly. The energies, orbitals, and
degeneracies are checkable results, not simplifications hiding wrong
answers. Fine structure is a separate next-order correction that this
course does not derive; doing so requires either full relativistic
quantum mechanics (the Dirac equation) or
<Term id="degenerate-perturbation-theory">degenerate perturbation theory</Term> applied to $\vec L\cdot\vec S$, both
beyond this course's scope. What is offered here is a map of what is
missing, which is a different thing from the calculation itself.

## The l-independence is a model artifact

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking the $l$-independence of $E_n$ (Hydrogen Energy Levels) is
exact in real hydrogen.** It's exact only in the non-relativistic,
spin-free model this course builds; real hydrogen's spectral lines show
fine splitting that depends on $j$ as well as $n$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming fine structure is a large effect.** At a relative size of
roughly $\alpha^2/n\lesssim5\times10^{-5}$, it's a precision correction,
not a qualitative change: this course's $E_n=-13.6/n^2$ remains an
excellent approximation for essentially every purpose outside
high-resolution spectroscopy.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. What two physical effects combine to produce fine structure, at a
   qualitative level?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Spin-orbit coupling and the relativistic correction to the kinetic
   energy.

   Spin-orbit coupling is a magnetic effect. In the electron's own rest
   frame the proton is the thing that moves, and a moving charge is a
   current, which makes a magnetic field at the electron. The electron's
   spin magnetic moment has an orientation energy in that field, which
   turns out to be proportional to $\vec L\cdot\vec S$. It is an
   interaction between the electron's motion and its own spin, mediated by
   the frame change, which is why it exists at all only for a particle
   with spin and only when it is orbiting.

   The relativistic kinetic correction has nothing to do with spin or
   magnetism. This course used $T=p^2/2m$, the first term of

   $$
   \sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}-mc^2 = \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \cdots
   $$

   and the $-p^4/8m^3c^2$ term is the next one. It lowers the energy of
   states whose electron moves fastest, which are the low-$l$ states
   penetrating closest to the nucleus, so it depends on $l$ as well as
   $n$.

   The reason they are grouped together rather than treated separately is
   that they come out the same size. Both are suppressed by $\alpha^2$
   relative to $E_n$, which is not a coincidence: $\alpha=v/c$ for the
   ground-state electron in the Bohr picture, and both are the
   leading $(v/c)^2$ correction to a non-relativistic treatment, reached
   by different routes. Keeping one and dropping the other would be
   inconsistent.

   One honesty note about the count. A complete accounting has a third
   piece of the same order, the Darwin term, which affects $l=0$ states
   only. It is needed because $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ vanishes when
   $\vec L=0$, so without it the $s$ states would receive only the kinetic
   correction and the final answer would not come out depending on $j$
   alone. "Two effects" is the right summary of the physics; "two terms"
   is one short of the calculation.

   </details>

2. Which specific result from Hydrogen Energy Levels does fine structure
   modify, and why does adding electron spin make this modification
   necessary?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It breaks the exact $l$-independence of $E_n$. In this course's model
   all $n^2$ states of a shell share one energy; with fine structure the
   shell splits, and the surviving label is $j$ rather than $l$.

   Spin's role deserves splitting in two, because only half of it depends
   on spin.

   The kinetic correction $-p^4/8m^3c^2$ needs no spin whatsoever. It
   already depends on $l$, through how deeply each orbital penetrates
   toward the nucleus, so the $l$-degeneracy would break even for a
   spinless electron. The accidental degeneracy is fragile, and
   relativity alone is enough to spoil it.

   What spin supplies is the $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ term, and with it the
   reason the final answer organizes by $j$. Set $\vec S=0$ and that term
   is identically zero: there is no magnetic moment for the orbital field
   to act on and nothing to couple. Spin also doubles the state space,
   from $n^2$ orbital states to $2n^2$, so there are more levels available
   to split apart than the spin-free model had.

   The upshot is that $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ end up degenerate while
   $2p_{3/2}$ sits above them: same $n$, different $l$, and the energy
   tracks $j$. Fine structure does not merely shift the levels. It changes
   which quantum number the answer is a function of, and that is a
   statement about which observables are still conserved.

   </details>

3. Roughly how large is the fine-structure correction relative to
   $E_n$ itself (order of magnitude)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   About $\alpha^2\approx5\times10^{-5}$, so a few parts in $10^5$, and it
   shrinks further as $\alpha^2/n$ at higher $n$.

   In absolute terms at $n=1$ that is roughly
   $5.3\times10^{-5}\times13.6\text{ eV}\approx7\times10^{-4}\text{ eV}$,
   under a millielectronvolt, against a level depth of $13.6\text{ eV}$.

   The reason the ratio falls rather than staying fixed is that the
   correction goes as $1/n^3$ while $|E_n|$ goes as $1/n^2$, so their
   quotient carries one leftover power of $n$. Fine structure is largest
   where the electron is deepest, fastest, and closest to the nucleus, and
   fades into irrelevance for Rydberg states.

   The practical reading: this course's $E_n=-13.6\text{ eV}/n^2$ is
   correct to four or five significant figures, which is better than most
   purposes need. It is not, however, below the noise floor of a
   spectrometer, and it was not below the noise floor of one in 1887,
   which is how Michelson and Morley found the splitting decades before
   anyone could explain it.

   </details>

4. Why is $\vec J=\vec L+\vec S$, rather than separate $L_z$ and $S_z$
   eigenstates, the right <Term id="basis">basis</Term> once spin-orbit coupling is included?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ does not commute with $L_z$ or $S_z$
   separately, but does commute with $\vec J^{\,2}$ and $J_z$. A basis is
   the right one when it diagonalizes the Hamiltonian, and only the total
   angular momentum basis does.

   The algebra makes this immediate. Square the total:

   $$
   \vec J^{\,2} = (\vec L+\vec S)^2 = \vec L^{\,2} + \vec S^{\,2} + 2\,\vec L\cdot\vec S \implies \vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac12\left(\vec J^{\,2} - \vec L^{\,2} - \vec S^{\,2}\right)
   $$

   Every operator on the right is diagonal in the $|j,m_j,l,s\rangle$
   basis, so the perturbation is too, with eigenvalue

   $$
   \langle \vec L\cdot\vec S\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\Big[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)\Big]
   $$

   No matrix has to be diagonalized. The right basis turns the hard part
   into an identity, which is the usual signature of having chosen it
   correctly.

   Run it for the $2p$ states, $l=1$ and $s=\tfrac12$, where addition of
   angular momentum gives $j=\tfrac32$ and $j=\tfrac12$:

   $$
   j=\tfrac32:\ \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[\tfrac{15}4 - 2 - \tfrac34\right] = +\frac{\hbar^2}{2}, \qquad j=\tfrac12:\ \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[\tfrac34 - 2 - \tfrac34\right] = -\hbar^2
   $$

   One level up, one down, in the ratio $1:-2$, which is the $2:1$
   degeneracy ratio inverted, as it must be for the shifts to average to
   zero. The state count checks out too: $3\times2=6$ states before
   coupling, and $4+2=6$ after, split as $2p_{3/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$.

   The physical reading behind the algebra. $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ is a
   torque each angular momentum exerts on the other, so neither is
   conserved on its own any more, and $m_l$ and $m_s$ stop being good
   quantum numbers. What is still conserved is the total, because the
   interaction is internal to the atom and the atom as a whole remains
   rotationally symmetric. That is the general lesson, not a hydrogen
   trick: when two subsystems couple, the conserved quantity is the sum,
   and the basis to work in is the one the sum diagonalizes.

   </details>

## Summary

- Fine structure combines spin-orbit coupling ($\vec L\cdot\vec S$) and
  a relativistic kinetic correction, both scaling as
  $\sim\alpha^2\times13.6\text{ eV}/n^3$, so the correction shrinks with
  $n$ faster than $|E_n|$ itself does.
- It breaks this course's exact $l$-independence of $E_n$: real
  hydrogen's energy levels split slightly by total angular momentum $j$.
- This course's results are the correct non-relativistic, spin-free
  hydrogen atom: a foundation to build on, and never a simplification
  to unlearn, with one clearly stated next-order correction sitting
  beyond its scope.
