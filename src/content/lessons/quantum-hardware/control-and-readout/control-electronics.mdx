## The Rack Between Diagram and Qubit

Between an abstract circuit diagram and a physical qubit sits a rack of
classical electronics whose job is to turn each gate symbol into a
precisely-shaped control pulse, microwave for a superconducting circuit,
laser for an ion or atom. This lesson makes that translation concrete,
reusing machinery already built and verified: Approximation Methods'
exact two-level <Term id="rabi-oscillation">Rabi model</Term>. It then
asks the question the idealized model skips entirely: what shape should
the pulse be?

## From Rotation Angle to Pulse Duration

### Resonant driving reproduces exact Rabi oscillation

Physical Qubit Platforms' superconducting-qubit lesson already
established: at exact resonance ($E_i=E_f$), this platform's
`exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0, 0, Omega, t)` reproduces
$P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ to machine precision, a verified
identity connecting the abstract two-level Schrödinger equation to
observable population transfer. Driving a real qubit with a microwave
tone at its resonance frequency, for a controlled duration $t$, realizes
this dynamics.

<Callout type="warning">
**Watch the Rabi convention when reading datasheets and papers.**
Throughout this track, $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ and a $\pi$-pulse
takes $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$. The more common convention in the
literature defines $\Omega$ so that $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$ and
$t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$. Same physics, but the two $\Omega$s differ by a
factor of 2, so a Rabi frequency copied from a paper into this lesson's
pulse-duration formulas produces gate times off by exactly that factor.
Convert first.
</Callout>

### From target rotation angle to pulse duration

A rotation by angle $\theta$ about the driven axis (in the idealized
resonant, rotating-frame picture) corresponds to $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$,
so an X gate ($\theta=\pi$, full population transfer) needs
$\Omega t=\pi/2$, while a half-way rotation ($\theta=\pi/2$) needs
$\Omega t=\pi/4$, exactly half the X gate's duration at the same drive
strength. This is a direct, computable relationship: given a fixed Rabi
frequency $\Omega$ (set by the hardware's drive amplitude), the *pulse
duration* alone determines which rotation angle is implemented.

### Two equivalent control knobs

Physically, there are two ways to reach a target rotation: fix $\Omega$
and vary pulse *duration* $t$ (used throughout this lesson), or fix a
reference duration and vary drive *amplitude* (which sets $\Omega$
itself, since $\Omega$ is proportional to the microwave field strength).
Real control electronics typically use whichever is more precisely
controllable in a given setup. Both reach the identical target physics,
$\Omega t=\theta/2$.

### Why the pulse isn't square

Everything so far has treated a pulse as a switch: drive at full
strength for duration $t$, then stop instantly. Real control
electronics almost never do this, and the reason is a Fourier fact. A
pulse that turns on and off abruptly is short in time, so it is *wide*
in frequency: a square envelope of duration $t$ has a sinc-shaped
spectrum whose power spreads over a bandwidth of roughly $1/t$ around
the carrier. Shrink the gate time toward nanoseconds and that spectral
skirt spreads across tens of megahertz or more.

For a transmon, that width lands somewhere dangerous. Superconducting
Qubits built the whole platform on the junction's anharmonicity, the
fact that the 1→2 gap sits *near* the 0→1 gap but not on it. Near is
the operative word: the offset is small compared to the qubit frequency
itself. A short square pulse's sinc skirt reaches that offset easily,
so a drive nominally addressed to 0→1 also delivers real power at the
1→2 transition and leaks population out of the qubit subspace entirely.
This is the same failure Superconducting Qubits' prediction exercise
flagged when it warned that pushing drive strength for faster gates
eventually "starts exciting unwanted transitions": stronger and shorter
driving means a wider spectrum, and the anharmonicity gap is the wall
it hits.

The fix is to shape the envelope. A smooth, Gaussian-style rise and
fall has a spectrum that falls off far faster than a sinc, so nearly
all the drive power stays concentrated at the 0→1 transition, at the
modest cost of a slightly longer pulse for the same rotation angle.
The standard refinement on top of this is **DRAG** (Derivative Removal
by Adiabatic Gate), which adds a small corrective component
proportional to the envelope's time derivative to cancel the residual
1→2 leakage a plain Gaussian still leaves. The arbitrary waveform
generators described below exist to synthesize these shaped envelopes,
sample by sample; a box that could only switch a tone on and off would
not be enough.

## Where the Abstraction Meets the Instrument

This is the concrete link between "abstract quantum circuit" (this
platform's Quantum Computing track) and "real hardware" (this track):
every gate you draw in a circuit diagram ultimately becomes a specific,
computed pulse duration and amplitude, generated by classical arbitrary
waveform generators and up-converted to the qubit's microwave frequency.

None of this happens at a single temperature. The pulse this section
computes is generated at room temperature, then travels down through
the same staged cooling Cryogenic Systems described, attenuated at each
stage to strip the thermal noise that stage's wiring would otherwise
inject, before ever reaching the qubit; the reflected readout signal
(Qubit Readout Techniques, next) makes the same trip in reverse, back up
through an amplifier chain to a room-temperature digitizer:

<ControlSignalChainDiagram ariaLabel="The full drive and readout signal chain: a room-temperature AWG and IQ mixer send a pulse down through the fridge's cooling stages, attenuated at each one, to the qubit, while the readout tone returns up through an amplifier chain to a room-temperature digitizer" />

The generator has to be fast enough to place a pulse edge where
the math says. A control-grade arbitrary waveform generator synthesizing
a baseband envelope for later up-conversion typically samples at 1-2
GS/s, a 0.5-1 ns tick, which is coarse next to the $4.17\text{ ns}$
pulse computed below but still gives several samples across it; slower,
cheaper AWGs (a few hundred MS/s) start to blur a pulse that short.
Systems that skip the mixer and synthesize the microwave tone directly,
the RF-system-on-chip designs increasingly used for this, run several
times faster still, and the arithmetic below is the same with a finer
tick. There's real cable delay to account for too: a meter
of coax adds roughly 5 ns of one-way propagation delay, and by the time
a signal has gone from a room-temperature rack down through a
meter-plus of cryostat wiring to the mixing chamber and back, tens of
nanoseconds have typically elapsed, latency that matters directly for
anything needing a fast measure-then-react loop, like active qubit
reset or mid-circuit feedback.

## A Quarter Turn, in Nanoseconds

**You want a $\theta=\pi/3$ rotation at Rabi frequency
$\Omega=2\pi\times20\text{ MHz}$. What pulse duration do you ask the
waveform generator for?**

$$
\Omega t = \frac\theta2 = \frac\pi6 \implies t = \frac\pi{6\Omega} = \frac\pi{6(2\pi\times20\times10^6)} \approx 4.17\text{ ns}
$$

Verified directly: `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0,0,Ω,t)` at
this $t$ gives $P_1\approx\sin^2(\pi/6)=0.25$, matching the target
rotation's expected population transfer exactly.

## The Factor of Two That Bites

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing rotation angle θ with the Rabi phase Ωt.** The relationship
is $\Omega t=\theta/2$, not $\Omega t=\theta$. This factor of 2
(already seen in Physical Qubit Platforms' $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$ formula
for a full $\theta=\pi$ rotation) is easy to drop, and dropping it costs
you exactly double the rotation you asked for.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming pulse duration is the only way to control rotation angle.**
Amplitude control (varying $\Omega$ at fixed duration) reaches the same
physics. Real hardware uses whichever is more practical to control
precisely, not one universally "correct" method.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using $\Omega t=\theta/2$, find the pulse duration for a $\theta=\pi/2$
   rotation at $\Omega=2\pi\times20\text{ MHz}$. Then say what fraction
   of shots would come back as 1 at that duration, and why a $\pi/2$
   pulse rather than a $\pi$ pulse is the sensitive place to check
   whether $\Omega$ is right.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \Omega t=\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{\pi}{4} \implies t=\frac{\pi}{4\Omega}=\frac{\pi}{4(2\pi\times20\times10^{6})}=\frac{1}{1.6\times10^{8}}\text{ s}=6.25\text{ ns}.
   $$

   Half the $\pi$-pulse duration at the same drive strength, which is the
   whole content of "duration sets the angle."

   **What comes back:** $P_1=\sin^2(\theta/2)=\sin^2(\pi/4)=0.5$. Checked
   against the engine, `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0, 0, Omega,
   6.25e-9)` returns $0.4999999999999979$. No individual shot returns
   0.5; each returns a 0 or a 1, and the 0.5 is what the fraction
   approaches over many shots. This is a state on the equator of the
   Bloch sphere, and the equator is where the $Z$ measurement carries the
   least information about which state you actually prepared.

   **Why $\pi/2$ is the sensitive check.** Differentiate the population
   with respect to the Rabi phase:

   $$
   \frac{dP_1}{d(\Omega t)} = \frac{d}{d(\Omega t)}\sin^2(\Omega t) = \sin(2\Omega t).
   $$

   At the $\pi/2$ pulse, $\Omega t=\pi/4$, that derivative is exactly 1,
   its largest possible value. At the $\pi$ pulse, $\Omega t=\pi/2$, it
   is 0. So a drive that is a few percent off its assumed $\Omega$ shifts
   the measured population at a $\pi/2$ pulse in direct proportion to the
   error, and shifts it only at *second* order at a $\pi$ pulse. The
   comfortable-looking test, "fire the X gate and check that the qubit
   flipped," is the least informative one available: a 5% error in
   $\Omega$ still leaves $P_1$ above 0.99, and it looks like a working
   gate.

   That is why real calibration does not stop at a single population
   measurement. It either scans the whole curve and fits, as the
   Calibration lesson does, or repeats a nominally identical pulse many
   times so that a small per-pulse error accumulates into something large
   enough to see.

   One practical caveat that this number brings out. A control-grade AWG
   samples at 1 to 2 GS/s, a 0.5 to 1 ns tick, and 6.25 ns does not land
   on either grid. Asking for 6 ns instead gives $\theta=1.508$ rad
   against a target of $1.571$, a 4% angle error and $P_1=0.469$ rather
   than 0.500. Asking for 7 ns is 12% off. The formula gives a real
   number; the instrument gives you the nearest tick.

   </details>

2. If a fixed pulse duration of 5 ns is used, what Rabi frequency
   $\Omega$ would implement a full $\theta=\pi$ (X gate) rotation?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \Omega t=\frac{\theta}{2}=\frac{\pi}{2} \implies \Omega=\frac{\pi}{2t}=\frac{\pi}{2(5\times10^{-9})}\approx3.1416\times10^{8}\ \text{rad/s},
   $$

   which in the frequency form this text quotes is
   $\Omega=2\pi\times50\text{ MHz}$. Verified: the engine at
   $\Omega=3.1416\times10^{8}$ and $t=5$ ns returns
   $P_1=1.0000000000000004$.

   This is question 1 of the Superconducting Qubits practice set run
   backwards, and it should give the same pair of numbers, which it does:
   $\Omega=2\pi\times50$ MHz has $t_\pi=5$ ns, so 5 ns demands
   $2\pi\times50$ MHz. Solving in either direction is the same single
   relation $\Omega t=\theta/2$.

   Two wrong turns.

   The first is answering "$3.14\times10^{8}$" and stopping, or answering
   "50 MHz" and stopping. Those are the same physical quantity written
   two ways, and the factor of $2\pi$ between them is exactly the factor
   that makes gate-time calculations go wrong when a number is copied
   between sources. Say which one you mean.

   The second is the convention trap this lesson's warning box flags. If
   you looked up a device's Rabi frequency in a paper written with
   $P_1=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$ and $t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$, the $\Omega$ that
   gives a 5 ns $\pi$-pulse there is $2\pi\times100$ MHz, twice this
   answer. Neither number is wrong; they label different quantities with
   the same symbol. Convert before substituting.

   </details>

3. Explain why $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$, not $\sin^2(\theta)$, is the
   correct relationship between measured population and rotation angle.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\theta$ is an angle swept on the *Bloch sphere*, and
   amplitudes live on a sphere of half-angles.

   Start from the state. A rotation by $\theta$ about the drive axis,
   applied to $|0\rangle$ at the north pole, produces a state at polar
   angle $\theta$ from the pole, and the canonical form for such a state
   is $\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$. The
   Born rule then gives $P_1=\sin^2(\theta/2)$ directly. Everything
   traces back to that half-angle in the canonical form.

   The half-angle is not a convention. It is there so that *orthogonal*
   states land at *antipodal* points. Two orthogonal states are $90°$
   apart in amplitude space, and the Bloch sphere puts them $180°$ apart,
   so the map from one angle to the other carries a factor of two.
   Without it, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ would not be opposite poles
   and the picture would not work at all.

   The consistency with the drive is then automatic rather than a second
   fact. This lesson's model is $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ and its
   translation rule is $\Omega t=\theta/2$. Substitute the second into
   the first and you get $\sin^2(\theta/2)$, as required. Both statements
   carry the same factor of two, which is why dropping it in one place
   and not the other produces a contradiction rather than a small error.

   Two checks that kill the wrong version instantly, and either is worth
   doing in your head before trusting an algebra step.

   Set $\theta=\pi$, the X gate. The right formula gives
   $\sin^2(\pi/2)=1$: the qubit flipped, which is what an X gate does.
   The wrong formula gives $\sin^2(\pi)=0$: the X gate did nothing.

   Set $\theta=\pi/2$, a quarter turn from the pole to the equator. The
   right formula gives $\sin^2(\pi/4)=0.5$, an equal superposition,
   which is what sitting on the equator means. The wrong formula gives
   $\sin^2(\pi/2)=1$, a complete flip from a half-way rotation.

   A structural version of the same objection: $\sin^2(\theta)$ has
   period $\pi$ in $\theta$, which would say a $180°$ rotation returns
   the qubit to where it started. The correct $\sin^2(\theta/2)$ has
   period $2\pi$, so a full turn returns and a half turn does not.

   </details>

4. Why does this lesson describe pulse-duration and pulse-amplitude
   control as "equivalent," rather than one being strictly better? Then
   name one situation where the two knobs are not interchangeable in
   practice.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because only the *product* $\Omega t$ appears in the rotation angle.
   The condition is $\Omega t=\theta/2$, so $(\Omega,t)$ and
   $(2\Omega,\ t/2)$ produce the identical rotation, and there is no
   third quantity in the expression to distinguish them. Duration is set
   by how long the generator gates the tone on; $\Omega$ is set by the
   drive amplitude, to which it is proportional. Either can be swept with
   the other held fixed, and real hardware picks whichever it can hold
   more steadily.

   Two wrong turns, both of which try to break the symmetry that is not
   there. The first is "amplitude gets there sooner, because $\Omega$
   enters squared." It does not: $\Omega$ and $t$ enter the angle
   symmetrically, in one product. (A squared dependence on $\Omega$ does
   appear elsewhere, in the *generalized* Rabi frequency that governs
   off-resonant driving, which is probably where the intuition comes
   from.) The second is "changing $\Omega$ tilts the rotation axis rather
   than the angle." The axis is set by the drive's *phase*, not its
   amplitude; $\Omega$ sets how fast the state turns about the axis you
   have already chosen.

   **Where they part company.** Once you leave the idealized square-pulse
   two-level model, the two knobs have different side effects, and real
   transmon control almost always fixes the duration and scales the
   amplitude. Three reasons, in rough order of importance.

   *Spectral width.* A pulse's bandwidth is set by its duration, roughly
   $1/t$. Shortening a gate to make it faster widens its spectrum, and
   the transmon's anharmonicity, the gap between the 0→1 and 1→2
   transitions, is exactly what that widening walks into. Amplitude
   scaling at fixed duration leaves the spectrum where it is, so the
   leakage behaviour is the same for every gate on the qubit. Duration
   scaling changes it gate by gate.

   *Resolution.* An AWG's time axis is quantized at its sample tick, 0.5
   to 1 ns. On a 6.25 ns pulse that is granularity of 8 to 16%, and
   question 1 showed the resulting angle error is 4% at the nearest tick.
   The same generator's amplitude is a 14- or 16-bit DAC value, finer by
   orders of magnitude. When the target is a few nanoseconds long,
   amplitude is simply the more precise knob.

   *Scheduling.* Fixed-duration gates make every operation on the chip
   the same length, which makes aligning simultaneous gates on different
   qubits, and reasoning about when a spectator qubit is idle, enormously
   simpler.

   None of that makes the lesson's claim wrong. The equivalence is a
   statement about the target physics, $\Omega t=\theta/2$, and it is
   exact. The asymmetry lives in the instrument and in the levels the
   two-level model leaves out.

   </details>

## Summary

- Resonant microwave driving realizes the two-level Rabi model
  already verified in Approximation Methods: $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$.
- A target rotation angle $\theta$ requires pulse duration
  $t=\theta/(2\Omega)$, a direct, computable translation from abstract
  gate to real control-pulse parameters.
- Duration and amplitude are two equivalent control knobs reaching the
  same target rotation; real hardware picks whichever is more precisely
  controllable.
- Real pulses are shaped, not square: a short square pulse's wide
  sinc-shaped spectrum would drive the transmon's nearby 1→2 transition,
  so hardware uses smooth Gaussian-style envelopes, with DRAG as the
  standard leakage-canceling refinement.
