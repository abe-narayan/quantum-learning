## The Number the Derivation Assumed

Control Electronics derived $t=\theta/(2\Omega)$ assuming $\Omega$ is
already known. In a real lab, it isn't: every device's actual <Term id="rabi-oscillation">Rabi
frequency</Term> depends on the specific hardware (drive line attenuation,
qubit-drive coupling strength, exact resonance frequency), and <Term id="calibration-drift">drifts
over time</Term> with temperature and other conditions. **Calibration** is the
experimental process of measuring these parameters directly on the
device in front of you, instead of trusting a datasheet value.

## Rabi and Ramsey, as Measurements

### The Rabi calibration experiment

The standard technique: apply a drive pulse of *known* duration $t$ (but
*unknown* $\Omega$), measure the resulting population $P_1$ via readout
(previous lesson), and repeat for a range of durations. Since
$P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ (Control Electronics), scanning $t$ traces out
the full oscillation, and the *duration of the first peak* directly
gives $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$, from which $\Omega=\pi/(2t_\pi)$ follows
immediately.

<Callout type="warning">
**Two Rabi conventions coexist in the wild.** This track's convention
is $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ with $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$; most published
work defines $\Omega$ via $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$, making
$t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$. A calibration scan itself is immune (the peak sits
where it sits), but the $\Omega$ you report depends on which formula
you invert. Compare a value extracted with this text's formulas against
one quoted in a paper without converting, and the two will disagree by
a factor of 2 even for a perfectly calibrated device.
</Callout>

### A worked numerical calibration

Suppose the true (unknown to the calibration procedure) Rabi frequency
is $\Omega_{\text{true}}=2\pi\times18\text{ MHz}$. Scanning
`exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0, 0, Omega_true, t)` over
$t\in[0,20]\text{ ns}$ (scoped to just past the expected first peak; a
real calibration scan doesn't need to sweep multiple oscillation
periods) in fine steps and finding the duration of maximum
$P_1$ gives $t_\pi\approx13.89\text{ ns}$. Inverting,
$\Omega_{\text{recovered}}=\pi/(2\times13.89\text{ ns})\approx2\pi\times18.0\text{ MHz}$,
matching the true (hidden) value to within the scan's resolution. This
is the procedure a real lab runs, except with noisy hardware
measurements standing in for this platform's exact engine calls.

### Finding the resonance frequency: the Ramsey experiment

The prediction above exposed the Rabi scan's blind spot: it silently
assumes the drive is already on resonance, and a detuned scan reports
an inflated $\Omega$ with no warning. The qubit's actual resonance
frequency is itself a calibrated quantity, and the standard tool for
measuring it is the **Ramsey experiment**. Apply a $\pi/2$ pulse to put
the qubit on the equator, turn the drive *off* for a free-evolution
time $\tau$, apply a second $\pi/2$ pulse, and measure. If the drive
frequency misses the true qubit frequency by a detuning $\Delta$, the
qubit's phase advances relative to the drive's at exactly that rate
during the wait, so scanning $\tau$ traces out fringes:

$$
P_1(\tau) = \tfrac12\left(1+\cos(\Delta\tau)\right)
$$

The fringes oscillate at the detuning frequency $\Delta$ itself, so
measuring the fringe frequency reveals precisely how far the drive sits
from the qubit's true resonance. Adjust the drive frequency and repeat:
the fringes slow down as $\Delta$ shrinks, and stop entirely on
resonance. Run the Ramsey calibration first, zero the detuning, and
only then run the Rabi scan; that ordering is what protects the scan
from recovering the wrong, inflated generalized Rabi frequency
$\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+(\Delta/2)^2}$ the prediction above warned
about. (That $\Delta/2$ is this track's $P_1=\sin^2(\Omega t)$
convention showing through: in the literature's
$P_1=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$ convention the same quantity is written
$\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}$, with an $\Omega$ twice as large. The box
above is not a pedantic aside.)

### Calibration must be repeated

Because the physical parameters drift (temperature fluctuations,
slow electronic drift, occasional hardware changes), this calibration
procedure isn't a one-time setup step: real devices re-run calibration
routines on a schedule, with a full sweep at least daily on many
superconducting processors and quicker single-parameter checks (a short
Rabi scan, say) run more often than that between the full sweeps. A gate computed from a
stale $\Omega$ value silently implements the *wrong* rotation angle,
with no error message, no crash, just a systematic bias baked into every
gate until the next recalibration catches it.

## The Unglamorous Link

Calibration is the unglamorous but unavoidable link between "the physics
works" (verified throughout this platform's engine) and "the specific
device in the lab today behaves as assumed." Every hardware course
lesson so far implicitly depends on accurate calibration; none of
Control Electronics' formulas are useful with a wrong $\Omega$.

## Inverting a Measured Rabi Peak

**If a Rabi scan's peak is found at $t=20\text{ ns}$ instead of the
expected $13.89\text{ ns}$, what does this imply about the actual
$\Omega$, and by roughly what factor was the assumed value wrong?**

$$
\Omega_{\text{actual}} = \frac{\pi}{2(20\text{ ns})} \approx 2\pi\times12.5\text{ MHz}
$$

Compared to the assumed $2\pi\times18\text{ MHz}$, the actual Rabi
frequency is about $12.5/18\approx0.69\times$ the assumed value (roughly
31% lower). Any gate computed using the wrong, assumed $\Omega$ would
implement a systematically incorrect rotation angle until recalibrated.

## Calibrating Once and Trusting It

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating calibration as a one-time setup step.** Real hardware drifts;
production systems recalibrate routinely, not just once when the device
is first built.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a miscalibrated gate produces an obvious error.** It doesn't.
It silently implements the wrong rotation angle, which can look like a
subtle, hard-to-diagnose algorithm bug rather than an obviously "broken"
gate.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Explain, in your own words, why scanning pulse duration and finding
   the first population peak recovers $\Omega$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because on resonance the curve's *shape* is already known and
   $\Omega$ is the only thing left to fix: the horizontal scale.

   $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$ has exactly one free parameter, and it
   enters only as a stretch of the time axis. Nothing about the curve's
   form is in question. So the whole calibration reduces to identifying
   one landmark on the curve and reading off where it fell. The first
   maximum is the landmark of choice: it is the first $t$ at which
   $\Omega t=\pi/2$, so

   $$
   t_\pi = \frac{\pi}{2\Omega} \implies \Omega = \frac{\pi}{2t_\pi}.
   $$

   Three reasons the *first peak* rather than any other feature.

   It is unambiguous. Later maxima are equally valid landmarks but
   require knowing how many periods you have crossed, and a scan that
   sweeps far enough to see several peaks costs more measurement time for
   no extra information.

   It is the largest signal. $P_1$ runs from 0 to 1, and the peak is the
   point furthest from the baseline, so it is where the measurement sits
   best above readout noise.

   It does not require the readout to be absolutely calibrated. This one
   matters more than it looks. To read $\Omega$ off a single measured
   *value* of $P_1$ you would need to know exactly what signal level
   corresponds to $P_1=1$, which is itself a separate calibration you may
   not yet have. The *position* of a maximum along the time axis is
   unchanged by any scaling or offset of the vertical axis. The scan
   deliberately extracts an $x$-coordinate, not a $y$-value.

   That last point also names the tempting wrong turn: fire a pulse of
   some convenient duration, measure $P_1$, and solve $\sin^2(\Omega
   t)=P_1$ for $\Omega$. Three things go wrong. The inverse is
   multivalued, since $\Omega t$ and $\pi-\Omega t$ and every $+n\pi$
   shift give the same population. It needs the absolute readout scale
   just discussed. And near the peak the population is stationary in
   $\Omega t$, so a measurement taken anywhere close to the peak carries
   almost no information about $\Omega$ at all.

   One blind spot to carry forward, since the lesson makes it the reason
   Ramsey exists. The scan assumes the drive is already on resonance. Run
   it detuned and the system oscillates at the *generalized* Rabi
   frequency $\Omega_R=\sqrt{\Omega^2+(\Delta/2)^2}$, which is always
   larger than $\Omega$, so the peak arrives early and inverting
   $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$ reports an inflated value. With $\Delta=\Omega$
   on a true $2\pi\times18$ MHz drive, the engine puts the peak at 12.42
   ns instead of 13.89 and the scan would report $2\pi\times20.1$ MHz.
   There is a visible tell, though: that peak only reaches $P_1=0.80$
   instead of 1.00. A first peak that does not come close to full
   population transfer is the signature of a detuned scan, and the cure
   is to run Ramsey first and zero $\Delta$.

   </details>

2. If a calibration scan finds $t_\pi=10\text{ ns}$, what is the
   corresponding $\Omega$ (in the form $2\pi\times f$, solving for $f$
   in Hz)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \Omega = \frac{\pi}{2t_\pi} = \frac{\pi}{2(10\times10^{-9})} \approx 1.5708\times10^{8}\ \text{rad/s}.
   $$

   To put that in the requested form, divide by $2\pi$:

   $$
   f = \frac{\Omega}{2\pi} = \frac{1}{4t_\pi} = \frac{1}{4\times10\times10^{-9}} = 2.5\times10^{7}\ \text{Hz} = 25\text{ MHz},
   $$

   so $\Omega=2\pi\times25\text{ MHz}$. Verified: the engine at this
   $\Omega$ and $t=10$ ns returns $P_1=1.0000000000000004$, a full
   $\pi$-pulse, which is what a scan peak at 10 ns means. It is also
   exactly the device in the Superconducting Qubits worked example, now
   arrived at from the measurement rather than assumed.

   The shortcut worth keeping is the middle line: $f=1/(4t_\pi)$ in this
   text's convention. Everything with a $\pi$ in it cancels, which is
   both convenient and a useful check that you inverted the right
   formula.

   Two wrong turns.

   Reporting "$1.57\times10^{8}$" when the question asked for the form
   $2\pi\times f$. Those are the same quantity, and the factor of $2\pi$
   between rad/s and Hz is the one that goes missing when a
   number is moved between a datasheet and a formula. Say which you mean.

   And the convention trap. In the more common literature convention,
   where $t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$, the same 10 ns peak would be reported as
   $\Omega=2\pi\times50$ MHz. Both numbers describe the identical
   physical device with the identical measured peak; they label different
   quantities with the same symbol. The scan itself is immune, since the
   peak sits where it sits, but the number you write down is not.

   </details>

3. Why must calibration be repeated periodically, rather than performed
   once when a device is built?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the quantities being calibrated are not device constants. They
   drift, on timescales of hours.

   What drifts, and why:

   **The qubit's own frequency.** In a superconducting device the
   dominant cause is two-level-system defects in the amorphous oxides in
   and around the junction. These couple to the qubit and move around
   over hours, which is the same mechanism that makes $T_1$ a median over
   a chip and a time window rather than a fixed number. Stray magnetic
   flux does the same job for flux-tunable qubits.

   **The delivered drive strength.** $\Omega$ depends on how much
   microwave power actually arrives at the qubit, which depends on the
   whole chain: amplifier gain, mixer offsets and IQ imbalance, cable
   loss, and the attenuators at each fridge stage. Room-temperature
   electronics drift with ambient temperature; the fridge's own stage
   temperatures move as its heat load changes.

   **The resonance the drive is compared against.** Since $\Omega$ is
   only recoverable correctly on resonance, any drift in the qubit
   frequency also corrupts the Rabi calibration, even if nothing about
   the drive changed.

   In practice that means a full parameter sweep at least daily on many
   superconducting processors, with quicker single-parameter checks, a
   short Rabi scan for instance, run more frequently between the full
   sweeps.

   The reason this cannot be handled by exception rather than by schedule
   is the point the lesson opens on: a stale $\Omega$ does not announce
   itself. There is no error, no crash, and no obviously broken gate,
   just a rotation angle that is quietly wrong. The calibration schedule
   *is* the alarm. Nothing else in the stack is watching.

   The wrong turn is treating drift as decoherence's cousin and
   concluding that better isolation would fix it. Drift and decoherence
   are different categories, which is exactly the distinction the Sources
   of Noise lesson draws next: drift is a systematic shift in a classical
   control parameter, undone completely by remeasuring it, while
   decoherence is information lost to an environment and no amount of
   retuning brings it back.

   </details>

4. Explain why a miscalibrated $\Omega$ produces a systematic (not
   random) error, and why this makes it especially important to catch
   via a deliberate calibration routine.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Why systematic.** The pulse duration is computed once, from the
   assumed $\Omega$, and then used for every gate. If the true drive
   strength is $\Omega_{\text{true}}$ and the assumed one is
   $\Omega_{\text{assumed}}$, every requested angle comes out scaled by
   the same factor:

   $$
   \theta_{\text{actual}} = \frac{\Omega_{\text{true}}}{\Omega_{\text{assumed}}}\,\theta_{\text{requested}}.
   $$

   That is a deterministic, repeatable, same-direction offset, not a
   fluctuation. Run the identical circuit a thousand times and it is
   wrong the same way a thousand times.

   **Why that is worse than it sounds, quantitatively.** Averaging is the
   standard defence against error, and it does nothing here, because
   averaging removes zero-mean noise and this has a mean. Worse, a
   coherent error *accumulates in amplitude* rather than in probability.
   Stochastic errors add up roughly linearly in the number of gates;
   coherent ones add up in the rotation angle, and infidelity goes as the
   square of that accumulated angle.

   The numbers make the point better than the scaling law does. Take a
   drive miscalibrated by 1%, so each $\pi$-pulse over-rotates to
   $1.01\pi$. A single gate has $P_1=0.999753$, an infidelity of
   $2.5\times10^{-4}$, which on any reasonable test looks like a working
   gate. Now apply it repeatedly, where a perfect X gate would return the
   qubit to $|0\rangle$ after an even number of applications:

   after 10 gates, $P_1=0.024$ where it should be 0, still small;

   after 50 gates, $P_1=0.500$, which is no information at all;

   after 100 gates, $P_1=1.000$ where it should be 0, the exact opposite
   of the right answer.

   A 0.025% per-gate error has become a complete inversion in a hundred
   gates. That is what "systematic" costs.

   **Why a deliberate routine is the answer.** Three reasons stack.

   It is silent. There is no crash and no obviously broken gate; the
   symptom is a plausible-looking wrong answer, which reads as an
   algorithm bug rather than a hardware fault and can burn a great deal
   of debugging time in the wrong place.

   The obvious spot check does not catch it. The natural test, "fire an X
   gate and confirm the qubit flipped," is exactly the least sensitive
   measurement available: at the $\pi$ pulse the population is stationary
   in $\Omega t$, so a small $\Omega$ error moves $P_1$ only at second
   order. The 0.999753 above is that insensitivity in action. Sensitivity
   is maximal at a $\pi/2$ pulse, or is obtained by repeating a pulse
   many times so the error amplifies into view, which is precisely what
   the 100-gate sequence above is.

   And it is completely fixable, unlike the alternative. A systematic
   error is one measurement away from being gone. Remeasure $\Omega$,
   recompute the durations, and the bias disappears with no hardware
   change and no error-correction overhead. Decoherence offers no such
   deal. Of everything on a device's error budget, this is the cheapest
   thing to repair and the most expensive thing to ignore.

   One caveat worth knowing. Randomized benchmarking, the standard way of
   quoting a gate fidelity, applies random gate sequences, and randomness
   partly scrambles a coherent error into an effective stochastic
   average. So a benchmarked error rate can look reassuring while a
   structured circuit, which repeats the same gate in the same direction,
   suffers the quadratic accumulation shown above. A good RB number is
   not by itself evidence that calibration is current.

   </details>

## Summary

- Rabi calibration finds a device's actual $\Omega$ experimentally: scan
  pulse duration, locate the first population-transfer peak, invert
  $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$.
- Ramsey calibration finds the resonance frequency: fringes from free
  evolution oscillate at the detuning $\Delta$, so the fringe frequency
  measures how far the drive sits from the qubit's true frequency, and
  zeroing it protects the Rabi scan from recovering an inflated
  $\Omega$.
- Demonstrated numerically: recovering a hidden $\Omega=2\pi\times18\text{ MHz}$
  to within scan resolution purely from scanned
  `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability` data.
- Calibration must be repeated regularly, since real hardware parameters
  drift. A miscalibrated device fails silently (wrong rotation angle),
  not with an obvious error.
