## Motivation

Measurement and Probability (Quantum Computing pillar) treats
measurement as an instantaneous, ideal projection. Real qubit readout is
neither instantaneous nor ideal: it takes real time, involves real
noise, and has a real, measurable error rate. This lesson covers how
readout actually works in hardware, and honestly quantifies how far it
falls short of the textbook ideal.

## Engineering Development

### Dispersive readout: measure the resonator, not the qubit

Directly probing a qubit's energy levels risks disturbing (or fully
collapsing) the state in an uncontrolled way. **Dispersive readout**
sidesteps this: the qubit is coupled to a separate microwave resonator,
engineered so that the resonator's own resonant frequency shifts
slightly depending on whether the qubit is in $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$
(a "dispersive," off-resonant coupling, strong enough to shift the
resonator's frequency, weak enough not to directly drive qubit
transitions). Sending a probe tone into the resonator and measuring the
reflected/transmitted signal's phase or amplitude reveals which
frequency the resonator sat at, and therefore, indirectly, which state
the qubit was in, without directly bombarding the qubit itself.

### Readout is probabilistic, not perfect

Real readout doesn't cleanly return "0" or "1." It returns an analog
signal (voltage, phase) that must be classified into one of two bins.
Noise in this signal means the classification is imperfect: a
qubit truly in $|0\rangle$ occasionally gets misclassified as "1," and
vice versa. **Readout fidelity** quantifies this: the probability the
reported classical bit matches the qubit's actual (pre-measurement)
state. Representative current values sit in the 95-99% range depending
on platform and integration time, good, but a real, nonzero error
source, not the textbook's perfect projective measurement.

### Readout error joins the broader error budget

Readout error compounds with gate errors and decoherence
(Physical Qubit Platforms' coherence-time discussion, and the Noise,
Decoherence & Scaling course) into the device's overall error
budget. A computation with perfect gates but 97% readout fidelity still
has a real 3% chance of reporting the wrong final answer on any single
run, motivating the standard practice of running a circuit many times
and taking a statistical distribution over outcomes, rather than trusting
any single shot.

## Physical Interpretation

Dispersive readout is a clean illustration of a broader hardware theme:
almost nothing in real quantum hardware is the clean, idealized
operation described in an introductory circuits course. Every
operation (gates, readout, even the "instantaneous" measurement
postulate) has a real, engineered, imperfect physical implementation
underneath it, with its own error characteristics worth knowing
explicitly rather than assuming away.

## Worked Example

**A circuit is run 1000 times on hardware with 97% readout fidelity (and
otherwise-perfect gates and no decoherence, for this idealized
example). Before computing it, guess roughly how many of the 1000
reported outcomes will be readout errors. Then check the number below.**

$$
1000 \times (1-0.97) = 1000\times0.03 = 30
$$

About 30 of the 1000 shots are expected to report the wrong bit purely
from readout error, even though the underlying quantum state and gates
were assumed perfect in this idealized example. This is exactly why real
experiments report statistics over many shots rather than trusting a
single run.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming readout directly measures the qubit.** Dispersive readout
measures a coupled RESONATOR's frequency shift, an indirect scheme,
specifically designed to avoid the disturbance a direct qubit
measurement would risk.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating readout fidelity as if it were 100%.** As the worked example
shows, even a "good" 97% fidelity produces a real, non-negligible error
rate on any single measurement, an important, quantifiable
contribution to a device's overall error budget, not a rounding
error to ignore.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Dispersive readout measures a qubit indirectly, via a coupled
  resonator's state-dependent frequency shift, not a direct
  measurement of the qubit itself.
- Readout fidelity (representatively 95-99%) is a real, nonzero error
  source, a probabilistic classification problem, not the textbook's
  perfect projective measurement.
- Readout error joins gate error and decoherence in a device's overall
  error budget, motivating multi-shot statistics over any single run.
