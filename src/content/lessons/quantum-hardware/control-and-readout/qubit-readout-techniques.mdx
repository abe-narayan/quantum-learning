## Measurement That Takes Time and Makes Mistakes

Measurement and Probability (Quantum Computing track) treats
measurement as an instantaneous, ideal projection. Real qubit readout is
neither instantaneous nor ideal: it takes a few hundred nanoseconds to a
couple of microseconds, involves real noise, and has a measurable
error rate. So what is actually happening during those hundreds of
nanoseconds, and how far does what you get back sit from the clean
projection the postulate promises?

## Dispersive Readout, and Its Error Budget

### Dispersive readout: measure the resonator, not the qubit

Directly probing a qubit's energy levels risks disturbing (or fully
<Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapsing</Term>) the state in an uncontrolled way. **Dispersive readout**
sidesteps this: the qubit is coupled to a separate microwave resonator,
engineered so that the resonator's own resonant frequency shifts
slightly depending on whether the qubit is in $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$
(a "dispersive," off-resonant coupling, strong enough to shift the
resonator's frequency, weak enough not to directly drive qubit
transitions). Sending a probe tone into the resonator and measuring the
reflected/transmitted signal's phase or amplitude reveals which
frequency the resonator sat at, and therefore, indirectly, which state
the qubit was in, without directly bombarding the qubit itself.

<DispersiveReadoutDiagram ariaLabel="Dispersive readout: the qubit couples only dispersively to a readout resonator, which is itself coupled to a feedline carrying the probe tone to and from an amplifier chain; the resonator, not the qubit, sits on the measured signal path" />

The feedline, the thing your measurement instrument is connected
to, only ever touches the resonator. The qubit sits one coupling step
further away, its state readable only through the effect it has on the
resonator's frequency, never sampled directly.

### Readout is probabilistic, not perfect

Real readout doesn't cleanly return "0" or "1." It returns an analog
signal (voltage, phase) that must be classified into one of two bins.
Noise in this signal means the classification is imperfect: a
qubit actually in $|0\rangle$ occasionally gets misclassified as "1," and
vice versa. **Readout fidelity** quantifies this: the probability the
reported classical bit matches the qubit's actual (pre-measurement)
state. Take 95-99% as the representative band this lesson computes with
rather than as a current specification: it is a mid-2020s figure, it
varies with platform and integration time, and the best devices already
sit at the top of it. A <Term id="shot-noise-standard-error">single-shot</Term> measurement typically
integrates the reflected signal for a few hundred nanoseconds up to a
couple of microseconds, longer integration averages out more noise and
buys higher fidelity, but it also holds the qubit in a measured state
longer. That is a direct speed-versus-accuracy tradeoff, and nothing
like the textbook's instantaneous, perfect projective measurement.

Where does that error come from? Every single shot lands
somewhere on the IQ plane, the 2D plane of the demodulated signal's
in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components, as one point drawn from a
noisy cloud centered near the "measured $|0\rangle$" location or the
"measured $|1\rangle$" location.

A threshold splits the IQ plane into two bins. Noise is what gives each
cloud its spread, and it's that spread, not the classifier, that lets
some $|0\rangle$ shots land on the $|1\rangle$ side of the boundary (and
vice versa): the single-shot physical origin of the error rate the bar
chart below reports as a shot count.

### Readout error joins the broader error budget

Readout error compounds with <Term id="gate-fidelity">gate errors</Term> and decoherence
(Physical Qubit Platforms' coherence-time discussion, and the Noise,
Decoherence & Scaling course) into the device's overall error
budget. A computation with perfect gates but 97% readout fidelity still
has a real 3% chance of reporting the wrong final answer on any single
run. That is why the standard practice is to run a circuit many times
and read the statistical distribution over outcomes; no single shot is
worth trusting on its own.

## Measuring the Proxy, Not the Thing

Dispersive readout is a clean illustration of a broader hardware theme:
almost nothing in real quantum hardware is the clean, idealized
operation described in an introductory circuits course. Every
operation (gates, readout, even the "instantaneous" measurement
postulate) has an imperfect physical implementation
underneath it, with its own error characteristics that you are better
off knowing explicitly than assuming away.

## What 97 Percent Fidelity Costs a Circuit

**A circuit is run 1000 times on hardware with 97% readout fidelity (and
otherwise-perfect gates and no decoherence, for this idealized
example). Before computing it, guess roughly how many of the 1000
reported outcomes will be readout errors. Then check the number below.**

$$
1000 \times (1-0.97) = 1000\times0.03 = 30
$$

About 30 of the 1000 shots are expected to report the wrong bit purely
from readout error, even though the underlying quantum state and gates
were assumed perfect in this idealized example. This is why real
experiments report statistics over many shots.

## Treating Readout as Free and Exact

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming readout directly measures the qubit.** Dispersive readout
measures a coupled RESONATOR's frequency shift, an indirect scheme,
specifically designed to avoid the disturbance a direct qubit
measurement would risk.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating readout fidelity as if it were 100%.** As the worked example
shows, even a "good" 97% fidelity produces a non-negligible error
rate on any single measurement, a quantifiable contribution to a
device's overall error budget, not a rounding error to ignore.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does dispersive readout measure a resonator instead of the qubit
   directly? What risk does this avoid?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the thing you have to connect a measuring instrument to is,
   necessarily, strongly coupled to the outside world, and you do not
   want that thing to be the qubit.

   Probing a qubit's energy levels directly means sending power at or
   near its own transition frequency. That is the same tone a gate is
   made of, so the probe drives the very transition it is trying to read.
   Worse, it puts the qubit on the measurement line permanently: a cold,
   lossy, amplifier-terminated path out of the fridge is an excellent
   decoherence channel, and once the qubit is coupled to it, it is
   coupled to it all the time, not only while you are measuring.

   Dispersive readout puts a resonator in between. The coupling is
   deliberately far off resonance, strong enough that the resonator's
   frequency shifts by a measurable amount depending on whether the qubit
   sits in $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, weak enough that no energy is
   exchanged between the two. The measured signal is a probe tone
   reflected off the *resonator*, and the qubit's state is inferred from
   which frequency the resonator was at. Your instrument touches the
   resonator; the qubit sits one coupling step away.

   The property that makes this work is worth naming, because it is what
   turns a clever trick into a usable measurement. In the dispersive
   regime the effective interaction is a state-dependent frequency shift,
   which commutes with the qubit's own $Z$ operator. An interaction that
   commutes with $Z$ cannot flip the qubit between $|0\rangle$ and
   $|1\rangle$. So the measurement distinguishes the two states without
   inducing transitions between them, and the qubit is left in the state
   the measurement reported. That is what makes mid-circuit measurement,
   conditional feedback and error-correction syndrome extraction possible
   at all: each of those needs the qubit to still be usable afterwards.

   The wrong turn is to name the avoided risk as "collapse." Collapse is
   not the risk; it is the point. A measurement is supposed to project
   the state. The risk is *uncontrolled* disturbance: a probe that drives
   transitions rather than reading them, and a permanent strong coupling
   to a lossy line. The lesson's phrase "disturbing (or fully collapsing)
   the state in an uncontrolled way" turns on that last clause.

   One honest residual, since the isolation is not total. The qubit is
   coupled to the resonator, the resonator is coupled to the feedline,
   and so the qubit has a path, second-hand, to decay out through the
   readout circuit. That is the Purcell effect, and it sets a ceiling on
   $T_1$ that gets worse the more strongly you couple the resonator to
   the line for signal. Real devices insert a Purcell filter, a circuit
   transparent at the readout frequency and opaque at the qubit's, to
   break that path while keeping the signal. The resonator buys enormous
   isolation, not perfect isolation.

   </details>

2. For 1000 shots at 99% readout fidelity, roughly how many are expected
   to be readout errors? Compare to the worked example's 97% case, then
   say what that same per-qubit fidelity implies for a 10-qubit register
   read out shot by shot.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   1000\times(1-0.99) = 1000\times0.01 = 10 \text{ shots}.
   $$

   Against 30 at 97%. Three times fewer errors, which is worth pausing
   on, because "97% to 99%" sounds like a two-point improvement and is in
   fact a factor-of-three reduction. The quantity that governs the error
   count is the *infidelity* $1-F$, not the fidelity, and infidelity went
   from 0.03 to 0.01. The same reading applies at the other end of the
   lesson's stated range: 95% fidelity gives 50 errors per 1000, five
   times worse than 99%, from a number that looks only four points
   lower.

   The wrong turn is stopping at "10 out of 1000, so 99% is basically
   fine." It is not fine on a real register, because every measured qubit
   pays the error separately. Ten qubits each read out at 99% give

   $$
   0.99^{10} \approx 0.904,
   $$

   so roughly one shot in ten comes back with at least one wrong bit in
   the string. At 97% the same register gives $0.97^{10}\approx0.737$,
   and better than one shot in four is corrupted somewhere. A per-qubit
   fidelity is not a per-outcome fidelity, and the gap between them grows
   with the width of the register.

   One further refinement worth carrying, because it matters as soon as
   you try to correct for readout error. The two error directions are not
   equal in practice. A qubit prepared in $|1\rangle$ has a few hundred
   nanoseconds to a couple of microseconds of readout integration during
   which it can relax toward $|0\rangle$, while a qubit in $|0\rangle$
   has no comparable route upward. So the $|1\rangle\to$ "0" error is
   typically the larger one, and a device's readout confusion matrix
   comes out asymmetric. That asymmetry is exactly what makes the
   confusion-matrix inversion in the Quantum Error Mitigation lesson
   worth doing, rather than just subtracting a flat error rate.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why running a circuit many times (rather than
   once) is standard practice, connecting directly to readout fidelity.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a single shot returns one classical bit that has a real
   $(1-F)$ chance of being wrong, and no way to tell whether it was, so
   the thing worth reporting is a distribution estimated over many shots
   rather than any individual outcome.

   Two separate reasons stack up behind that sentence, and it is worth
   keeping them apart because they behave differently.

   **The intrinsic one.** A quantum circuit generally does not have a
   deterministic answer. The Born rule specifies a probability
   distribution over outcomes, and a single sample cannot estimate a
   distribution. This would be true on perfect hardware with perfect
   readout.

   **The instrumental one.** On top of that, readout misclassifies some
   shots. At 97% fidelity, 30 of 1000 reported bits are wrong even when
   the underlying state and every gate were exactly right.

   Now the part that is easy to get wrong, and it matters. Repetition
   fixes the first problem cleanly, because shot noise averages away:
   the standard error of an estimated probability shrinks as
   $1/\sqrt{n}$. Repetition does *not* fix the second problem, because
   readout error is not zero-mean noise. It is a systematic bias, and an
   asymmetric one, so more shots estimate the *biased* distribution more
   and more precisely. A million shots on a device with an asymmetric
   confusion matrix converges confidently to the wrong answer.

   Removing that bias takes a different tool: measure the confusion
   matrix by preparing known states, then invert it to recover the true
   distribution from the measured one. That is readout-error mitigation,
   and it is a separate step from taking more shots. Shots buy precision;
   mitigation buys accuracy, and they are not substitutes.

   </details>

4. Readout fidelity and gate fidelity are both sources of error. Are
   they the same kind of error, or different? (Hint: think about WHEN
   each error occurs relative to the actual quantum state.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Different kinds, and the timing is the whole distinction.

   **A gate error happens to the quantum state, while it is still
   quantum.** A miscalibrated rotation or a noisy pulse leaves the
   register in the wrong state. Everything downstream then operates on
   that wrong state, so the error propagates: a small over-rotation early
   in a circuit can be carried, amplified, and spread across qubits by
   the entangling gates that follow.

   **A readout error happens after the state is gone.** The projection
   already occurred, the qubit really was in $|0\rangle$, and what went
   wrong is that a noisy analog signal landed on the wrong side of a
   threshold on the IQ plane. It is a classical misclassification of a
   correct quantum result.

   Three consequences follow, and each of them is a practical difference
   rather than a philosophical one.

   **Propagation.** Readout error does not propagate. It corrupts one
   reported bit and there are no subsequent gates for it to corrupt.
   Gate error is the opposite: its damage is done to the object the rest
   of the circuit is about to use.

   **Repair.** Readout error is a stochastic channel on classical bits
   with a matrix you can measure directly, by preparing $|0\rangle$ and
   $|1\rangle$ and counting misclassifications, and then invert after the
   fact. Gate error cannot be undone this way. Once quantum information
   has leaked into an environment, no classical post-processing recovers
   it; you either prevent the error by calibrating, or correct it inside
   the circuit with an error-correcting code.

   **How each enters the error budget.** Gate error compounds with
   circuit *depth*, roughly $p^N$ over $N$ sequential gates. Readout
   error is paid once per measured qubit per shot, so it scales with
   register *width*, as question 2's $0.99^{10}$ showed. A deep circuit
   on a few qubits is gate-limited; a shallow circuit reading out many
   qubits is readout-limited. Collapsing both into one "device fidelity"
   number hides which of the two you are actually fighting.

   The clean split does blur at one specific place, and pretending
   otherwise would be dishonest. Readout integration takes hundreds of
   nanoseconds to a couple of microseconds, and during that window the
   qubit can relax through $T_1$. That is a genuine change to the quantum
   state, happening inside the measurement, and it gets reported as a
   readout error. It is also, as question 2 noted, why the confusion
   matrix comes out asymmetric. The boundary between "error to the state"
   and "error in classifying the state" is a real boundary, but the
   measurement window straddles it.

   </details>

## Summary

- Dispersive readout measures a qubit indirectly, via a coupled
  resonator's state-dependent frequency shift, not a direct
  measurement of the qubit itself.
- Readout fidelity (representatively 95-99%) is a nonzero error
  source: a probabilistic classification problem sitting exactly where
  the textbook puts a perfect projective measurement.
- Readout error joins gate error and decoherence in a device's overall
  error budget, motivating multi-shot statistics over any single run.
