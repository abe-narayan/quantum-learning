## Why Millikelvin, and Not Merely Cold

Physical Qubit Platforms mentioned superconducting and <Term id="spin-qubits">spin qubits</Term>
operate at millikelvin temperatures almost in passing. That number is
worth stopping on. This lesson works out why a chip needs to sit
that cold: not "cold is generally good for delicate things," but a
specific, computable physical requirement.

## Thermal Occupation, Computed

### Thermal photon occupation

A qubit's energy-level structure is, to good approximation near its
lowest transition, a <Term id="quantum-harmonic-oscillator">harmonic mode</Term> at some frequency $\omega$
(Superconducting Qubits' anharmonic oscillator, right near its 0-1 gap).
In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the mean number of thermal
photons occupying that mode follows the **Bose-Einstein distribution**:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "n", symbol: "n̄", gloss: "The mean number of thermal photons occupying the qubit's mode." },
    { id: "hw", symbol: "ħω", gloss: "The qubit's transition energy: Planck's constant times its frequency." },
    { id: "kT", symbol: "k_BT", gloss: "The thermal energy scale at temperature T: Boltzmann's constant times T." },
  ]}
>
$$
\bar n = \frac1{\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1}
$$
</EquationReveal>

$\bar n\ll1$ means the mode is almost always found in its ground state,
the reliable, well-defined starting point a qubit computation needs. $\bar n\gtrsim1$ means the mode is thermally populated: even with
no computation happening yet, thermal noise has already randomized which
level the system sits in, before any gate is applied.

### The numbers, computed directly

For a representative 5 GHz superconducting qubit frequency,
`thermalPhotonOccupation` gives:

| Temperature | $\bar n$ |
|---|---|
| 15 mK (dilution fridge base) | $\approx1.1\times10^{-7}$ |
| 100 mK | $\approx0.10$ |
| 4 K (simple cryocooler) | $\approx16.2$ |
| 300 K (room temperature) | $\approx1250$ |

Two things jump out of this table. Room temperature is wildly unusable
($\bar n\sim1000$, thousands of thermal photons per mode). More
subtly, **4 K, right around liquid helium's own boiling point (4.2 K)
and cold enough to freeze every other element solid, is still nowhere
near cold enough** ($\bar n\approx16$, over an order of magnitude too
warm). That's why dilution refrigerators push further, to the
$10$-$20$ mK range, instead of stopping at the comparatively
easy-to-reach 4 K stage.

### Staged cooling

A dilution refrigerator cools in stages: roughly room temperature, then
two stages of a mechanical **pulse-tube cryocooler**, a first stage at
roughly $50$ K anchoring the outer radiation shield, and a second at
$\sim4$ K, and finally the dilution stage itself (a different
cooling mechanism, based on the physics of mixing two
isotopes of helium) reaching the millikelvin range. Modern "dry"
fridges, including every machine photographed in this course, cool
those upper stages this way, with no cryogenic liquids to refill;
older "wet" systems used a liquid-nitrogen bath at $77$ K for the same
radiation-shield job, which is why many diagrams (including the one
below) still carry that label. Each stage is progressively harder to build and more expensive
to operate, a real engineering reason systems don't just try to jump
straight to millikelvin in one step.

The dilution stage itself isn't one step either: it further splits into a
**still** ($\sim0.7$-$1$ K, where the helium mixture is distilled and the
first heavy filtering of control lines happens), a **cold plate**
($\sim100$ mK, the same 100 mK checkpoint already in the table above,
with $\bar n\approx0.10$), and finally the **mixing chamber** ($10$-$20$
mK, matching this lesson's $15$ mK dilution-fridge-base figure), where the
qubit chip itself is mounted:

<DilutionRefrigeratorDiagram ariaLabel="Vertical cross-section of a dilution refrigerator's cooling stages, from 300 K room temperature down through the radiation shield (labeled 77 K here; around 50 K in a modern dry fridge), the 4 K pulse-tube stage, the still, the cold plate, and the 10-20 mK mixing chamber where the qubit chip sits" />

Each plate is progressively colder and physically smaller, nested inside
the one above it; hardware gets placed at whichever stage its own
temperature tolerance allows, cheapest (room temperature) components as
far out as possible, and only the qubit chip itself at the very
coldest, hardest-to-reach stage.

What removes heat at the bottom is a form of evaporative
cooling, with a twist: the "evaporation" happens into another liquid.
Cooled far enough, a mixture of the two helium isotopes separates into
two liquid phases, a concentrated ³He-rich phase floating on a dilute
phase that is mostly ⁴He with a small dissolved fraction of ³He. In
the **mixing chamber**, the boundary between the two phases sits right
where the qubit chip's plate is anchored, and every ³He atom that
crosses from the concentrated phase into the dilute one absorbs heat,
just as a molecule evaporating from a liquid into vapor does. The
dilute phase keeps accepting ³He even in the
millikelvin range, so this evaporation-into-a-liquid keeps pumping
heat where ordinary evaporation would have long since stalled. The
**still** is what keeps the cycle going: warmed to its
$\sim0.7$-$1$ K operating point, it preferentially boils ³He (the more
volatile isotope) out of the dilute phase, and room-temperature pumps
circulate that ³He back around the loop, down through the **cold
plate**'s pre-cooling stage, to re-condense and cross the phase
boundary again.

## The Gap Between 4 K and 15 mK

The 4 K vs. 15 mK gap in the table above is the single most important
number in this lesson: it's not a matter of "colder is nicer," but a
specific, computed threshold. $\bar n$ crosses from safely below 1 to
well above 1 somewhere in that gap, which is why the last,
hardest cooling stage (the dilution refrigerator itself, not just a
simpler mechanical cryocooler) is unavoidable for this class of qubit.

## The Temperature Where One Photon Arrives

**At what rough temperature does $\bar n=1$ for a 5 GHz qubit?**

Setting $\bar n=1$: $\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)=2$, so
$T=\hbar\omega/(k_B\ln2)$. With $\omega=2\pi\times5\times10^9\text{ Hz}$:

$$
T = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})(2\pi\times5\times10^9)}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(\ln2)} \approx 0.35\text{ K} = 350\text{ mK}
$$

Consistent with the table: at $100$ mK, $\bar n\approx0.10$ (below 1);
by $4$ K, $\bar n\approx16$ (well above 1). The crossover sits
somewhere in between, matching this estimate.

## Assuming Liquid Helium Is Cold Enough

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking any cryogenic temperature is "cold enough."** As the table
shows, 4 K, cold enough to freeze nearly every other gas solid, still
gives $\bar n\approx16$, more than an order of magnitude too warm for
reliable qubit operation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming higher qubit frequency always makes thermal noise worse.**
The formula shows the opposite: HIGHER $\omega$ at fixed $T$ gives a
SMALLER $\bar n$ (larger $\hbar\omega/k_BT$ makes the exponential
larger). This is part of why qubit designs favor GHz-scale transition
frequencies rather than much lower ones.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $\bar n$ for a 5 GHz qubit at 50 mK, and state whether this is
   "safe" ($\bar n\ll1$) by this lesson's standard.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \frac{\hbar\omega}{k_BT} = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})(2\pi\times5\times10^{9})}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(0.05)} \approx 4.80, \qquad \bar n = \frac{1}{e^{4.80}-1} \approx 8.3\times10^{-3}.
   $$

   Checked against the engine, `thermalPhotonOccupation(5e9, 0.05)`
   returns $0.008304$. So yes, it clears the lesson's bar: $\bar n$ is
   about $1/120$, comfortably below 1, and 50 mK sits well on the cold
   side of the 350 mK crossover.

   It is worth reading the number rather than just the verdict, because
   "safe" hides a lot of range. The dilution fridge base of 15 mK gives
   $\bar n\approx1.1\times10^{-7}$. Moving from 15 mK to 50 mK, barely a
   factor of three in temperature, costs almost five orders of magnitude
   in occupation, because $\bar n$ depends on temperature through
   $e^{-\hbar\omega/k_BT}$ and an exponential of a reciprocal moves
   violently.

   And $8.3\times10^{-3}$ is not negligible for every purpose. To leading
   order the residual thermal excited-state population of the qubit is
   $\bar n$ itself, so about 0.8% of the time the chip starts a circuit
   in $|1\rangle$ rather than $|0\rangle$, before a single gate has run.
   Set that against a device targeting 99.9% gate fidelity and the
   state-preparation error alone is eight times the per-gate error
   budget. "Safe" by the $\bar n\ll1$ criterion and "good enough for a
   deep circuit" are different questions, and this is why fridges are
   specified at 10 to 20 mK rather than at the loosest temperature that
   technically passes.

   The wrong turn to avoid is reaching for a linear intuition: 50 mK is
   about three times 15 mK, so surely $\bar n$ is about three times
   worse. It is roughly 74,000 times worse. Nothing about this formula is
   linear in $T$.

   </details>

2. Using the worked example's method, estimate the temperature at which
   $\bar n=1$ for a 10 GHz qubit (double the frequency). Is it higher or
   lower than the 5 GHz case's 350 mK, and does this match the
   frequency-dependence stated in Common Mistakes?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **About 690 mK: higher, and exactly double.**

   Setting $\bar n=1$ gives $\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)=2$, so
   $T=\hbar\omega/(k_B\ln2)$. With $\omega=2\pi\times10\times10^{9}$:

   $$
   T = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})(2\pi\times10\times10^{9})}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(\ln 2)} \approx 0.69\text{ K} = 690\text{ mK}.
   $$

   Confirmed: `thermalPhotonOccupation(10e9, 0.6924)` returns exactly
   1.0.

   The doubling is not a coincidence and is worth extracting as a
   general fact. In the crossover formula $T$ appears linearly in
   $\omega$, so the $\bar n=1$ temperature is strictly proportional to
   the qubit frequency. More generally, $\bar n$ depends on $\omega$ and
   $T$ only through their ratio, so any two operating points with the
   same $\omega/T$ have identical thermal occupation. Doubling the
   frequency buys exactly a doubling of the temperature you are allowed
   to run at.

   **Does it match Common Mistakes?** Yes, and it is the same statement
   read from the other side. Common Mistakes says a *higher* $\omega$ at
   fixed $T$ gives a *smaller* $\bar n$, because $\hbar\omega/k_BT$ grows
   and the exponential in the denominator grows with it. Here the
   quantity held fixed is $\bar n=1$ instead of $T$, so the extra
   headroom shows up as a warmer permitted temperature rather than as a
   smaller occupation. Both are the same monotonicity.

   Sanity check against the table's fixed points: at 4 K the 5 GHz qubit
   has $\bar n\approx16.2$, and the 10 GHz qubit has $\bar
   n\approx7.84$, comfortably smaller as predicted. At 100 mK the 5 GHz
   qubit has $\bar n\approx0.0998$ and the 10 GHz qubit
   $\approx0.0083$, and notice that this last number is precisely the 5
   GHz value at 50 mK from question 1. Same ratio $\omega/T$, same
   answer.

   The wrong turn worth naming is the intuition that a higher-frequency
   qubit is "more energetic" and therefore more thermally exposed. The
   opposite is true, and the reason is the thermal bath's point of view:
   a bigger $\hbar\omega$ is a *more expensive* quantum for the bath to
   supply at a given $k_BT$, so it supplies one less often.

   </details>

3. Why is reaching 4K comparatively "easy" while continuing to 15mK is
   comparatively "hard"? What does this lesson say motivates a
   multi-stage cooling design instead of one big cooling step?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Two different reasons, and it is worth keeping them apart: the first
   is about mechanism, the second about cost.

   **4 K is reached by a machine; 15 mK needs a different physical
   process.** A mechanical pulse-tube cryocooler compresses and expands
   ordinary helium gas in a closed cycle, plugs into the wall, and holds
   its second stage near 4 K indefinitely with nothing to refill. Below
   about 1 K that approach stops working, because ordinary evaporative
   cooling of liquid helium stalls: the vapour pressure over the liquid
   falls exponentially as it cools, so there are eventually no atoms
   leaving to carry heat away. The dilution stage is a different
   mechanism entirely, evaporation into another *liquid* rather than into
   vapour. Cooled far enough, a ³He/⁴He mixture separates into a
   concentrated ³He phase floating on a dilute one, and every ³He atom
   crossing the boundary absorbs heat. That keeps working in the
   millikelvin range because the dilute phase keeps accepting ³He even as
   $T\to0$, where ordinary evaporation has long since given up. So the
   step from 4 K to 15 mK is not "the same cooler, turned up." It is a
   different physical process, requiring the still, the cold plate, the
   mixing chamber and a circulating ³He loop.

   **Cooling power collapses as it gets colder.** A pulse tube removes
   watts at 4 K. A mixing chamber removes hundreds of microwatts at 100
   mK and only tens of microwatts near 20 mK, a fall of several orders
   of magnitude over two decades of temperature. Every milliwatt that
   reaches the bottom of the fridge is therefore catastrophic in a way
   the same milliwatt at 4 K is not.

   **Which is what motivates the stages.** The lesson puts it as an
   engineering cost tradeoff, and the mechanism is heat interception.
   Radiation from the room and conduction down the coaxial control lines
   both have to be stopped, and the cheapest place to stop any watt of it
   is at the warmest stage that can absorb it. So the 50 K shield
   intercepts nearly all the room-temperature radiative load, the 4 K
   stage takes the next tranche, the still and cold plate take more, and
   the mixing chamber is left with only the residue. Hardware follows the
   same rule: everything sits at the warmest stage its own tolerance
   allows, and only the qubit chip itself goes all the way down.

   The wrong turn is imagining one very powerful cooler could do the
   whole job in a single step. The Carnot bound alone will not settle
   this, and it is worth being careful about why. A reversible cascade
   of stages has exactly the same efficiency as one reversible jump, so
   thermodynamics does not favour staging by itself. What it does supply
   is the scale of the bill: lifting heat from 15 mK to a 300 K room has
   a Carnot coefficient of performance of
   $T_c/(T_h-T_c)\approx5\times10^{-5}$, so even a perfect machine spends
   about 20 mW of work per microwatt removed at the bottom, and real
   dilution units fall orders of magnitude short of Carnot. The case for
   staging is the two arguments above it: no single working fluid spans
   300 K to 15 mK, and the heat intercepted at a warm stage never reaches
   the cold one, where removing it would be thousands of times more
   expensive.

   </details>

4. If a research group discovered a qubit design with a much LOWER
   transition frequency (say, 500 MHz instead of 5 GHz), would this make
   the cryogenic requirement easier or harder to meet, holding
   temperature fixed? Use the formula to justify your answer.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Much harder,** and by a margin that would put the design out of
   reach of a standard dilution refrigerator.

   Everything is in the exponent. Dropping the frequency by a factor of
   ten drops $\hbar\omega/k_BT$ by a factor of ten, and $\bar n$ depends
   on that ratio exponentially. At the fridge base of 15 mK:

   $$
   \bar n(5\text{ GHz}, 15\text{ mK}) \approx 1.1\times10^{-7}, \qquad \bar n(500\text{ MHz}, 15\text{ mK}) \approx 0.253.
   $$

   Both from `thermalPhotonOccupation`. That is a factor of about
   $2.2\times10^{6}$ worse, from a tenfold change in frequency, and
   $0.253$ is not $\ll1$ by any reading. A qubit sitting at the coldest
   place in the building would spend roughly a fifth of its idle time
   thermally excited.

   The crossover formula makes the same point more cleanly. $T$ for
   $\bar n=1$ is proportional to $\omega$, so a 500 MHz qubit crosses
   $\bar n=1$ at

   $$
   T = \frac{346\text{ mK}}{10} \approx 34.6\text{ mK},
   $$

   which is *below* nothing at all: a mixing chamber at 15 mK is colder
   than that, so the qubit is technically under its crossover. Being just
   under $\bar n=1$ is not the standard, though. To recover the same
   $1.1\times10^{-7}$ that a 5 GHz qubit enjoys at 15 mK you would need
   the same ratio $\omega/T$, which means 1.5 mK. That is roughly a
   factor of ten below the base temperature of a standard commercial
   dilution refrigerator, and it is not a matter of buying a bigger one.

   That scaling relation is the compact way to hold all of this. $\bar n$
   is a function of $\omega/T$ alone, so lowering the frequency by a
   factor $k$ demands lowering the temperature by the same factor $k$ to
   stay where you were.

   The wrong turn is a plausible-sounding one: a lower-frequency qubit is
   a lower-energy, gentler, less demanding device, so surely it is easier
   on the fridge. Lower energy is exactly the problem. A cheap quantum is
   one the thermal bath can afford to supply often. This is the same fact
   as Common Mistakes' warning, run in the unfavourable direction, and it
   is a real design constraint rather than a curiosity: it is part of why
   superconducting qubit frequencies sit in the gigahertz range instead
   of somewhere more convenient for the control electronics.

   </details>

## Summary

- Thermal photon occupation $\bar n=1/(\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1)$
  turns "is this cold enough?" into a number for a given qubit
  frequency and temperature.
- For a representative 5 GHz qubit: $\bar n\approx10^{-7}$ at 15 mK
  (safe), but already $\approx16$ at 4 K. 4 K alone is insufficient,
  which is why dilution refrigerators exist as a distinct, harder cooling
  stage.
- Staged cooling (room temp → ~50 K → 4 K → millikelvin) reflects real
  engineering cost tradeoffs, not an arbitrary design choice; the final
  dilution stage works by ³He "evaporating" across a liquid-liquid
  phase boundary in the mixing chamber, with the still pumping ³He
  around the loop.
