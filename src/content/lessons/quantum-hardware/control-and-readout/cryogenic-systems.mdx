## Motivation

Physical Qubit Platforms mentioned superconducting and spin qubits
operate at millikelvin temperatures almost in passing. This lesson makes
precise exactly *why*: not "cold is generally good for delicate
things," but a specific, computable physical requirement.

## Engineering Development

### Thermal photon occupation

A qubit's energy-level structure is, to good approximation near its
lowest transition, a harmonic mode at some frequency $\omega$
(Superconducting Qubits' anharmonic oscillator, right near its 0-1 gap).
In thermal equilibrium at temperature $T$, the mean number of thermal
photons occupying that mode follows the **Bose-Einstein distribution**:

$$
\bar n = \frac1{\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1}
$$

$\bar n\ll1$ means the mode is almost always found in its ground state,
exactly the reliable, well-defined starting point a qubit computation
needs. $\bar n\gtrsim1$ means the mode is thermally populated: even with
no computation happening yet, thermal noise has already randomized which
level the system sits in, before any gate is applied.

### The numbers, computed directly

For a representative 5 GHz superconducting qubit frequency,
`thermalPhotonOccupation` gives:

| Temperature | $\bar n$ |
|---|---|
| 15 mK (dilution fridge base) | $\approx1.1\times10^{-7}$ |
| 100 mK | $\approx0.10$ |
| 4 K (simple cryocooler) | $\approx16.2$ |
| 300 K (room temperature) | $\approx1250$ |

Two things stand out directly from this table: room temperature is
wildly unusable ($\bar n\sim1000$, thousands of thermal photons per
mode), and, more subtly, **4 K, though very cold, is still nowhere
near cold enough** ($\bar n\approx16$, over an order of magnitude too
warm). This is exactly why dilution refrigerators push further, to the
$10$-$20$ mK range, rather than stopping at the comparatively easy-to-reach
4 K stage.

way, `thermalPhotonOccupation` swept continuously over temperature) shows
the crossover isn't a cliff, it's a smooth exponential, with the horizontal
axis plotted as $\log_{10}(T/\text{K})$ and the vertical axis as
$\log_{10}(\bar n)$ so both the $10^{-7}$ and $10^3$ ends stay visible on
the same plot:

### Staged cooling

A dilution refrigerator cools in stages: roughly room temperature, then
liquid nitrogen temperature ($\sim77$ K), a pulse-tube stage ($\sim4$ K,
reachable with comparatively simple mechanical cooling), and finally the
dilution stage itself (a genuinely different cooling mechanism, based on
the physics of mixing two isotopes of helium) reaching the millikelvin
range. Each stage is progressively harder to build and more expensive
to operate, a real engineering reason systems don't just try to jump
straight to millikelvin in one step.

## Physical Interpretation

The 4 K vs. 15 mK gap in the table above is the single most important
number in this lesson: it's not a matter of "colder is nicer," but a
specific, computed threshold. $\bar n$ crosses from safely below 1 to
well above 1 somewhere in that gap, which is exactly why the last,
hardest cooling stage (the dilution refrigerator itself, not just a
simpler mechanical cryocooler) is unavoidable for this class of qubit.

## Worked Example

**At what rough temperature does $\bar n=1$ for a 5 GHz qubit?**

Setting $\bar n=1$: $\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)=2$, so
$T=\hbar\omega/(k_B\ln2)$. With $\omega=2\pi\times5\times10^9\text{ Hz}$:

$$
T = \frac{(1.055\times10^{-34})(2\pi\times5\times10^9)}{(1.381\times10^{-23})(\ln2)} \approx 0.35\text{ K} = 350\text{ mK}
$$

Consistent with the table: at $100$ mK, $\bar n\approx0.10$ (below 1);
by $4$ K, $\bar n\approx16$ (well above 1). The crossover sits
somewhere in between, matching this estimate.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking any cryogenic temperature is "cold enough."** As the table
shows directly, 4 K, genuinely very cold by everyday standards, still
gives $\bar n\approx16$, more than an order of magnitude too warm for
reliable qubit operation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming higher qubit frequency always makes thermal noise worse.**
The formula shows the opposite: HIGHER $\omega$ at fixed $T$ gives a
SMALLER $\bar n$ (larger $\hbar\omega/k_BT$ makes the exponential
larger). This is part of why qubit designs favor GHz-scale transition
frequencies rather than much lower ones.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Thermal photon occupation $\bar n=1/(\exp(\hbar\omega/k_BT)-1)$
  quantifies exactly how "safe" a given qubit frequency and temperature
  combination is.
- For a representative 5 GHz qubit: $\bar n\approx10^{-7}$ at 15 mK
  (safe), but already $\approx16$ at 4 K. 4 K alone is insufficient,
  which is why dilution refrigerators exist as a distinct, harder cooling
  stage.
- Staged cooling (room temp → 77 K → 4 K → millikelvin) reflects real
  engineering cost tradeoffs, not an arbitrary design choice.
