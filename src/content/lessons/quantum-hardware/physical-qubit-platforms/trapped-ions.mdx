## Starting From a Single Charged Atom

A trapped-ion quantum computer starts from a single charged atom
suspended in vacuum, and its first engineering problem is simply
keeping that ion in place: steady enough, for long enough, that lasers
can address its internal states repeatedly and entangle it with a
neighbor on demand. That confinement is harder than it sounds. A
static electric field can't do the job alone; Earnshaw's theorem, a
result from classical electrostatics, rules out trapping a charge in
3D with any static field configuration at all.

<Question>
So how does a trapped-ion quantum computer hold a single ion still enough to compute with it?
</Question>

That engineering problem, and its solution, define the whole platform.
Where a superconducting qubit is an entirely artificial circuit, a
trapped-ion qubit uses a real atom, with the qubit encoded in two of its
naturally-occurring internal energy levels. That is a different
engineering starting point, and it shows up directly in the platform's
tradeoffs.

## The Paul Trap and the Shared Motional Mode

### The trap itself

The answer to the puzzle just posed is a **Paul trap**: it
confines the ion using rapidly oscillating (radio-frequency) electric
fields rather than a static one, because Earnshaw's theorem rules the
static option out. The field oscillates fast enough that the ion feels
an effective, time-averaged confining potential. Multiple ions in the
same trap arrange themselves in a line, held apart by mutual Coulomb
repulsion balanced against the trap's confinement:

<HardwarePlatformSchematic variant="trapped-ion" ariaLabel="A linear chain of trapped ions held between RF electrodes, sharing a vibrational mode" />

The chain's ions aren't independent; the dashed line through them above
is the shared vibrational mode the next section relies on for two-qubit
gates.

The schematic shows the RF electrodes and leaves one thing out that a
real linear trap has. The oscillating field confines the ion in the two
directions *transverse* to the chain; confinement *along* the chain axis
comes from a pair of static DC endcap electrodes at the ends, held at a
voltage that pushes the ions inward. That is not a violation of the
theorem just invoked. Earnshaw forbids a static minimum in all three
directions at once, and these endcaps supply exactly one of them, with
the RF pseudopotential covering the other two. The division of labour is
worth carrying, because the axial mode those DC electrodes define is the
one a two-qubit gate usually drives.

Two physical conditions are worth fixing in mind, because they are the
opposite of a superconducting chip's. The trap electrodes themselves sit
near room temperature (no dilution refrigerator), but the ion has to be
in ultra-high vacuum, below roughly $10^{-11}$ mbar, or a stray
background-gas collision knocks it out of the trap mid-computation. And
the "wiring" is optical: every gate, every state preparation, and every
readout is a laser beam that has to be frequency-stabilized and pointed
at one atom. The engineering burden moved from the fridge to the optics
table; it did not go away.

### Why coherence times are so much longer

The qubit states are real atomic energy levels (electronic or hyperfine,
meaning nuclear-spin-related, transitions), which are, unlike a
lithographically-fabricated superconducting circuit, identical for every
ion of the same species and extremely well isolated from environmental
noise. Nature already built a "device" whose levels don't drift.
Trapped-ion <Term id="coherence">coherence times</Term> routinely reach seconds, several orders of
magnitude longer than superconducting qubits' microseconds. Read the
"about 1 s" this lesson computes with as a deliberately conservative
round number for a working register, not a record: dedicated
single-ion experiments on hyperfine qubits, with magnetic shielding and
dynamical decoupling, have reported coherence surviving for minutes.
Neutral atoms sit in the same regime for the same reason, both being
real atoms with hyperfine levels, and the capstone's chart is explicit
that it is not ranking the two. The cost
falls mainly on **two-qubit** gate speed: these mediate the interaction
through the ions' shared mechanical (vibrational) motion, which is slow
compared to a superconducting circuit's direct capacitive coupling, often
microseconds rather than nanoseconds. A <Term id="single-qubit-gates">single-qubit gate</Term>, driving one
ion's internal transition directly with a laser and no shared motion
involved, isn't bottlenecked the same way and can be considerably faster;
it's the entangling step specifically that pays the speed cost.

### All-to-all connectivity via the shared motional mode

The ions in a linear trap share collective vibrational modes (the whole
chain can oscillate together, like coupled pendulums). A two-qubit gate
works by using one ion's internal state to briefly excite this shared
motion, which the *other* ion's internal state can then sense, entangling
them regardless of physical separation along the chain. This gives
trapped ions a real engineering advantage over most solid-state
platforms: any ion can be entangled with any other in the same trap, not
just its physical neighbors, a different <Term id="qubit-routing-swap-overhead">connectivity
graph</Term> from superconducting qubits' typically nearest-neighbor layout.
The two connectivity graphs, drawn on the same five qubits, make the
contrast concrete rather than just asserted in prose:

## The Tradeoff, Now Visible

The core platform tradeoff is now visible directly: superconducting
qubits sacrifice coherence time for speed and (relatively) easier
fabrication at scale; trapped ions sacrifice speed for coherence time and
gain flexible connectivity. Neither is strictly better; which matters
more depends on the specific algorithm and error-correction overhead, a
question the Noise, Decoherence & Scaling course returns to
quantitatively.

IonQ and Quantinuum (built on Honeywell's original ion-trap group) have
staked entire product lines on this tradeoff, betting that fewer,
longer-lived, fully-connected qubits beat a much larger but noisier and
sparsely-wired array for near-term algorithms. Quantinuum's H-series
systems have repeatedly reported among the highest published two-qubit
<Term id="gate-fidelity">gate fidelities</Term> of any hardware platform, a commercial payoff of the
atomic-level isolation this lesson's coherence-time argument predicts
rather than a separate engineering achievement layered on top of it.

Treat any specific fidelity figure you read as dated, including that
one. Which platform holds the two-qubit record has changed more than
once, and a number quoted without a system name and a year is not worth
much. When you see one, check what it was measured on, when, and whether
it is a best pair or a median over the whole device.

## Gate Budget, Ion Against Superconductor

**A trapped-ion two-qubit gate takes about $10\mu s$; the ion's coherence
time is about $1s$. Compare this "gate count budget" to a superconducting
qubit's ($10ns$ gate, $10\mu s$ coherence).**

Trapped ion: $1s/10\mu s=10^5$ gates fit within one coherence time.
Superconducting: $10\mu s/10ns=10^3$ gates fit within one coherence
time. Despite trapped ions being roughly 1000× *slower* per gate, their
much longer coherence time gives them a *larger* raw gate budget in this
comparison, a concrete numerical illustration of why "gate speed alone"
is a misleading platform comparison metric.

## Comparing Coherence Times Without Gate Times

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming faster gates always mean a "better" platform.** As the worked
example shows directly, the ratio of coherence time to gate time, not
gate speed alone, sets the realistic operation budget.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking trapped-ion connectivity is unlimited for free.** All-to-all
connectivity via the shared motional mode is a real architectural
advantage, but it doesn't scale trivially: as more ions share one
trap's vibrational modes, gate fidelity and speed both degrade, a real
engineering constraint the Noise, Decoherence & Scaling course develops.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why can't a static (non-oscillating) electric field alone trap a
   charged ion in 3D? Then name the one trap design that does hold an ion
   with static fields, and say why it is not a counterexample.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because trapping requires a genuine minimum of the potential energy,
   and a static electric field in empty space cannot produce one.

   In the charge-free region where the ion sits, the electrostatic
   potential obeys Laplace's equation, $\nabla^2\varphi=0$. The ion's
   potential energy is $U=q\varphi$, so $\nabla^2U=0$ too, which written
   out is

   $$
   \frac{\partial^2U}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial z^2}=0.
   $$

   A minimum in 3D needs all three of those second derivatives positive,
   which would make the sum positive. The sum is zero. So if the field
   curves upward in $x$ and $y$, it must curve downward in $z$ by exactly
   as much. Every static configuration gives a saddle, never a bowl. That
   is Earnshaw's theorem, and the ion always finds the downhill direction
   and leaves.

   The wrong turn here is treating this as an engineering shortfall:
   "make the field stronger," or "add more electrodes and shape it
   better." Neither can work, and it is worth being clear about why.
   Laplace's equation holds for *every* static configuration in the
   region, whatever the electrode geometry or voltage. Adding electrodes
   adds more solutions to the same equation, so it produces a different
   saddle, not a bowl. This is a theorem, not a limit of current
   fabrication.

   The Paul trap's answer is to keep the saddle and spin it. An RF drive
   flips the confining and anti-confining axes fast compared to the ion's
   response, so the ion is pushed inward along whichever axis is
   momentarily downhill before it can travel far along the other one.
   Averaged over a drive cycle, the ion feels an effective pseudopotential
   that does have a real minimum. Nothing about Earnshaw has been
   evaded; the field is simply no longer static.

   The design worth naming is the **Penning trap**, which confines an ion
   with a static electric quadrupole plus a static magnetic field. It is
   not a counterexample. Earnshaw's theorem is a statement about forces
   derived from a potential satisfying Laplace's equation, and the
   magnetic force $q\mathbf v\times\mathbf B$ is not one of those: it
   depends on the ion's velocity, and it does no work. The static
   electric part still provides confinement along one axis and
   anti-confinement in the perpendicular plane, exactly as the theorem
   demands; the magnetic field bends the resulting outward motion into a
   circle. Penning traps are standard in precision mass spectrometry and
   have been used for qubit work, but the quantum computing platforms in
   this lesson are overwhelmingly Paul traps.

   </details>

2. Using the worked example's numbers, if a superconducting device
   somehow achieved a $100\mu s$ coherence time at the same $10ns$ gate
   speed, would its gate budget exceed the trapped ion's $10^5$? Compute
   the new ratio.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. It reaches $10^4$, still a factor of 10 short.

   $$
   \frac{100\ \mu\text{s}}{10\text{ ns}} = \frac{100\times10^{-6}}{10\times10^{-9}} = 10^4 \text{ gates}.
   $$

   The tenfold improvement in coherence time bought exactly a tenfold
   improvement in budget, from $10^3$ to $10^4$, because the budget is
   linear in coherence time. Nothing accelerates here. To match the ion's
   $10^5$ at a 10 ns gate you would need $10^5\times10\text{ ns}=1$ ms of
   coherence, a hundred times the lesson's baseline rather than ten.

   The wrong turn is an order-of-magnitude blur: seeing "100 μs" next to
   "1 s" and reading them as roughly comparable because both are "much
   longer than a nanosecond." They differ by four orders of magnitude,
   and on a log axis that is most of the chart. Write both budgets as
   powers of ten before comparing them and the gap is unmissable.

   One caveat that cuts against the superconducting side, so it is worth
   stating rather than quietly enjoying. The two numbers being divided
   are not the same kind of gate. The ion's 10 μs is its *two-qubit*
   entangling gate, the operation that routes through the shared motional
   mode and sets the platform's pace. The superconducting 10 ns is a
   single-qubit gate; a two-qubit gate on that platform is more like tens
   to a few hundred nanoseconds. Count the operation that actually
   bottlenecks each platform and the hypothetical device's budget shrinks
   further. The comparison as posed is generous to the superconducting
   entry, and it still loses.

   </details>

3. Explain, in your own words, how the shared vibrational mode allows a
   two-qubit gate between two ions that aren't in direct contact. Then
   say what has to be true of that vibrational mode when the gate
   finishes, and what goes wrong if it isn't.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The motion is a bus, and the gate is two conditional trips on it.

   Ions in one trap are not independent objects. Each one's charge pushes
   on its neighbours, so their positions are coupled and the chain moves
   in collective normal modes, like beads on a shared spring. A laser
   tuned to a *motional sideband* of an ion's internal transition couples
   that ion's internal state to one of those shared modes: it can add or
   remove a quantum of the chain's vibration, conditioned on which
   internal state the ion is in.

   That is the whole trick. Illuminate ion A so that it excites the
   shared mode only if it is in $|1\rangle$. The chain is now vibrating
   in a way that depends on A's internal state, and every ion in the
   chain shares that vibration, including one sitting several ions away.
   Now illuminate ion B so that its internal state responds to whether
   that vibration is there. B's state has become correlated with A's,
   with no direct A-to-B interaction anywhere in the story. Because the
   mode belongs to the whole chain rather than to any pair, it works for
   any two ions, which is where the platform's all-to-all connectivity
   comes from.

   **What has to be true at the end:** the motional mode must come back
   to exactly the state it started in, and be left unentangled with
   either qubit. A real entangling gate is designed so the excursion into
   the motional degree of freedom closes: the trajectory in phase space
   returns to its origin, and the vibration is handed back untouched
   while a state-dependent phase is kept.

   If it does not close, the two internal states end up entangled with
   the vibration. Nobody measures the vibration, so it is traced out, and
   tracing out a system that your qubits are entangled with leaves a
   *mixed* two-qubit state instead of a clean Bell state. The gate does
   not fail loudly; it returns a state with reduced purity, which reads
   as reduced fidelity. This is also the mechanism behind the lesson's
   warning that all-to-all connectivity does not scale for free: more
   ions in a trap means more, more closely spaced motional modes, more
   spectator modes for the drive to touch accidentally, and more heating,
   all of which make the closure condition harder to satisfy for every
   pair at once.

   Two seductive wrong answers are worth separating out. The first is
   "their Coulomb repulsion couples the internal states." It does not.
   The charge couples the ions' *positions*; the qubit lives in
   electronic or hyperfine levels that two ions micrometres apart have
   essentially no direct interaction between. Coulomb repulsion builds
   the bus; it is not the gate. The second is "one laser hits both ions,
   so they are linked." A beam that addresses two ions separately applies
   a product of single-ion operations, and a product of single-qubit
   operations can never create entanglement, however many ions it lands
   on.

   </details>

4. Name one advantage and one disadvantage of trapped ions relative to
   superconducting qubits, using this lesson's specific numbers.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Advantage: coherence, and so gate budget.** About 1 s against about
   10 μs is five orders of magnitude, and it converts into $10^5$ gates
   inside a coherence time against $10^3$. There is a second advantage in
   the same lesson, connectivity: within one trap every ion can be
   entangled with every other, which on five qubits is ten available
   pairs against the superconducting chain's four.

   **Disadvantage: speed.** The entangling gate takes about 10 μs against
   roughly 10 ns, so each operation is about a thousand times slower.
   That is wall-clock time, and it is not free just because the budget is
   larger: a circuit of fixed depth takes a thousand times longer to run,
   and the number of shots per second, which is what a variational
   algorithm's runtime is actually made of, drops by the same factor.

   The wrong turn to avoid is concluding "so trapped ions are better."
   The two numbers point in opposite directions, and which one binds
   depends entirely on the workload. A deep circuit run once is limited
   by the gate budget, where ions win. A shallow circuit run a million
   times, which is what most near-term variational work looks like, is
   limited by throughput, where they lose badly.

   Three further honest notes, since the question asks for the lesson's
   own numbers.

   The coherence figures are not the same kind of quantity. A trapped
   ion's qubit levels are properties of an atom, identical for every ion
   of the species and stable by nature; a transmon's $T_1$ is a median
   over a chip that drifts between calibrations as two-level-system
   defects move around.

   The engineering burden moved rather than vanished. A trapped-ion
   system needs no dilution refrigerator, and instead needs ultra-high
   vacuum below about $10^{-11}$ mbar, plus a frequency-stabilized laser
   pointed at every individual atom. Room-temperature electrodes are not
   the same thing as a simple machine.

   Finally, resist quoting a fidelity as the advantage. Quantinuum's
   ion systems have repeatedly reported among the best published
   two-qubit fidelities, but that record has changed hands more than
   once, and a fidelity number without a system name, a year, and a note
   on whether it is a best pair or a device median is not evidence of
   anything.

   </details>

## Summary

- Trapped ions use real atoms confined by oscillating electric fields
  (a Paul trap), a different physical starting point from superconducting
  circuits: room-temperature electrodes, ultra-high vacuum, and lasers
  in place of coaxial lines.
- Coherence times reach seconds (vs. superconducting qubits' microseconds),
  at the cost of slower gates (microseconds, not nanoseconds).
- The shared motional mode gives all-to-all connectivity within one
  trap, an architectural advantage that nevertheless faces its own
  scaling limits.
