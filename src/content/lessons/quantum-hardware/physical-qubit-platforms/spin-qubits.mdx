## Quantum Dots and Exchange Coupling

### The quantum dot

A **quantum dot** is a nanoscale region of semiconductor, created by
precisely patterned electrodes on a chip, small enough to confine a
single electron:

<HardwarePlatformSchematic variant="spin-qubit" ariaLabel="An electron confined in a gate-defined quantum dot, patterned electrodes forming the confining potential" />

This confinement uses the same physics as
this platform's Wave Mechanics course's infinite/finite square well
(spatial confinement forcing discrete energy levels), applied here in a
real fabricated device rather than an idealized 1D model. The confined
electron's spin, up or down along some chosen axis, is the qubit,
directly realizing spin-1/2's two states, $|\!\uparrow\rangle$ and
$|\!\downarrow\rangle$.

Modeling the dot's confining potential as a finite square well (half-width
$1.5$, depth $8$, natural units, matching Wave Mechanics' own convention)
makes that parallel visual rather than only verbal:

The dashed line marks the confined electron's ground-state energy, and
the overlaid curve its ground-state wavefunction: peaked inside the dot,
decaying into the classically forbidden region outside it, the
finite-well physics Wave Mechanics' Finite Square Well lesson already
derived (the same $A\cos(kx)$ inside, $Be^{-\kappa|x|}$ outside

a lithographically patterned confining region instead of an idealized 1D
box. Like any finite well, a real dot supports more than one bound level;
sketching the lowest three shows the same qualitative ladder (only the
ground state's energy above is solved exactly, the way the Finite Square

Read the gaps, not the absolute heights: they widen going up, the
signature of a box-like confining region rather than the evenly spaced
ladder a harmonic well would give. A transmon's ladder, by contrast,
narrows going up, which is the anharmonicity Superconducting Qubits
depends on. Same picture, opposite trend, different qubit.

### Why small size matters

A gate-defined quantum dot is tens of nanometers across. A transmon's
circuit is hundreds of micrometers (four orders of magnitude larger in
linear size), and a trapped ion's confinement region larger still. A
spin qubit is therefore built at roughly the scale existing transistors
are built at, which means spin-qubit fabrication can, in principle,
lean on the same lithographic tooling and manufacturing infrastructure
the semiconductor industry has spent decades perfecting for classical
chips: a concrete scalability argument distinct from any advantage
claimed by the other platforms in this course.

The catch is that "small" has not yet meant "many." As of the mid-2020s
the largest published spin-qubit registers hold on the order of ten
qubits, against hundreds for superconducting chips and thousands of
atoms in a tweezer array. Nor does small mean warm: most spin-qubit
devices still run in a dilution refrigerator, though silicon "hot qubit"
work has demonstrated operation at around 1 K and above, where cooling
power is orders of magnitude cheaper than at 10 mK. And the dominant noise source is
electrical: the same gate electrodes that define the dot couple it to
charge fluctuations in the surrounding material, which is why
isotopically purified silicon-28 (removing the nuclear-spin bath) has
become standard and why charge noise, not the spin itself, sets the
coherence a real device achieves.

### Coherence and gate speed

Spin qubits sit in an intermediate regime, with one condition attached.
The long <Term id="coherence">coherence times</Term> this platform is quoted for, milliseconds and
beyond, are measured in isotopically purified silicon-28 with dynamical
decoupling applied; on unpurified material the raw dephasing time is
microseconds or less, no better than a transmon. Nuclear-spin qubits do
better again, being extremely well isolated from charge noise. Gate
operations, meanwhile, are driven electrically or magnetically at
speeds closer to superconducting qubits' nanosecond scale than to
trapped ions' microseconds. That combination lands them in a stretch of
the coherence/speed tradeoff space neither platform already covered
quite occupies, provided the material is purified.

Driving a spin electrically or magnetically is, physically, the
original context the two-level Rabi problem was built for (electron/
nuclear spin resonance): an oscillating field resonant with the
$|\!\uparrow\rangle$-$|\!\downarrow\rangle$ energy gap rotates the spin
the same way a microwave tone rotates a superconducting qubit or a laser
rotates a trapped ion's internal state.

### Exchange coupling: how two-qubit gates work

A single confined spin is only half the story: two-qubit gates need two
spins to interact. Placing two quantum dots close together lets their
electrons' wavefunctions overlap slightly, and that overlap produces an
**exchange interaction** (Heisenberg exchange, strength $J$) that couples
the two spins' states to each other. $J$ isn't fixed: raising
or lowering the potential barrier between the dots, typically with a gate
voltage, tunes the wavefunction overlap and switches the exchange
coupling on or off on demand, which is what implements a two-qubit gate
(a SWAP-like or <Term id="cnot-controlled-gates">CNOT-equivalent operation</Term>) between neighboring spins.
Because this coupling comes from direct wavefunction overlap, it's
inherently short-range: it only works between physically adjacent dots.
That's why spin-qubit devices are laid out with nearest-neighbor,
dot-to-dot connectivity rather than the all-to-all coupling a trapped-ion
chain enjoys.

Exchange works *because of* <Term id="pauli-exclusion-principle">Pauli exclusion</Term>, not despite it. Two
electrons are identical fermions, so their full two-particle wavefunction
(spatial part times spin part) has to come out antisymmetric under
swapping the two electrons. That requirement locks spin and space
together: pair the electrons' spins into the antisymmetric combination
(the **singlet**) and the spatial wavefunction is forced symmetric; pair
the spins into any symmetric combination (a **triplet**) and the spatial
wavefunction is forced antisymmetric instead. A symmetric spatial
wavefunction overlaps the confining potential differently than an
antisymmetric one, so singlet and triplet sit at different energies.
That splitting *is* $J$. Identical Particles already built this
antisymmetrize/symmetrize machinery for two electrons in two spatial
orbitals; here it's the same operator, applied to two spins instead:

The singlet preset above is the antisymmetric combination Identical

triplet preset is its symmetric counterpart. A working
exchange gate is, physically, a controlled rotation between these two
energy-split combinations, not a new piece of two-qubit-gate machinery
invented from scratch for this platform.

## Manufacturability as the Pitch

Spin qubits' central engineering pitch isn't raw coherence time or gate
speed individually. It's *manufacturability at scale*, riding on
existing semiconductor fabrication expertise. Whether this translates
into a practical advantage depends on solving spin-qubit-specific
challenges (uniform, reliable dot fabrication; addressing individual
spins without disturbing neighbors) that remain active engineering
problems.

<ResearchConnection title="Betting a production fab line on spin qubits" source="Intel Components Research; academic groups at Delft, UNSW, and RIKEN">
Intel is the clearest bet on this pitch: its Components Research group
fabricates spin-qubit test chips (Tunnel Falls) on the same 300mm
production line and tooling it uses for commercial transistors, and it
has paired that with Horse Ridge, cryogenic control chips designed to
sit inside the refrigerator next to the qubits instead of routing every
control line out to room-temperature electronics. Academic groups at
Delft, UNSW, and RIKEN have pushed isotopically-purified silicon devices
toward the multi-qubit, high-fidelity regime that would make this
manufacturing argument pay off.
</ResearchConnection>

Whether "the same fab line as classical chips" beats "atoms and photons
that are identical by nature" is the question the rest of this course's
comparison turns on.

## A Particle in a Box, at Nanometre Scale

**Why is confining a single electron in a quantum dot conceptually the
same problem as Wave Mechanics' particle-in-a-box, when the physical
device in front of you looks nothing like one?**

Both are the same underlying physics: a particle (there, idealized; here,
a real electron) confined by a potential energy barrier to a finite
spatial region, forcing discrete energy levels via the boundary
conditions the wavefunction must satisfy. The quantum dot's confining
potential is created by real electrode voltages rather than an idealized
infinite wall, but the qualitative consequence, quantized energy levels
from spatial confinement, is the identical physical mechanism Wave
Mechanics already derived.

## Assuming Small Means Easy

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking "smaller" automatically means "easier to build at scale."**
Small size buys you a manufacturing-compatibility advantage and nothing
else on its own; spin qubits still face unresolved engineering challenges
(uniformity across many fabricated dots, precise individual addressing)
before that advantage translates into a working large-scale device.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing spin qubits with superconducting qubits because both are
"solid-state."** The qubit itself is physically different: a
single confined electron's spin, versus a collective circuit
excitation, with correspondingly different physics governing
coherence and control.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. What physical mechanism confines an electron in a quantum dot, and
   how does it parallel Wave Mechanics' particle-in-a-box?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Voltages on lithographically patterned gate electrodes shape an
   electrostatic potential landscape in the semiconductor, and that
   landscape has a local minimum a few tens of nanometres across. An
   electron that falls into it has nowhere lower to go, so it stays. That
   is the whole mechanism: no walls, no box, just a dip in the potential
   energy made out of applied voltages.

   The parallel with the particle in a box is that both are the same
   statement of quantum mechanics: confine a particle to a finite region
   and its wavefunction has to satisfy boundary conditions there, and
   only a discrete set of energies admits a solution that does. The
   quantization is a consequence of confinement, not of anything special
   about idealized walls. Modelled as a finite square well of half-width
   1.5 and depth 8 in the natural units Wave Mechanics uses, this
   lesson's dot has a ground state at $E_0\approx-7.598$, with the same
   $A\cos(kx)$ inside and $Be^{-\kappa|x|}$ outside construction, matched
   for continuity at the edge.

   The one genuinely different thing is that the dot's well has **finite
   depth**. Three things follow that the infinite box does not have. The
   wavefunction does not vanish at the boundary; it leaks into the
   classically forbidden region and decays as $e^{-\kappa|x|}$, with
   $\kappa\approx3.90$ here. There are only finitely many bound states
   rather than an infinite ladder. And an electron can leave: lower the
   barrier with a gate voltage and it tunnels out, which is not a defect
   but the mechanism by which dots are loaded and emptied in the first
   place. The spacing pattern differs too. Read the lesson's ladder and
   the gaps *widen* going up, which is the box-like signature, in
   contrast with a transmon's ladder, whose gaps narrow.

   The wrong turn here is the one that quietly changes what the qubit is.
   It is tempting, having drawn a ladder of confined energy levels, to
   read the bottom two rungs as $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$. They are
   not. Those are *orbital* levels, and their spacing in a real dot is
   large, in the millielectronvolt range. The qubit is the confined
   electron's **spin**, up or down along a chosen axis, and the splitting
   between those two states is set by an applied magnetic field through
   the Zeeman effect. The confinement's job is to hold exactly one
   electron in one place so that its spin can be addressed and coupled to
   a neighbour. It is the packaging, not the two-level system.

   </details>

2. Name the specific manufacturing advantage spin qubits claim over
   superconducting qubits or trapped ions.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   They are built at roughly the size and with roughly the tooling of a
   transistor, so the semiconductor industry's existing fabrication
   infrastructure applies to them almost directly.

   The size gap is the concrete part. A gate-defined quantum dot is tens
   of nanometres across. A transmon's circuit is hundreds of micrometres,
   about four orders of magnitude larger in linear dimension, and a
   trapped ion's confinement region is larger still. Whatever else is
   true, a spin qubit is a nanoscale feature patterned on silicon, which
   is the thing the industry has spent five decades learning to do
   repeatably and at volume. Intel's Tunnel Falls chips are fabricated on
   the same 300 mm production line and with the same tooling as its
   commercial transistors, which is the clearest statement of the claim
   anyone has made.

   Notice what kind of argument this is. Every other platform in the
   course pitches a *physical* property: superconducting qubits pitch
   gate speed, trapped ions pitch coherence and connectivity, neutral
   atoms pitch reconfigurable geometry, photons pitch flight. Spin qubits
   pitch **manufacturing infrastructure**. It is an economic and
   industrial argument about how a large machine would get built, not a
   claim about how any individual qubit performs.

   Two wrong turns, both of which promote the claim into something the
   lesson does not say.

   The first is reading it as "smaller, so more qubits fit." The lesson
   is blunt that small has not yet meant many: published spin-qubit
   registers are on the order of ten qubits, against hundreds of
   superconducting qubits on a chip and thousands of atoms in a tweezer
   array. Areal density is not the advantage today; tooling is.

   The second is reading "manufacturable" as any claim about coherence or
   speed. Longest coherence in this course belongs to trapped ions and
   neutral atoms, fastest gates to superconducting circuits, and
   all-to-all connectivity through a shared mode to ions. Spin qubits
   claim none of those. Attaching one of them to this platform is the
   most common way this question goes wrong.

   </details>

3. Where does spin qubits' coherence/speed regime sit relative to
   superconducting qubits and trapped ions, per this lesson?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Intermediate on both axes, and closer to superconducting qubits on
   speed than to ions.

   Gates run at roughly 10 to 100 nanoseconds, driven electrically or
   magnetically. That is within an order of magnitude of a
   superconducting gate's ~10 ns, and two to three orders faster than a
   trapped ion's ~10 μs entangling gate depending on where in that range
   a given device lands. Coherence reaches milliseconds, which sits between a transmon's
   ~10 μs and an ion's ~1 s, a hundred times better than the first and a
   thousand times short of the second. The gate budget lands where you
   would expect from those two:

   $$
   \frac{1\text{ ms}}{100\text{ ns}} = 10^4 \text{ gates},
   $$

   against $10^3$ for a representative superconducting device and $10^5$
   for a trapped ion. Squarely in between, which is the point: this is a
   stretch of the tradeoff space neither of the other two platforms
   occupies.

   **The condition attached to that answer is not optional.** The
   millisecond figure is measured in isotopically purified silicon-28,
   with dynamical decoupling applied. Strip either away and the claim
   collapses: on ordinary silicon, whose natural isotopes carry nuclear
   spins that form a fluctuating magnetic bath around the electron, raw
   dephasing times are microseconds or less, which is no better than a
   transmon. Purification is the reason the intermediate regime exists,
   which is why Si-28 has become standard rather than being an optional
   refinement.

   The wrong turn is quoting "milliseconds" as a property of the
   platform. It is a property of the platform *plus* a specific material
   and a specific pulse sequence, and a real device that skipped either
   would not show it. There is a related trap in the other direction:
   the dominant noise source in a purified device is not the nuclear
   spins any more but electrical charge fluctuation, coupling in through
   the very gate electrodes that define the dot. Purifying the isotopes
   removes one bath and leaves another, so the coherence a real device
   achieves is set by charge noise rather than by the spin itself.

   Nuclear-spin qubits, where the qubit lives in the nucleus rather than
   the electron, do better again, being far better isolated from that
   charge noise. They are correspondingly harder to drive quickly, which
   is the same isolation-versus-coupling tradeoff the capstone draws as
   the pattern behind every row of the table.

   </details>

4. Is spin qubits' "manufacturability" advantage automatically decisive
   for building a large-scale quantum computer? What open challenges
   does this lesson name? Then say why a modern fab's tolerance for
   parameter spread does not transfer to qubits.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. It is a real advantage that has not yet been converted into a
   large machine, and the lesson names what stands in the way.

   The challenges it identifies:

   **Uniform, reliable dot fabrication.** The dot's confining potential,
   and therefore the electron's environment, depends on nanometre-scale
   geometry and on the local disorder in the material. Nominally
   identical dots do not come out identical.

   **Addressing individual spins without disturbing neighbours.** Dots
   sit close together by design, since exchange coupling requires
   wavefunction overlap. A field aimed at one spin is not far from the
   next one.

   **Registers still on the order of ten qubits**, as of the mid-2020s,
   against hundreds of superconducting qubits and thousands of trapped
   atoms.

   **Charge noise**, coupling in through the same gate electrodes that
   create the dot, which is what sets a real device's coherence even
   after isotopic purification has dealt with the nuclear-spin bath.

   **Still cold.** Most spin-qubit devices run in a dilution
   refrigerator. Silicon "hot qubit" work has demonstrated operation
   around 1 K and above, where cooling power is orders of magnitude
   cheaper than at 10 mK, but that is a demonstration rather than the
   standard operating point.

   To that list the lesson's own physics adds one more: exchange coupling
   comes from direct wavefunction overlap, so it is inherently
   short-range and the connectivity is nearest-neighbour, dot to dot. A
   spin-qubit chip inherits the routing and SWAP overhead a
   superconducting chip has, and gets none of a trapped-ion chain's
   all-to-all relief.

   The seductive wrong turn is "the fab already exists, so scaling is a
   solved problem." A modern fab is extraordinarily good at making
   billions of transistors whose parameters vary by a few percent, in a
   context where that variation is absorbed by binning, redundancy and
   error-correcting logic that has been refined for decades. A qubit is
   not that kind of device. Its operating frequency depends sensitively
   on nanometre-scale details, and there is no equivalent of binning: a
   qubit that came out at the wrong frequency is a qubit you have to
   calibrate around, not one you sell as a slower part.

   There is a second, quieter version of the same overreach. The 300 mm
   line makes the dots. It does not make the wiring, and a large
   spin-qubit register faces the same control-line bottleneck a large
   superconducting chip does, since every qubit still needs its lines
   routed out of a cold environment. Intel's Horse Ridge cryogenic
   control chips exist because that problem is separate from
   the fabrication one and has to be solved on its own.

   </details>

## Summary

- Spin qubits confine a single electron in a semiconductor quantum dot,
  encoding the qubit directly in its spin: the same physics as Wave
  Mechanics' confinement problems and Angular Momentum & Spin's
  spin-1/2 system, built into real hardware.
- Small device size offers a distinct scalability argument:
  compatibility with existing semiconductor fabrication tooling.
- Sits in an intermediate coherence/speed regime: faster gates than
  trapped ions, and longer coherence than superconducting qubits once
  the silicon is isotopically purified, with its own unresolved
  fabrication-uniformity challenges.
- Register sizes are still on the order of ten qubits, charge noise from
  the defining electrodes dominates coherence, and most devices still
  need a dilution refrigerator. Small has not yet delivered many.
- Two-qubit exchange gates are a direct consequence of Pauli exclusion:
  the singlet/triplet energy splitting $J$ comes from the same
  antisymmetrization requirement Identical Particles derived for any
  two electrons, applied here to spin instead of spatial orbitals.
