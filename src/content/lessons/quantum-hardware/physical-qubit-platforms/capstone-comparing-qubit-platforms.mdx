## Motivation

Five lessons, five platforms, each with its own physical mechanism and
its own tradeoffs. This capstone doesn't introduce new physics. It puts
everything side by side, which is where the actual engineering
decision-making happens in practice.

## The Comparison

| Platform | Coherence time | Gate speed | Connectivity | Scaling path |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superconducting | ~microseconds | ~nanoseconds (fast) | mostly nearest-neighbor | lithographic fabrication, many labs/companies actively scaling |
| Trapped ions | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds (slow) | all-to-all within a trap | chain length limited; modular/networked traps an active research direction |
| Neutral atoms | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds | flexible, reconfigurable geometry | hundreds of qubits already demonstrated; among the largest current counts |
| Photonic | effectively no decoherence in flight; loss-limited instead | very fast per operation | naturally networked (light travels) | strong for communication/networking; two-qubit gates remain the hard problem |
| Spin qubits | intermediate–long | ~nanoseconds | nearest-neighbor (dot-to-dot) | leverages existing semiconductor fabrication tooling |

(These are representative orders of magnitude for illustrating the
tradeoff space, not precise specifications of any particular device;
real numbers vary by generation, vendor, and continue to improve.)

Photonic's bar is highlighted because it isn't really on the same

capture and the table's loss-limited caveat spells out.

### The general pattern: no free lunch

Every platform trades something for something else: superconducting
qubits trade coherence for speed and fabrication maturity; trapped ions
and neutral atoms trade gate speed for coherence and (for neutral atoms)
flexible geometry; photons trade an entirely different error model
(loss, not decoherence) for a much harder gate mechanism; spin qubits
trade smaller, more standard fabrication for intermediate performance on
every other axis. This isn't a temporary state of immaturity to be
"solved." It reflects genuine underlying physics tradeoffs: strong
environmental isolation, which improves coherence, tends to also
weaken the coupling needed for fast gates.

## Worked Example

**A research group needs to run a long calculation requiring many
sequential gates, but connectivity between arbitrary qubits matters less
than raw operation count. Which platform's tradeoff profile favors this?**

Trapped ions or neutral atoms: both offer long coherence times, giving a
large "gate budget" (as computed directly in the Trapped Ions lesson's
worked example: $\sim10^5$ gates before decoherence dominates, versus
$\sim10^3$ for a representative superconducting device), favoring
platforms where coherence time, not raw gate speed, is the limiting
resource for this specific priority.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Looking for a single "best" platform.** This lesson's comparison table
has no row that wins on every column. The right platform depends on
which axis a specific application actually needs most.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating these numbers as fixed, permanent specifications.** Every
platform's numbers have improved substantially over time and continue
to; this table describes representative *orders of magnitude* and
qualitative tradeoffs, not a snapshot to memorize as timeless fact.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Comparing coherence time, gate speed, connectivity, and scalability
  side by side shows every platform trading strength on one axis for
  weakness on another; no platform dominates across the board.
- This reflects genuine physical tradeoffs (isolation vs. coupling
  strength), not simply immature engineering that will resolve itself.
- Application priorities (raw gate count vs. fast iteration vs.
  networking) should drive platform choice, not a single "best" ranking.
