## Motivation

Five separate lessons each made one platform's physics feel like the
obvious choice, in isolation. Read side by side, none of them do:
superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photons, and spin
qubits each trade something real for something else. This capstone adds
no new physics. It puts all five in the same room, which is where the
actual engineering decision-making happens in practice.

## The Tradeoff Space, Side by Side

Same four axes, five platforms, one table: this is what an actual
engineering decision between them rests on.

| Platform | Coherence time | Gate speed | Connectivity | Scaling path |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superconducting | ~microseconds | ~nanoseconds (fast) | mostly nearest-neighbor | lithographic fabrication, many labs/companies actively scaling |
| Trapped ions | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds (slow) | all-to-all within a trap | chain length limited; modular/networked traps an active research direction |
| Neutral atoms | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds | flexible, reconfigurable geometry | hundreds of qubits already demonstrated; among the largest current counts |
| Photonic | effectively no decoherence in flight; loss-limited instead | very fast per operation | naturally networked (light travels) | strong for communication/networking; two-qubit gates remain the hard problem |
| Spin qubits | intermediate–long | ~nanoseconds | nearest-neighbor (dot-to-dot) | leverages existing semiconductor fabrication tooling |

(These are representative orders of magnitude for illustrating the
tradeoff space, not precise specifications of any particular device;
real numbers vary by generation, vendor, and continue to improve.)

The table's five rows are five physically distinct structures, not just
five sets of numbers; each platform lesson's own schematic,

Five different physical mechanisms for the same underlying job (an
isolated, controllable two-level system) is exactly why the table above
has no row that wins on every column: each structure's own geometry is
what produces its particular coherence/speed/connectivity tradeoff.

### Coherence time, on an actual log scale

The table's "~microseconds" vs. "~seconds" entries aren't just different
adjectives, they're different by up to six orders of magnitude. Plotting
the representative numbers each platform's own lesson already worked
with (bar height is $\log_{10}(\text{seconds})$, shifted up by 6 so every
bar is positive; the caption on each bar is the real value) makes the
size of that gap honest instead of ordinal:

<BarChart
  ariaLabel="Coherence time in seconds, log scale, across four platforms: superconducting around 10 microseconds, spin qubits around 1 millisecond, trapped ions around 1 second, neutral atoms around 13 seconds"
  bars={coherenceTimeBars}
/>

Photonic qubits aren't on this chart at all, deliberately. $T_1$/$T_2$
decay is a *rate*, something that happens gradually and can be assigned a
time constant; "no decoherence in flight" isn't a very long rate, it's the
absence of the gradual-decay mechanism the other four platforms all
share. Forcing it onto this axis with some large placeholder number would
misrepresent it as "just longer," when the Photonic Qubits lesson's whole
point is that its dominant error (loss) has a different mathematical
shape entirely.

### Gate speed, the same way

Gate time spans just as many orders of magnitude, in the opposite
direction from coherence: here a taller bar means *faster* (the value
plotted is $-\log_{10}(\text{seconds})$), so the platforms that were tall
on the coherence chart tend to be short here, and vice versa:

<BarChart
  ariaLabel="Single-qubit gate speed across four platforms, log scale with taller meaning faster: superconducting around 10 nanoseconds, spin qubits around 100 nanoseconds, neutral atoms around 1 microsecond, trapped ions around 10 microseconds"
  bars={gateTimeBars}
/>

Photonic is excluded here too, for a related but different reason: a
passive polarization rotation has essentially no drive time at all, while
the platform's actual bottleneck, the probabilistic two-photon gate, isn't
clocked the way a Rabi-driven pulse is. There's no single "gate time"
number to plot for a gate that sometimes doesn't happen.

Lay the two charts side by side and the "no free lunch" pattern isn't an
assertion anymore, it's arithmetic: superconducting and spin qubits sit
at the fast end of the gate-speed chart and the short end of the
coherence chart; trapped ions and neutral atoms sit at the opposite end
of both. No platform is tall on both charts at once.

### Connectivity: a shared mode versus a redrawable map

The table's "connectivity" column hides two genuinely different
mechanisms behind similar-sounding phrases. Trapped ions get all-to-all
coupling for free from one shared vibrational mode; superconducting
qubits get nearest-neighbor coupling from fixed, lithographically-drawn
wires; neutral atoms get something in between, not an intrinsically
richer graph, but a graph that can be *redrawn* between shots because
each atom sits in its own independently-steerable tweezer:

### The general pattern: no free lunch

<PredictBeforeReveal

  correctValue="worse"
  explanation="This is the pattern underneath every row of the table above: trapped ions and neutral atoms isolate their qubits extremely well (seconds-long coherence) and pay for it with microsecond gates driven through comparatively weak mechanical or Rydberg coupling; superconducting and spin qubits accept noisier, shorter-lived qubits in exchange for the strong, fast coupling a solid-state circuit or gate voltage can provide directly. The same physical mechanism that keeps the environment out also keeps the control fields' coupling weak."
/>

Every platform trades something for something else: superconducting
qubits trade coherence for speed and fabrication maturity; trapped ions
and neutral atoms trade gate speed for coherence and (for neutral atoms)
flexible geometry; photons trade an entirely different error model
(loss, not decoherence) for a much harder gate mechanism; spin qubits
trade smaller, more standard fabrication for intermediate performance on
every other axis. This isn't a temporary state of immaturity to be
"solved." It reflects genuine underlying physics tradeoffs: strong
environmental isolation, which improves coherence, tends to also
weaken the coupling needed for fast gates.

## Worked Example

**A research group needs to run a long calculation requiring many
sequential gates, but connectivity between arbitrary qubits matters less
than raw operation count. Which platform's tradeoff profile favors this?**

Trapped ions or neutral atoms: both offer long coherence times, giving a
large "gate budget" (as computed directly in the Trapped Ions lesson's
worked example: $\sim10^5$ gates before decoherence dominates, versus
$\sim10^3$ for a representative superconducting device), favoring
platforms where coherence time, not raw gate speed, is the limiting
resource for this specific priority.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Looking for a single "best" platform.** This lesson's comparison table
has no row that wins on every column. The right platform depends on
which axis a specific application actually needs most.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating these numbers as fixed, permanent specifications.** Every
platform's numbers have improved substantially over time and continue
to; this table describes representative *orders of magnitude* and
qualitative tradeoffs, not a snapshot to memorize as timeless fact.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Comparing coherence time, gate speed, connectivity, and scalability
  side by side shows every platform trading strength on one axis for
  weakness on another; no platform dominates across the board.
- This reflects genuine physical tradeoffs (isolation vs. coupling
  strength), not simply immature engineering that will resolve itself.
- Application priorities (raw gate count vs. fast iteration vs.
  networking) should drive platform choice, not a single "best" ranking.
