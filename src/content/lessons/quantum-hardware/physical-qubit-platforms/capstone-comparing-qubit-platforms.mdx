## Five Platforms, One Decision

Superconducting circuits, trapped ions, <Term id="neutral-atoms">neutral atoms</Term>, photons and <Term id="spin-qubits">spin
qubits</Term> each looked like the obvious answer inside their own lesson.
That is what reading one platform at a time does to you. Set against
each other on the same five axes, coherence time, gate speed, two-qubit
gate fidelity, connectivity, and scaling path, they stop being five
candidate answers and become one tradeoff surface.

This capstone adds no new physics. The thing to watch as the table and
the two charts below fill in is not which row comes out on top: it is
which pair of columns keeps moving in opposite directions, because that
pattern turns out to have a physical cause, and naming it is what lets
you predict where a platform you have never met will sit.

## The Tradeoff Space, Side by Side

Same five axes, five platforms, one table: this is what a decision
between them rests on.

| Platform | Coherence time | Gate speed | Two-qubit gate fidelity | Connectivity | Scaling path |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Superconducting | ~microseconds | ~nanoseconds (fast) | ~99.5-99.9% | mostly nearest-neighbor | lithographic fabrication, many labs/companies actively scaling |
| Trapped ions | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds (slow) | ~99.9% (best published at or above 99.9%) | all-to-all within a trap | chain length limited; modular/networked traps an active research direction |
| Neutral atoms | ~seconds (long) | ~microseconds | ~99.5% | flexible, reconfigurable geometry | thousands of atoms already trapped (6,100 in one 2025 array); the largest current counts, though trapped-atom count is not yet gate-ready qubit count |
| Photonic | effectively no decoherence in flight; loss-limited instead | very fast per operation | probabilistic fusion gates: succeed or fail heralded, a different regime from a fidelity spec | naturally networked (light travels) | strong for communication/networking; two-qubit gates remain the hard problem |
| Spin qubits | intermediate to long (purified silicon) | ~10-100 ns | ~99%, best published above 99.5% | nearest-neighbor (dot-to-dot) | leverages existing semiconductor fabrication tooling; registers still around ten qubits |

(These are representative orders of magnitude for illustrating the
tradeoff space, drawn from published results through the mid-2020s, not
specifications of any particular device; real numbers vary by
generation and vendor, and continue to improve. Read every figure here
as carrying an implicit date, and go looking for the current one before
quoting any of them in an argument that matters. The
fidelity column especially rewards that caution: it moves fastest, and
the gap between a platform's best published result and its typical
device is often wide. The photonic entry isn't a dodge: a fusion gate
either succeeds or heralds its own failure, so "what fraction succeed"
and "how faithful the survivors are" are two separate questions, and
compressing them into one percentage would misrepresent the platform.)

Five different physical mechanisms for the same underlying job (an
isolated, controllable two-level system) is why the table above has no
row that wins on every column: each structure's own geometry is what
produces its particular coherence/speed/connectivity tradeoff.

### Coherence time, on an actual log scale

The table's "~microseconds" vs. "~seconds" entries aren't just different
adjectives, they're different by up to six orders of magnitude. Plotting
the representative numbers each platform's own lesson already worked
with (bar height is $\log_{10}(\text{seconds})$, shifted up by 6 so every
bar is positive; the caption on each bar is the real value) makes the
size of that gap honest instead of ordinal:

<BarChart
  ariaLabel="Coherence time in seconds, log scale, across four platforms: superconducting around 10 microseconds, spin qubits around 1 millisecond, trapped ions around 1 second, neutral atoms around 13 seconds"
  bars={coherenceTimeBars}
  maxValue={8}
/>

Read the top two bars as the same order of magnitude, not as a ranking.
Their provenance is not matched. The neutral-atom figure is one specific
published result, the 12.6 s hyperfine coherence of the 2025 Caltech
tweezer array; the trapped-ion figure is the conservative round number
this track's worked comparisons use, and single trapped ions have been
held coherent for far longer than a second in dedicated experiments. The
chart is honest about the six-order-of-magnitude gap between the
solid-state platforms and the atomic ones, which is the point it exists
to make. It is not evidence about which of the two atomic platforms
holds a record.

<Term id="photonic-qubits">Photonic qubits</Term> aren't on this chart at all, deliberately. $T_1$/$T_2$
decay is a *rate*, something that happens gradually and can be assigned a
time constant; "no decoherence in flight" describes the absence of that
gradual-decay mechanism entirely, not an unusually long rate the other
four platforms also share. Forcing it onto this axis with some large placeholder number would
misrepresent it as "just longer," when the Photonic Qubits lesson's whole
point is that its dominant error (loss) has a different mathematical
shape entirely.

### Gate speed, the same way

Gate time spans just as many orders of magnitude, in the opposite
direction from coherence: here a taller bar means *faster* (the value
plotted is $-\log_{10}(\text{seconds})$), so the platforms that were tall
on the coherence chart tend to be short here, and vice versa:

<BarChart
  ariaLabel="Gate speed across four platforms, log scale with taller meaning faster: single-qubit gate times for superconducting around 10 nanoseconds, spin qubits around 100 nanoseconds, and neutral atoms around 1 microsecond, plus the two-qubit entangling gate time for trapped ions around 10 microseconds"
  bars={gateTimeBars}
  maxValue={8}
/>

One caution about reading this chart: the bars don't all measure the
same kind of gate, and the captions say which is which. The
superconducting, spin-qubit, and neutral-atom bars are *single-qubit*
gate times; the trapped-ion bar is the platform's *two-qubit* entangling
gate, because that shared-motion step is the operation that sets the
platform's pace (the Trapped Ions lesson noted a single-qubit
ion gate, a direct laser drive with no shared motion involved, runs
considerably faster). Plotting the ion's defining bottleneck rather than
its best case is the honest comparison, but only with the mismatch
labeled.

Photonic is excluded here too, for a related but different reason: a
passive polarization rotation has essentially no drive time at all, while
the platform's real bottleneck, the probabilistic two-photon gate, isn't
clocked the way a Rabi-driven pulse is. There's no single "gate time"
number to plot for a gate that sometimes doesn't happen.

Lay the two charts side by side and the "no free lunch" pattern turns
from assertion into arithmetic: superconducting and spin qubits sit
at the fast end of the gate-speed chart and the short end of the
coherence chart; trapped ions and neutral atoms sit at the opposite end
of both. No platform is tall on both charts at once.

mechanisms behind similar-sounding phrases. Trapped ions get all-to-all
coupling for free from one shared vibrational mode; superconducting
qubits get nearest-neighbor coupling from fixed, lithographically-drawn
wires; neutral atoms get something in between, not an intrinsically

### The general pattern: no free lunch

Every platform trades something for something else: superconducting
qubits trade coherence for speed and fabrication maturity; trapped ions
and neutral atoms trade gate speed for coherence and (for neutral atoms)
flexible geometry; photons trade an entirely different error model
(loss, not decoherence) for a much harder gate mechanism; spin qubits
trade smaller, more standard fabrication for intermediate performance on
every other axis. This isn't a temporary state of immaturity to be
"solved." It reflects a physics tradeoff: strong environmental
isolation, which improves coherence, tends to also weaken the coupling
needed for fast gates.

## Choosing for a Specific Workload

**A research group needs to run a long calculation requiring many
sequential gates, but connectivity between arbitrary qubits matters less
than raw operation count. Which platform's tradeoff profile favors this?**

Trapped ions or neutral atoms: both offer long coherence times, giving a
large "gate budget" (as computed directly in the Trapped Ions lesson's
worked example: $\sim10^5$ gates before decoherence dominates, versus
$\sim10^3$ for a representative superconducting device), favoring
platforms where coherence time, not raw gate speed, is the limiting
resource for this specific priority.

## Practice Questions

1. An application has to distribute entanglement between two
   superconducting processors sitting in different buildings. Using this
   lesson's table, which platform's entry suits the link itself, and what
   has to be added at each end before that link can attach to a
   superconducting processor at all?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **The link is photonic.** The table's photonic row is the only one
   whose connectivity entry says "naturally networked (light travels),"
   and that is the property the job actually needs. Every other row
   encodes its qubit in something bolted to a chip, a trap or a
   refrigerator. A photon is in motion by its nature, its dominant error
   grows with distance flown rather than with time elapsed, and the
   installed telecom fibre between two buildings was built around the
   wavelength where that loss is smallest.

   It is worth being explicit about why the other rows' real strengths do
   not help. A trapped ion's seconds-long coherence is a superb property
   of a stationary qubit, and coherence does not transport a state
   anywhere.
   Superconducting qubits' nanosecond gates shorten the local circuits at
   each end and leave the distance untouched. Spin qubits' density is a
   fabrication property of an endpoint. All three are answers to a
   different question.

   **What has to be added: a transducer at each end.** This is the part
   the table hides, and it is the hard part. A superconducting qubit's
   excitation is a microwave photon at a few gigahertz, living inside a
   10 mK refrigerator. The link carries an optical photon at a few
   hundred terahertz, at room temperature, in a fibre. Nothing connects
   those two by default, so each processor needs a microwave-to-optical
   converter, and the return path needs the reverse.

   Microwave-to-optical transduction is an active research problem
   precisely because current converters are lossy and add noise, and the
   architecture's entanglement-distribution rate is set by that step
   rather than by the fibre. The fibre is the easy, solved part of this
   design. Note also that the conversion has to happen at the cold end
   and its own dissipation lands in the refrigerator's budget, which is
   the wiring-and-cooling problem the Control & Readout course takes up.

   One structural point in the link's favour, since it changes the
   protocol available. Photon loss is heralded: a detector that does not
   fire tells you the attempt failed. So entanglement distribution runs
   as repeat-until-success, and a lossy link costs rate rather than
   correctness. That is a much better failure mode than an error you
   cannot see, and it is why a link with single-digit percent
   transmission is still usable.

   </details>

2. Explain why "strong environmental isolation" (good for coherence)
   tends to work against "fast, strong coupling" (good for gate speed),
   using at least one specific platform's physical mechanism as an
   example. Then say why better shielding does not let a platform escape
   the tradeoff.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because isolation and controllability are the same quantity read
   twice. A qubit's coherence is protected by having only weak couplings
   to degrees of freedom outside itself, and your control field is a
   degree of freedom outside itself. There is no filter that admits the
   drive you want and rejects the bath you do not, because at the level
   of the interaction they are the same kind of term.

   **Trapped ions, worked through.** The qubit lives in two internal
   levels of a single atom, floating in ultra-high vacuum, physically
   touching nothing. Nothing in its surroundings couples to it, which is
   exactly why coherence reaches seconds. Now try to entangle it with
   another ion. There is no wire to run between them and no direct
   interaction between two sets of internal atomic levels micrometres
   apart. The only channel available is indirect: the chain's collective
   mechanical motion, reached through laser sidebands. Excite it
   conditionally on one ion, sense it with the other. It works, and it
   takes microseconds, because you are steering a qubit through its
   own mechanical vibration rather than driving it directly.

   **Superconducting circuits, the other end.** Two transmons can be
   coupled by putting a capacitor between them, which is about as direct
   as coupling gets, and the gates take tens of nanoseconds. The same
   circuit is embedded in a real solid: amorphous oxide near the
   junction, dielectric loss in the substrate, and control lines running
   out to room-temperature electronics. Every one of those is a channel
   too, and $T_1$ comes out in microseconds, drifting between
   calibrations as two-level-system defects move around. The strong
   coupling that makes the gate fast is not accidentally accompanied by
   the loss; it is the same accessibility.

   **Neutral atoms make the tradeoff explicit rather than implicit.**
   Their gate mechanism is to promote an atom to a Rydberg state, whose
   enormous orbit interacts strongly with a neighbour. That is
   deliberately making the qubit far less isolated, for the duration of
   the gate and no longer. The Rydberg state's radiative lifetime and its
   sensitivity to stray fields are the bill, and it arrives exactly when
   the coupling is used. Nowhere is the tradeoff clearer: the platform
   buys coupling by temporarily giving up isolation, and pays in fidelity
   for the window it is given up.

   The wrong turn is imagining that better shielding would let you have
   both, that with enough engineering you could block the noise and leave
   the control channel open. Shielding does help, and it is why filtered,
   attenuated coaxial lines and magnetic shields exist. But it addresses
   noise arriving *through a specific channel you already knew about*.
   The tradeoff described here is about the qubit's intrinsic coupling
   strength to the outside world, and that number appears in both the
   decoherence rate and the gate rate.

   </details>

3. If a new fabrication breakthrough dramatically improved superconducting
   qubits' coherence time without changing anything else, would this
   table's fundamental "no free lunch" pattern disappear? Why or why not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No, and the reason is worth separating into two claims that get
   confused with each other.

   **The numbers would change, and that matters.** A superconducting
   device with, say, a millisecond of coherence at 10 ns gates would have
   a $10^5$ gate budget, matching trapped ions while keeping gates a
   thousand times faster. For a great many workloads that would settle
   the platform question outright. Nothing in this lesson says such an
   improvement is impossible; the lesson says the opposite, that every
   platform's numbers have improved substantially and continue to, and
   warns against reading the table as a permanent specification.

   **The pattern would not disappear, because the table has five columns
   and the pattern is about the whole surface.** Improving one cell in
   one row does not produce a row that wins everywhere. Superconducting
   connectivity would still be nearest-neighbour, fixed at fabrication
   time, so an algorithm needing arbitrary pairs would still pay SWAP
   routing that a trapped-ion chain does not. Neutral atoms would still
   be the only row whose geometry can be redrawn between shots. Photons
   would still be the only carrier that travels, so the networking
   argument of question 1 would be untouched. "No free lunch" is the
   claim that no row dominates every column, and one improved cell leaves
   that claim standing.

   **Now the part that requires care, because the hypothesis is doing
   real work.** The question says the improvement changes *nothing else*,
   and the underlying claim of this lesson is a mechanism, not a
   coincidence: strong isolation improves coherence and weakens the
   coupling that fast gates need. A device that genuinely reached
   seconds of coherence *while keeping* nanosecond gates would be
   evidence against that mechanism, or at least evidence that it does not
   bind as tightly as the current data suggests. So the honest answer is
   conditional. The mechanism has not been repealed by an improvement
   that leaves the coupling strength alone, but a platform that broke the
   isolation-versus-coupling link in a demonstrated way would be a real
   counterexample and should be treated as one.

   The wrong turn is to treat "no free lunch" as a conservation law: some
   metaphysical rule that every gain must be paid for, so improvement is
   futile. That is not a physical statement and it is not what the lesson
   argues. The argument is specific and falsifiable, about isolation and
   coupling strength being two readings of one number, and the right
   response to a breakthrough is to ask which number moved rather than to
   assume something must have gotten worse.

   </details>

4. Which platform's connectivity advantage comes from a shared physical
   mode, and which platform's comes from reconfigurable geometry?
   Explain the difference between these two mechanisms.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Shared physical mode: **trapped ions**. Reconfigurable geometry:
   **neutral atoms**.

   **Trapped ions** get all-to-all coupling from one collective
   vibrational mode of the whole chain. Any ion can excite it and any
   other ion can sense it, so on five ions all ten pairs are available,
   and they are available *within a single shot*, with no dependence on
   how far apart along the chain the two ions sit.

   **Neutral atoms** have no shared bus at all. Rydberg blockade only
   reaches inside the blockade radius, so at any instant the coupling
   graph is whatever the current arrangement of the tweezers makes it,
   which is sparse. What the platform buys instead is the ability to
   *choose that sparse graph* and to change it between runs, because
   every atom sits at its own separately steerable focus.

   **The difference, stated sharply.** Ions have a fixed graph that
   happens to be complete. Atoms have a sparse graph that happens to be
   redrawable. Those are not the same advantage and they do not have the
   same cost.

   The ion's version is free within a shot and degrades with size: more
   ions in one trap means more motional modes, crowded closer together,
   more spectator modes for the drive to touch by accident, and more
   heating, so fidelity and speed both fall as the chain grows.

   The atom's version costs time rather than fidelity. Rearranging the
   array happens between shots, and it is slow compared with the gates,
   so a large neutral-atom machine can return fewer shots per second than
   a small superconducting chip. Nothing degrades inside the shot; you
   simply wait between them.

   The consequence for a compiler is the practical version of this.
   Against ions there is no routing problem: every logical pair maps to a
   physical pair. Against atoms there is a routing problem, but the
   compiler has a primitive no chip-based platform offers, which is to
   physically move a qubit instead of SWAPping its state along a chain.

   The wrong turn is reading "reconfigurable" as a synonym for
   "all-to-all," which quietly upgrades the atom row into the ion row.
   Redrawable is strictly weaker inside any one circuit. It is also worth
   not crediting superconducting qubits with either mechanism: their
   graph is drawn in lithography, so it is neither complete nor
   changeable afterwards, which is exactly why the routing overhead the
   Quantum Software track develops falls hardest on that platform.

## Summary

- Comparing coherence time, gate speed, two-qubit gate fidelity,
  connectivity, and scalability side by side shows every platform
  trading strength on one axis for weakness on another; no platform
  dominates across the board.
- This reflects a physical tradeoff (isolation vs. coupling strength),
  not immature engineering that will resolve itself.
- Application priorities (raw gate count vs. fast iteration vs.
  networking) should drive platform choice, not a single "best" ranking.
