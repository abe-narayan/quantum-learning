## Trapping Without Charge

Trapped ions rely on the ion's electric charge, but that same charge
means ions repel each other, limiting how densely they can be packed in
a single trap. <Term id="neutral-atoms">Neutral atoms</Term> sidestep this entirely.

## Tweezers, Arrays, and the Rydberg Blockade

### Optical tweezers: trapping without charge

A neutral atom has no net charge for a static or oscillating electric
field to grip the way an ion trap does. Instead, **optical tweezers**
use a tightly focused laser beam: an atom is attracted toward the point
of highest light intensity (a consequence of how an atom's induced
electric dipole interacts with the light field), effectively pinning it
at the beam's focus. Because each tweezer is just a separately-steerable
focused laser spot, an experiment can create many independent traps
arranged in essentially any 2D (or even 3D) pattern, a qualitatively
different scaling path from a trapped-ion chain's fixed linear geometry:

Each dot above is one independently-steerable tweezer; the filled ones
show a real array can be loaded sparsely and arbitrarily, in whatever
pattern your experiment calls for.

### Reconfigurable arrays

Since each atom sits in its own independently-controlled optical
tweezer, the *entire array geometry* can be reconfigured between
experiments (or even mid-experiment): atoms can be rearranged, and
individual defects (a trap that failed to load an atom) can sometimes be
corrected by moving a neighboring atom into the gap. This flexibility
has let neutral-atom platforms scale to thousands of trapped atoms, the
largest single-array particle counts of any platform to date. Count
trapped atoms and count computational qubits separately, though: an
array that holds thousands of atoms is not yet an array that runs
thousands of high-fidelity entangling gates.

### Rydberg blockade: how two-qubit gates work

Neutral atoms don't have a shared mechanical mode the way trapped ions
do, so they need a different trick for two-qubit gates. Instead,
two-qubit gates typically use **Rydberg blockade**: briefly
exciting an atom to a highly excited ("Rydberg") state, whose enormously
large electron orbit creates a strong interaction with any *nearby*
atom also being excited, strong enough to shift the neighbor's energy
levels and prevent it from being excited to the same Rydberg state at
the same time. This blockade effect, one atom's excitation
conditionally suppressing its neighbor's, is what implements an
entangling two-qubit gate, conceptually similar in spirit to Quantum
Algorithms I's <Term id="cnot-controlled-gates">controlled-phase gates</Term>, with atomic physics here doing the
work an abstract circuit element does there.

## Reconfigurability as the Real Advantage

Neutral atoms' central engineering advantage isn't raw speed, and it
isn't per-qubit <Term id="coherence">coherence time</Term> alone. It's *scalable, flexible
geometry*: the ability to lay out and rearrange large numbers of
identical, naturally-uniform qubits (since, like trapped ions, they're
real atoms, not fabricated devices that vary chip-to-chip).

This scaling path is not hypothetical. In September 2025 a Caltech group
published, in Nature, a tweezer array holding more than 6,100 cesium
atoms across roughly 12,000 sites, with a hyperfine coherence time of
12.6 s and an imaging survival probability of 99.989% per round. Read
those two numbers for what they are: coherence and non-destructive
imaging at that scale, not entangling-gate fidelity, which the same
paper does not claim. What they do establish is that adding atoms did
not cost qubit quality. That is also why this is the platform with
paying customers today:
QuEra's Aquila system has been cloud-accessible for years, Pasqal sells
neutral-atom systems directly to industrial and government labs in
Europe, and Atom Computing and Harvard/QuEra collaborations have pushed
past 1,000 and then 3,000 atoms in a single array. The
reconfigurable-tweezer argument this lesson makes about scaling is the
argument each of those roadmaps is selling.

### What a tweezer array pays for

Set against that, three costs are real and specific to tweezer arrays:

- **Atoms fall out.** A tweezer holds an atom for seconds, not
  indefinitely, and a trap that loses its atom mid-circuit produces a
  qubit that is neither $|0\rangle$ nor $|1\rangle$ but *absent*. That is
  a leakage error: it takes the qubit outside the computational
  subspace, so a code built to catch bit flips and phase flips cannot
  see it at all until the loss itself is detected. Loaded-site imaging
  and mid-circuit reloading exist to detect and repair it, and both cost
  time.
- **The duty cycle is slow.** Loading, imaging, and rearranging an array
  into the requested geometry happens between shots, and takes far
  longer than the gates do. A neutral-atom device can be enormous and
  still return fewer shots per second than a small superconducting chip.
- **Gate fidelity trails the leaders.** The Rydberg-blockade entangling
  gate is harder to make clean than a trapped ion's motional gate or a
  transmon's coupler, and published two-qubit fidelities for this
  platform have historically sat behind both. Scale and fidelity are
  separate axes; this platform leads on the first, not yet on the
  second.

## Why There Is No Shared Bus Here

**Why can't a plain (non-Rydberg) neutral atom implement a two-qubit
gate as directly as an ion's shared vibrational mode?**

An ordinary (non-excited) neutral atom has essentially no long-range
interaction with its neighbors. That absence of interaction is what made
it easy to trap densely in the first place, but it also means
two ordinary neutral atoms sitting near each other don't naturally
influence one another's quantum state. Rydberg blockade solves this by
*temporarily* creating a strong interaction on demand (via the Rydberg
excitation), where trapped ions rely on an always-on coupling through
their shared vibrational mode.

## Assuming Neutral Means Non-Interacting

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming "neutral" means "no interactions, ever."** Neutral atoms have
no long-range Coulomb interaction, but Rydberg excitation deliberately
creates a strong, controllable interaction on demand. The platform's
entire two-qubit gate mechanism depends on this being possible.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing optical tweezers with a laser used to drive a gate.**
Tweezers are for *trapping/positioning* the atom; gates and readout
generally use separate, differently-tuned lasers. Trapping and
manipulation are distinct engineering functions, even though both use
light.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does an ion trap need electric fields specifically, while a
   neutral atom cannot be trapped the same way?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because an ion trap grips *net charge*, and a neutral atom has none to
   grip.

   The force an electric field exerts on a charge is $\mathbf F=q\mathbf
   E$. That is the entire mechanism a Paul trap runs on, and it is
   linear in the charge. Set $q=0$ and the force is zero, no matter how
   strong the field or how cleverly the electrodes are shaped. There is
   nothing to fix.

   An optical tweezer does something structurally different. Light is
   also an electric field, but instead of pulling on a charge that is
   already there, it *induces* a dipole in the atom, $\mathbf d=\alpha
   \mathbf E$, and then acts on the dipole it just created. The
   interaction energy goes as $U\propto-\alpha\langle E^2\rangle$, which
   is proportional to intensity rather than to field, so the force is a
   gradient of intensity and points toward the brightest spot. Focus a
   beam tightly and the brightest spot is the focus, so that is where the
   atom collects.

   The wrong turn worth naming is "a neutral atom has no charge, so
   electric fields do nothing to it." That is exactly backwards about
   the mechanism actually used. A neutral atom has no net charge but is
   very much polarizable, and the whole platform runs on that
   polarizability. The correct statement is narrower: no *net* charge
   means no first-order force, so trapping has to come from the
   second-order, induced-dipole response instead.

   That "second-order" is not a footnote, because it sets the platform's
   costs. The field must first induce the dipole and then act on it, so
   the trap is weak: tweezer depths are in the millikelvin range, and
   an atom stays for seconds rather than indefinitely. When one leaves,
   the site holds no qubit at all, which is the leakage-style error mode
   the lesson describes.

   A second, related mistake is picturing the beam as *pushing* the
   atom. Radiation pressure does exist and does push, along the beam
   direction. It is not the trapping force. Trapping is the dipole
   gradient force, and a tweezer is engineered around it: the beam is
   held far off resonance on purpose, so that it shifts the atom's
   levels without exciting it, and it is focused tightly enough that
   the intensity gradient at the focus dominates the scattering push.

   </details>

2. What specific engineering advantage do reconfigurable optical-tweezer
   arrays provide that a fixed trapped-ion chain does not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The array's *geometry* is a control parameter, set by where the beams
   are pointed rather than by physics or by fabrication, and it can be
   changed between shots or even mid-experiment.

   In a linear Paul trap the ions' positions are not chosen. They are the
   equilibrium of mutual Coulomb repulsion against the trap's confinement,
   which puts the ions in a line with a spacing the trap dictates. You
   cannot decide that ion 3 should sit beside ion 17. In a tweezer array
   every atom sits at its own separately steerable focus, so any 2D or
   even 3D pattern is available and can be redrawn.

   Three concrete payoffs follow.

   **Defect repair.** Tweezer loading is stochastic: a given site catches
   an atom or does not. Rather than accepting the holes, an experiment
   images the array, sees which sites loaded, and moves atoms into the
   target pattern before the circuit runs. A missing ion in a chain is
   not repairable this way.

   **Layout matched to the circuit.** Because Rydberg blockade is
   distance-limited, which pairs can interact is decided by how far apart
   they are placed. Pairs a circuit needs to entangle go inside the
   blockade radius, pairs that must stay independent go outside it. The
   connectivity graph is drawn to fit the algorithm instead of the
   algorithm being routed onto a fixed graph.

   **Site count.** Tweezer arrays have reached thousands of sites in one
   plane, over 6,100 atoms across roughly 12,000 sites in a 2025 Caltech
   result, while an ion chain gets harder to work with as it lengthens:
   more ions share the trap's motional modes, the modes crowd together,
   and gate fidelity and speed both degrade.

   Two wrong turns are worth flagging, because both overstate the
   advantage in ways the lesson explicitly guards against.

   The first is reading "thousands of atoms" as "thousands of qubits."
   The Caltech result reports coherence and non-destructive imaging at
   that scale. It does not claim entangling-gate fidelity at that scale,
   and the paper does not pretend otherwise. Trapped-atom count and
   gate-ready qubit count are separate numbers.

   The second is reading "reconfigurable" as "all-to-all." It is not the
   same property. A trapped-ion chain genuinely has every edge available
   at once, through the shared mode. A tweezer array has a graph you can
   *redraw between runs*, which is a different and in some ways weaker
   thing: within a single shot the available edges are still whatever the
   current geometry provides. Rearrangement also happens between shots
   and is slow compared with the gates, so a very large neutral-atom
   machine can still return fewer shots per second than a small
   superconducting chip.

   </details>

3. Explain Rydberg blockade in one or two sentences, focusing on what
   happens to a neighboring atom's ability to also become excited. Then
   state the condition, in terms of the interaction strength and the
   exciting laser, that has to hold for the suppression to be complete.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Exciting atom A to a Rydberg state gives it an enormous electron orbit
   and therefore a strong interaction with any nearby atom, and that
   interaction shifts atom B's Rydberg level far enough that the laser
   which was resonant for B no longer reaches it. So while A is excited,
   B cannot be excited to the same state at all: suppressed, not merely
   weakened.

   **The condition.** Write the interaction energy between two Rydberg
   atoms a distance $r$ apart as $V(r)$, and let $\Omega$ be the Rabi
   frequency of the exciting laser. The laser can bridge a detuning of
   roughly $\Omega$. Blockade is complete when the shift it has to bridge
   is larger than that:

   $$
   V(r) \gg \hbar\Omega .
   $$

   The blockade radius $r_b$ is where those two are equal, $V(r_b)=\hbar
   \Omega$. Inside it the shift wins and B stays in its ground state;
   outside it the laser wins and B excites as though A were not there.
   Since $V(r)$ falls off steeply with distance, typically as $1/r^6$ for
   the van der Waals case, the crossover is sharp, which is why the
   lesson's slider shows a threshold rather than a fade.

   Notice what the condition implies about driving harder. A stronger
   Rydberg laser raises $\Omega$, which shrinks $r_b$. Turning up the
   power to get a faster gate shrinks the very radius the gate depends
   on, so drive strength and blockade range trade against each other and
   the array's spacing has to be designed against the drive you intend to
   use.

   Two wrong pictures to discard. The first is mechanical: the huge
   Rydberg orbital does not push B away. Neither atom moves; what moves
   is where B's energy levels sit. The second is global: blockade is not
   a suppression across the whole array. It is radius-limited, and it has
   to be, because a suppression that applied everywhere and always would
   leave no conditional dependence between one specific pair, which is
   the thing the entangling gate is built out of.

   </details>

4. Is Rydberg blockade's interaction always-on (like ions' Coulomb
   repulsion) or switched on deliberately? Why does this distinction
   matter for gate design?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Switched on deliberately. Two ground-state neutral atoms sitting
   micrometres apart have essentially no long-range interaction. The
   strong interaction exists only while an atom is in the Rydberg state,
   and that lasts only as long as the Rydberg laser says so. The coupling
   is created for the gate and destroyed afterwards.

   Ions are the opposite. Coulomb repulsion between two charges cannot be
   turned off, and the shared motional mode it produces is there whether
   the current circuit wants it or not.

   Three consequences for gate design follow, and they cut in both
   directions.

   **Idle qubits are genuinely idle.** With no default coupling, an atom
   not being addressed is not weakly coupled to anything, so there is no
   accumulating spectator phase from its neighbours to undo. A large part
   of trapped-ion gate design is about *not* exciting couplings you did
   not ask for, and about closing the excursion into a mode that is
   always available to be excited. Neutral atoms simply do not have that
   bookkeeping when the Rydberg laser is off.

   **The cost is concentrated into the gate window.** The Rydberg state
   is a genuinely fragile place to keep an atom: it decays radiatively on
   a timescale far shorter than the ground hyperfine coherence time the
   platform advertises, and its enormous orbit that makes the interaction
   strong also makes it very sensitive to stray electric fields. So the
   entangling gate has to finish quickly compared with the Rydberg
   lifetime, and it carries an error budget the idle qubit does not. This
   is a large part of why a platform with seconds of coherence still has
   entangling fidelities trailing trapped ions.

   **Which pairs can interact is a geometry decision.** Because the
   coupling only reaches inside $r_b$, the compiler's connectivity graph
   is set by where the tweezers put the atoms. Combined with question 2,
   that means the coupling graph is chosen twice over: once by placing
   atoms inside or outside the blockade radius, and again by rearranging
   them between shots.

   The wrong turn is treating "no interaction by default" as unqualified
   good news, the same overreach as "neutral means non-interacting."
   Switchable coupling removes the always-on crosstalk problem and buys a
   new one: every entangling operation now requires driving a transition
   to a fragile state and getting back down cleanly, and the fidelity of
   that round trip is the platform's binding constraint.

   </details>

## Summary

- Optical tweezers trap neutral atoms with focused laser light. An ion
  trap's electric fields have nothing to grip here, since a neutral atom
  carries no net charge.
- This enables large, flexible, reconfigurable 2D/3D arrays, a
  different scaling path from trapped ions' linear chains, reaching the
  largest trapped-atom counts of any current platform, which is not the
  same claim as the largest count of gate-ready qubits.
- Two-qubit gates use Rydberg blockade: a temporary, deliberately
  induced strong interaction between neighboring excited atoms, not an
  always-on coupling like trapped ions' shared vibrational mode.
- The bill comes as atom loss (a leakage error, not a bit flip), a slow
  load-image-rearrange cycle between shots, and entangling-gate
  fidelities that have historically trailed both ions and transmons.
