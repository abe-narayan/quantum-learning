## A Qubit You Can Fabricate

A superconducting qubit is, physically, a tiny electrical circuit: an
inductor and capacitor, cooled in a <Term id="dilution-refrigerator">dilution refrigerator</Term> to
millikelvin temperatures (colder than deep space) so that thermal noise
doesn't wash out the delicate quantum states. The engineering challenge
this lesson opens with is Approximation Methods' oscillator, revisited
here as real hardware.

## Anharmonicity From a Josephson Junction

### The Josephson junction: breaking the harmonic spacing

A **Josephson junction**, a thin insulating barrier between two
superconductors, replaces the plain inductor with a circuit element
that's nonlinear by construction, not just approximately so. Physically, the qubit is this whole
circuit, capacitor and junction together, on a chip:

<HardwarePlatformSchematic variant="superconducting" ariaLabel="Superconducting transmon qubit: an LC circuit with a Josephson junction replacing the inductor" />

This nonlinearity makes the energy levels
*unevenly* spaced (anharmonic), so a microwave pulse tuned to the
0→1 gap no longer resonantly drives 1→2 as well. That is what makes an
artificial "atom" out of a circuit, isolating a clean two-level qubit
($|0\rangle,|1\rangle$) from what would otherwise be an infinite
oscillator ladder. Put the two ladders side by side and you can see the
difference directly:

(Illustrative spacing, chosen to show the *pattern* of shrinking gaps at
higher $n$, not a measured device's specific anharmonicity.) In the
plain harmonic case, every gap is identical, so a drive resonant with
0→1 is equally resonant with 1→2. In the transmon case, the 0→1 gap
(highlighted) is measurably different from 1→2, which is what lets a
microwave pulse address only the qubit subspace.

### Coherence time and gate speed

Superconducting qubits' defining tradeoff is relatively short coherence
times (typically microseconds, with the energy-relaxation time $T_1$ and
the coherence time $T_2$ both in this range; through the mid-2020s
transmons commonly reached order 100 μs, and the 10 μs figure this
track's worked comparisons use is a deliberately conservative round
number) but very fast gates
(<Term id="single-qubit-gates">single-qubit gates</Term> typically tens of nanoseconds). The *ratio* of gate
time to coherence time, not either number alone, is what determines
how many operations fit before decoherence dominates; the Noise,
Decoherence & Scaling course develops this tradeoff quantitatively.

This is also the platform every major commercial player bet on first,
specifically because it's a chip. IBM's public roadmap ran from the
127-qubit Eagle processor (2021) through the 1,121-qubit Condor (2023),
then pivoted toward smaller, higher-fidelity "Heron" chips linked
together into modular systems. Qubit count in isolation stopped being
the target. Google went the other way and scaled the <Term id="surface-codes">surface code</Term>
instead of the register. Neither move required inventing new physics;
both are the same lithographic, wire-up-many-identical-circuits argument
this section opened with, playing out at production scale.

<HistoricalMoment date="2024">
Google's Willow chip reports that its surface-code <Term id="logical-error-rate">logical error rate</Term> keeps dropping as the code is scaled up: the first real evidence that error correction on this platform gets easier, not harder, with size.
</HistoricalMoment>

### What this platform is bad at

Every one of those chips still has to be wired. A superconducting qubit
is controlled through coaxial lines running from room-temperature
electronics down through every stage of a dilution refrigerator to a
chip held near 10 mK, and a typical design needs two or three of them
per qubit (drive, readout, and often a flux bias). Cooling power,
physical space in the fridge bore, and the heat each cable conducts
downward are all finite, which is why "how many qubits" and "how many
wires" have become close to the same engineering question (Scaling
Challenges works the numbers).

Two more weaknesses belong in any honest account of the platform:

- **Fabrication spread.** Nominally identical junctions come out of the
  fab with different critical currents, so nominally identical qubits
  come out with different frequencies. Chips therefore need per-qubit
  calibration (Calibration), and unlucky frequency collisions between
  neighbors degrade specific gates on specific pairs.
- **Two-level-system defects.** Amorphous oxides in and around the
  junction host microscopic defects that couple to the qubit and drift
  over hours. A qubit's $T_1$ is not a fixed device constant; it moves
  from one calibration to the next, which is why coherence figures are
  quoted as medians over a chip and a time window.

### Gate time from the Rabi model

A single-qubit gate is implemented by driving the qubit at its resonance
frequency for a carefully timed pulse. In the idealized resonant-drive
model, the population transfer follows the two-level Rabi
formula $P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$. This platform's `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability`,
evaluated with equal energies ($E_i=E_f$, exact resonance), reproduces
this closed form to machine precision (already verified in
Approximation Methods' time-dependent perturbation theory context, now
reused directly for real hardware). A full population transfer
("$\pi$-pulse," implementing an X gate) occurs at
$t=\pi/(2\Omega)$, a computable gate time from a given drive
strength $\Omega$ (the Rabi frequency), not a hardcoded number.

<Callout type="warning">
**A convention to check before quoting numbers from papers.** This text
consistently writes the resonant Rabi oscillation as
$P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t)$, giving $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$. Most labs and
papers instead define the Rabi frequency so that
$P_1(t)=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$, giving $t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$. The physics is
identical; the symbol $\Omega$ differs by a factor of 2 between the two
conventions. Take a Rabi frequency quoted in a paper and plug it
directly into this text's formulas without converting, and every gate
time you compute will be wrong by that factor of 2.
</Callout>

## A Pi Pulse in Ten Nanoseconds

**A superconducting qubit is driven at Rabi frequency
$\Omega=2\pi\times25\text{ MHz}$. How long is a $\pi$-pulse (X gate)?**

$$
t_\pi = \frac{\pi}{2\Omega} = \frac{\pi}{2(2\pi\times25\times10^6)} = \frac1{100\times10^6}\text{ s} = 10\text{ ns}
$$

Confirmed directly: `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0,0,Omega,t)`
at $t=\pi/(2\Omega)$ gives $P_1\approx1.0$ to machine precision. This
platform's own engine, reused unchanged from a purely mathematical
context, correctly predicts a realistic superconducting-qubit gate
time (tens of nanoseconds, matching the "fast gates" claim above).

## Forgetting Why the Ladder Must Be Uneven

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking a plain LC circuit alone makes a good qubit.** Its evenly
spaced levels mean any drive resonant with 0→1 is equally resonant with
every other adjacent transition. The Josephson junction's
anharmonicity is what makes isolating a two-level subspace possible at
all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Comparing platforms by coherence time alone.** A short coherence time
paired with proportionally fast gates can still allow many operations.
It's the ratio (roughly, gate count before decoherence) that matters,
not either number in isolation.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does an evenly-spaced (harmonic) energy ladder make a poor
   two-level qubit, specifically in terms of pulse resonance?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because "resonant with the 0→1 gap" and "resonant with the 1→2 gap"
   are the same statement when the gaps are equal. A drive pulse is a
   tone at one frequency, and what makes it act on a transition is that
   its frequency matches that transition's energy gap. In a harmonic
   ladder $E_1-E_0=E_2-E_1=E_3-E_2=\dots$, so a tone tuned to the bottom
   rung is tuned, with exactly zero detuning, to every rung above it.

   Follow what that does to a would-be X gate. Population driven from
   $|0\rangle$ into $|1\rangle$ does not stop there; the same tone keeps
   pushing it into $|2\rangle$, then $|3\rangle$. Instead of the clean
   two-level Rabi oscillation this lesson's $\pi$-pulse formula assumes,
   the circuit climbs the ladder, and there is no pulse duration at which
   it sits cleanly in $|1\rangle$.

   The wrong turn worth naming is "just use a weaker or a longer pulse,
   so the drive is spectrally narrow enough to pick out only 0→1."
   Spectral selectivity is a real lever, and it is exactly how a
   transmon's anharmonicity gets exploited in practice. But selectivity
   works by *detuning*: a narrow pulse rejects a transition because that
   transition sits some distance away in frequency. In a harmonic ladder
   that distance is zero. Narrowing the pulse does nothing but make the
   gate slower, because there is nothing to narrow away from.

   One more consequence, since it changes what kind of error this is.
   Population that ends up in $|2\rangle$ has left the computational
   subspace entirely. It is not a bit flip and not a phase flip, so it is
   not the kind of error a code built for bit flips and phase flips can
   detect. That is a leakage error, and it is the reason anharmonicity is
   treated as a hard requirement of the platform rather than a
   convenience.

   </details>

2. Using $t_\pi=\pi/(2\Omega)$, find the $\pi$-pulse duration for
   $\Omega=2\pi\times50\text{ MHz}$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   t_\pi = \frac{\pi}{2\Omega} = \frac{\pi}{2(2\pi\times50\times10^6)} = \frac{1}{4\times50\times10^6} = \frac{1}{2\times10^8}\text{ s} = 5\text{ ns}.
   $$

   Do the cancellation before reaching for a calculator: the $\pi$ in the
   numerator kills the $2\pi$ inside $\Omega$, leaving $1/(4f)$ with
   $f=50$ MHz. Checked against the engine,
   `exactTwoLevelTransitionProbability(0, 0, Omega, 5e-9)` returns
   $1.0000000000000004$, so the drive really has moved the full
   population to $|1\rangle$ at that instant.

   Compare with the lesson's worked example: doubling $\Omega$ from
   $2\pi\times25$ MHz to $2\pi\times50$ MHz halved the gate from 10 ns to
   5 ns. $t_\pi$ and $\Omega$ are inversely proportional, which is why
   pushing more microwave power at the chip is a genuine lever for faster
   gates, up to the point where a stronger drive starts reaching the 1→2
   transition that the anharmonicity was supposed to protect.

   The mistake this question is set to catch is dropping the factor of 2
   and computing $t=\pi/\Omega=10$ ns. That is not merely "a bit long."
   Evaluating the engine at $t=\pi/\Omega$ gives $P_1\approx10^{-28}$: the
   state has gone all the way to $|1\rangle$ and all the way back to
   $|0\rangle$. A gate mistuned this way does not degrade gracefully; it
   looks, on a readout histogram, exactly like a pulse that never fired.
   Note that this is also the answer you get by taking a Rabi frequency
   out of a paper written in the other convention
   ($P_1=\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$, $t_\pi=\pi/\Omega$) and feeding it straight
   into this text's formula. Check which convention a number was quoted
   in before you use it.

   </details>

3. What physical role does the Josephson junction play that a plain
   inductor cannot provide?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It is a *nonlinear* inductor, and nonlinearity is precisely what an
   ordinary inductor is defined not to have.

   A plain inductor obeys $\Phi=LI$, a straight line. Combine it with a
   capacitor and the circuit's energy is quadratic in the flux variable,
   which is the definition of a harmonic oscillator, and a harmonic
   oscillator's levels are evenly spaced. This is not a fixable
   imperfection in the fabrication; it is what "linear element" means.
   Any circuit built only from capacitors and linear inductors is
   harmonic, and question 1 already said what that costs.

   A Josephson junction instead has the current-phase relation
   $I=I_c\sin\delta$, and its energy $U=-E_J\cos\delta$. Expand the
   cosine and the quadratic term reproduces an ordinary inductor while
   the quartic and higher terms do not. Those extra terms are what make
   the rungs of the ladder unequal, so the 1→2 gap sits a measurable
   distance from the 0→1 gap and a pulse can be aimed at one and not the
   other.

   Two wrong turns are worth naming, because both are common and both
   sound reasonable.

   The first is "the junction is what makes the circuit
   superconducting." It is not. The capacitor plates, the leads and the
   electrodes on either side of the junction are all superconducting
   already. The junction is a thin insulating barrier interrupting that
   superconductor, and its contribution is the nonlinear current-phase
   relation across the barrier, nothing else.

   The second is subtler: "any nonlinear element would do, so why not a
   nonlinear resistor?" It would indeed break the even spacing, and it
   would also dissipate. Dissipation is an energy channel out of the
   qubit into the rest of the world, which is the same thing as $T_1$
   decay. The Josephson junction is valuable specifically because it is
   the one circuit element that is nonlinear *and* non-dissipative. That
   pairing, not nonlinearity on its own, is what makes the platform
   possible.

   </details>

4. Two devices are proposed. Device A does 10 ns gates and holds
   coherence for 10 μs. Device B does 100 ns gates and holds coherence
   for 500 μs. Which has the larger gate budget, and by what factor?
   Then say why neither the gate-time column nor the coherence-time
   column would have told you on its own.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Device B, by a factor of 5.

   $$
   \frac{10\ \mu\text{s}}{10\text{ ns}} = 10^3 \text{ gates}, \qquad \frac{500\ \mu\text{s}}{100\text{ ns}} = 5\times10^3 \text{ gates}.
   $$

   Now look at what each column said in isolation. On gate time alone,
   Device A wins outright: its gates are ten times faster. On coherence
   time alone, Device B wins outright: it lives fifty times longer. Both
   comparisons are true, and neither of them is the number you care
   about, because the question a circuit actually asks is "how many
   operations fit before the state is gone," and that is a count, not a
   duration.

   The units make the argument by themselves. Coherence time is in
   seconds. Gate time is in seconds. A count of gates is dimensionless,
   so the only combination of the two that can be a gate budget is the
   ratio. This is also why the tempting-looking alternative, coherence
   time *minus* gate time, cannot be right: subtracting two times leaves
   a time, and $10\ \mu\text{s} - 10\text{ ns}$ is still about 10 μs no
   matter how the gates perform.

   The pattern generalizes past these two rows, and the rest of this
   course leans on it. A platform can be a thousand times slower per gate
   and still have the larger budget, provided its coherence time is more
   than a thousand times longer. Trapped Ions works exactly that case:
   about $10^5$ gates against a superconducting device's $10^3$, from a
   gate that is roughly 1000 times slower.

   One honest caveat, because the ratio is a ceiling and not a promise.
   It counts how many gates fit inside the coherence window; it says
   nothing about whether they are accurate enough to be worth running.
   A device can have room for $10^5$ gates and a per-gate error rate that
   makes the answer meaningless well before then, which is the separate
   compounding-error problem Scaling Challenges takes up.

   </details>

## Summary

- Superconducting qubits are engineered nonlinear LC circuits; the
  Josephson junction's anharmonicity isolates a usable two-level
  subspace from an otherwise evenly-spaced oscillator ladder.
- Typical regime: microsecond coherence times, nanosecond gate times,
  meaning fast gates relative to decoherence, a real engineering advantage.
- Gate time follows directly from the Rabi model already built and
  verified in Approximation Methods, reused here unchanged for a genuine
  hardware calculation.
- The costs are real: roughly one microwave line per qubit down into a
  ~10 mK fridge, per-qubit frequency spread from fabrication, and
  drifting two-level-system defects that make $T_1$ a median rather than
  a constant.
