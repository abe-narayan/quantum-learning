## Motivation

Every platform so far, superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral
atoms, encodes a qubit in something that sits still, confined at a
fixed location. A photonic qubit is different in kind: the qubit *is* a
particle of light, and it doesn't sit anywhere. It travels, typically
through optical fiber or free space, at the speed of light.

## Engineering Development

### Encoding a qubit in light

A single photon's **polarization** (the orientation of its oscillating
electric field, horizontal vs. vertical, or left- vs. right-circular)
gives a natural two-level system: $|H\rangle$ and $|V\rangle$ map
directly onto $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$:

<HardwarePlatformSchematic variant="photonic" ariaLabel="Polarization encoding: a photon's horizontal or vertical polarization maps to qubit basis states" />

Alternatively, **path
encoding** uses which of two physical routes (waveguides or fiber paths)
the photon travels; a photon in a superposition of two paths is a
qubit in superposition, directly realizing the same math this platform's
`the-bloch-sphere` and interferometer-based lessons already built,
now with an actual physical interferometer.

### (Nearly) no decoherence, but real loss

A photon traveling through good optical fiber or free space doesn't
interact much with its environment along the way; there's no
thermal bath jostling it the way a solid-state qubit sits in one. This
gives photonic qubits exceptionally long *coherence* times in the usual
sense. The real limiting factor is different: **photon loss**. Fiber
absorption, imperfect optical components, and coupling inefficiencies
mean a photon has some probability of simply not arriving at all, which
functions like a qubit vanishing outright rather than dephasing
gradually. This is a genuinely different failure mode from every
previous platform's gradual $T_1$/$T_2$ decay, and it demands different
engineering solutions (the Noise, Decoherence & Scaling course develops
loss as its own error channel).

### Why two-photon gates are hard

Every previous platform's two-qubit gate relies on some direct physical
interaction: Coulomb-mediated shared motion for ions, Rydberg blockade
for neutral atoms, direct capacitive coupling for superconducting
circuits. Photons, by contrast, essentially don't interact with each
other at all in ordinary optical media (two light beams normally pass
straight through one another). Building an effective photon-photon
interaction requires either exotic nonlinear optical materials
(inefficient with current technology) or clever measurement-based
tricks (using photon detection itself, combined with post-selection, to
implement an effective, probabilistic, two-qubit gate). This is *the*
central engineering challenge specific to photonic quantum computing,
without a clean analogue on any other platform covered so far.

## Physical Interpretation

Photonic qubits' tradeoff is genuinely distinct from every other
platform's speed-vs-coherence axis: essentially perfect coherence in
flight, but a fundamentally hard interaction problem for multi-qubit
gates, plus a qualitatively different error mode (loss, not gradual
decoherence). This is also why photonic qubits are the natural carrier
for quantum communication and networking between separate quantum
computers, even when a different platform (superconducting, ion, atom)
handles the actual computation.

## Worked Example

**Why doesn't a photon's polarization "decohere" the way a
superconducting qubit's state does, even over a long fiber trip?**

Decoherence (Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics' dephasing channel)
requires the qubit to interact, even weakly, with an uncontrolled
environment that gradually randomizes its phase. A photon traveling
through well-isolated fiber largely doesn't interact with anything along
its path; there's no "bath" continuously nudging it the way a solid-state
qubit sits embedded in a noisy physical device. The dominant failure mode
instead is binary: the photon survives to be detected, or it's lost
(absorbed, scattered) along the way, a fundamentally different
statistical structure than gradual dephasing.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming "no decoherence" means "no errors."** Photon loss is a real,
often dominant error source for photonic platforms. It just has a
different mathematical character (binary survive/lost) than gradual
$T_1$/$T_2$ decay.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming photonic two-qubit gates work the same way as matter-qubit
gates, just with light instead.** The lack of a natural photon-photon
interaction is a fundamentally different (and harder) engineering
problem than anything the other platforms in this course face.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Photonic qubits encode information in a photon's polarization or
  path, the only platform in this course where the qubit travels rather
  than sits still.
- Photons barely interact with their environment in transit, giving
  excellent coherence in the usual sense; the dominant error is loss
  (a photon failing to arrive), a qualitatively different failure mode.
- The near-total absence of natural photon-photon interaction makes
  two-qubit gates the platform's defining engineering challenge, without
  a direct analogue among the matter-based platforms.
