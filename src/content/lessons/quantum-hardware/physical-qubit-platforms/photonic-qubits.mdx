## The Qubit That Travels

Every platform so far, superconducting circuits, trapped ions, <Term id="neutral-atoms">neutral
atoms</Term>, encodes a qubit in something that sits still. A <Term id="photonic-qubits">photonic qubit</Term>
travels instead, typically through optical fiber or free space.

## Encoding in Light, and Paying in Loss

### Encoding a qubit in light

A single photon's **polarization** (the orientation of its oscillating
electric field, horizontal vs. vertical, or left- vs. right-circular)
gives a natural two-level system: $|H\rangle$ and $|V\rangle$ map
directly onto $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$:

<HardwarePlatformSchematic variant="photonic" ariaLabel="Polarization encoding: a photon's horizontal or vertical polarization maps to qubit basis states" />

Alternatively, **path
encoding** uses which of two physical routes (waveguides or fiber paths)
the photon travels; a photon in a superposition of two paths is a
qubit in superposition, directly realizing the same math this platform's
`the-bloch-sphere` and interferometer-based lessons already built,
now with an actual physical interferometer.

### No dilution refrigerator required

Every other platform in this course needs serious cooling: millikelvin
temperatures for a superconducting circuit, ultra-high vacuum and laser
cooling for a trapped ion or neutral atom, cryostat-adjacent electronics
for a <Term id="quantum-dot">quantum dot</Term>. A photon needs none of that. The waveguides, beam
splitters, and modulators that create and steer it run at room
temperature, because the photon isn't sitting in a noisy thermal
environment the way a solid-state qubit is; it's just moving through
transparent material. (The highest-efficiency single-photon detectors,
confusingly, often *do* use cryogenic superconducting nanowires, but
that's a readout-stage engineering choice, not a requirement of the
qubit itself the way a dilution refrigerator is for a transmon.) This is
also why a photon is the natural qubit for shipping quantum information
between two computers sitting in different refrigerators, or different
buildings: it's the one carrier built to travel through an uncontrolled
environment without falling apart.

### (Nearly) no decoherence, but real loss

A photon traveling through good optical fiber or free space doesn't
interact much with its environment along the way; there's no
thermal bath jostling it the way a solid-state qubit sits in one. This
gives photonic qubits exceptionally long <Term id="coherence">*coherence* times</Term> in the usual
sense. The real limiting factor is different: **photon loss**. Fiber
absorption, imperfect optical components, and coupling inefficiencies
mean a photon has some probability of simply not arriving at all, which
functions like a qubit vanishing outright rather than dephasing
gradually. This failure mode has a different shape entirely from every
previous platform's gradual <Term id="t1-t2-coherence-times">$T_1$/$T_2$ decay</Term>, and it demands different
engineering solutions (the Noise, Decoherence & Scaling course develops
loss as its own error channel).

### Why two-photon gates are hard

Every previous platform's two-qubit gate relies on some direct physical
interaction: Coulomb-mediated shared motion for ions, Rydberg blockade
for neutral atoms, direct capacitive coupling for superconducting
circuits. Photons, by contrast, essentially don't interact with each
other at all in ordinary optical media (two light beams normally pass
straight through one another). Building an effective photon-photon
interaction requires either exotic nonlinear optical materials
(inefficient with current technology) or clever measurement-based
tricks (using photon detection itself, combined with post-selection, to
implement an effective, probabilistic, two-qubit gate). This is *the*
central engineering challenge specific to photonic quantum computing,
without a clean analogue on any other platform covered so far.

## Loss Instead of Decoherence

Photonic qubits sit on a different axis from every other platform's
speed-vs-coherence tradeoff entirely: essentially perfect coherence in
flight, a hard interaction problem for multi-qubit gates, and a
qualitatively different error mode (loss, not gradual decoherence). This
is also why photonic qubits are the natural carrier for quantum
communication and networking between separate quantum computers, even
when a different platform (superconducting, ion, atom) handles the
actual computation.

Two companies have built entire roadmaps around opposite readings of
that tradeoff. PsiQuantum is betting on "fusion-based" photonic quantum
computing, using huge numbers of comparatively cheap, room-temperature-
compatible photonic components and heavy error correction to compensate
for probabilistic gates, with a stated goal of a utility-scale machine
built inside a conventional chip fab. Xanadu instead builds continuous-
variable photonic processors (Borealis, Aurora) that encode information
in the light field itself rather than single-photon polarization, and
has already run cloud-accessible boson-sampling demonstrations. Both
bets accept the same two-photon-gate problem this lesson just described
as the central cost of doing business; they differ on how much error
correction versus how much probabilistic-gate loss they're willing to
absorb.

## Surviving a Long Fiber

**Why doesn't a photon's polarization "decohere" the way a
superconducting qubit's state does, even over a fiber trip long enough
that you would expect it to?**

Decoherence (Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics' dephasing channel)
requires the qubit to interact, even weakly, with an uncontrolled
environment that gradually randomizes its phase. A photon traveling
through well-isolated fiber largely doesn't interact with anything along
its path; there's no "bath" continuously nudging it the way a solid-state
qubit sits embedded in a noisy physical device. The dominant failure mode
instead is binary: the photon survives to be detected, or it's lost
(absorbed, scattered) along the way. That is a different statistical
structure from gradual dephasing.

**How much of a single-photon signal survives 50 km of telecom fiber?**

Good telecom fiber at its low-loss wavelength attenuates about
0.2 dB/km. Over 50 km, the total loss is:

$$
50\text{ km}\times0.2\text{ dB/km}=10\text{ dB}
\implies P_{\text{survive}}=10^{-10/10}=0.10
$$

Only 10% of photons arrive at all; the other 90% are simply gone, each
one lost at a single random point along the way. Note what the loss
budget depends on: *distance traveled*, not time elapsed. A matter
qubit's $T_1$ clock runs whether or not the qubit goes anywhere, so
its error grows with how long the computation takes; a photon's
dominant error grows with how far it has to fly. That inversion is why
photons excel at links (where distance is the job) and matter qubits
excel at storage (where time is).

## Mistaking Low Decoherence for Low Error

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming "no decoherence" means "no errors."** Photon loss is often
the dominant error source for photonic platforms. It just has a
different mathematical character (binary survive/lost) than gradual
$T_1$/$T_2$ decay, so it will not show up anywhere you look for a
coherence time.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming photonic two-qubit gates work the same way as matter-qubit
gates, just with light instead.** The lack of a natural photon-photon
interaction is a different, and harder, engineering problem than
anything the other platforms in this course face.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Name two physical properties of a photon that can each encode a
   qubit, and state the general requirement a property has to meet before
   it can be used this way.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Polarization** and **path**, the two this lesson works with.
   Polarization uses the orientation of the photon's oscillating electric
   field, mapping $|H\rangle\to|0\rangle$ and $|V\rangle\to|1\rangle$ (or
   equivalently left- and right-circular). Path encoding uses which of
   two waveguides or fibres the photon travels down, so a photon in a
   superposition of two arms of an interferometer is a qubit in
   superposition.

   A third is worth knowing, because real long-distance links use it:
   **time-bin**, where the qubit is which of two arrival time slots the
   photon lands in. Time-bin survives a fibre trip better than
   polarization does, since ordinary fibre slowly rotates polarization as
   the cable warms, bends, and ages.

   **The general requirement.** The property must have two settings that
   are reliably preparable, reliably distinguishable at measurement, and
   preserved in flight, and it has to actually vary from photon to
   photon. That last clause is what rules candidates out. A photon's rest
   mass is not an encoding, and not because it is hard to measure: it is
   zero for every photon there has ever been, so there is no second
   setting to pair against the first. A property that takes one universal
   value carries no information at all.

   The mistake to watch for is confusing "two values I can talk about"
   with "two values this photon could have." Energy, for instance, is a
   real and variable property, and frequency-bin encoding is a genuine
   scheme built on it. Mass is not in that category.

   </details>

2. Why is "loss" a different kind of error from $T_1$/$T_2$
   decoherence, in terms of what happens to the qubit?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Under decoherence the qubit is still there and its state has degraded.
   Under loss the qubit is not there.

   $T_1$ and $T_2$ describe continuous processes. The state drifts toward
   the ground state or toward a mixture, a little more with every passing
   microsecond, and at every instant there is still a two-dimensional
   system holding some density matrix. There is no single moment you can
   point at and call "the error." That is what a time constant describes:
   a rate of gradual degradation.

   Loss is binary and instantaneous. The photon is fully present, in
   whatever state it was prepared in, right up until one random moment
   when it is absorbed or scattered, and after that there is no photon to
   measure. The register has lost a dimension rather than having one
   corrupted. This is why the lesson's two curves have different shapes:
   one fades, the other is flat and then drops.

   Three consequences follow from that structural difference, and they
   are the reason the distinction is worth insisting on.

   **It is not a bit flip or a phase flip.** A code built to detect $X$
   and $Z$ errors is looking for a qubit that came back wrong. A lost
   photon does not come back at all, so it is invisible to that code
   until the loss itself is detected. In the standard vocabulary this is
   a leakage error: the state has left the computational subspace
   entirely.

   **But its location is known, and that helps enormously.** A detector
   that fires nowhere tells you *which* qubit went missing, which makes
   loss an erasure error rather than an error at an unknown position.
   Erasures are much cheaper to correct than unlocated errors, with
   substantially higher fault-tolerance thresholds, and photonic
   architectures are designed around exactly this. The property that
   looks like the platform's weakness is partly recovered by being
   self-announcing.

   **The clock is different.** A matter qubit's $T_1$ runs on time
   elapsed, whether or not the qubit goes anywhere. A photon's loss
   budget runs on distance flown: about 0.2 dB per kilometre in good
   telecom fibre, so 50 km costs 10 dB and $10^{-10/10}=10\%$ of photons
   arrive, while 100 km costs 20 dB and 1% arrive.

   The wrong turn is the one the lesson calls out directly: reading "no
   decoherence" as "no errors," and then looking for the platform's error
   budget where a coherence time would be. It is not there and never will
   be. The number that governs a photonic link is a transmission
   probability, and a system characterized only by its coherence time has
   not been characterized at all.

   </details>

3. Why don't two ordinary light beams naturally interact the way two
   trapped ions' motion or two neutral atoms' Rydberg states do?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because Maxwell's equations in vacuum are linear, and a photon carries
   no charge for another photon to act on.

   Linearity is the whole story at the classical level: if two field
   configurations are each solutions, their sum is a solution, so two
   beams superpose and then continue on their original paths as though
   nothing had happened. Nothing in that structure lets one beam change
   what the other does. Every other platform in this course has a
   handle that light lacks. An ion carries charge, so it pushes on its
   neighbour by Coulomb repulsion, and the shared motion that produces is
   the bus a two-qubit gate rides. A Rydberg atom has an enormous induced
   dipole, giving a strong dipole-dipole interaction on demand. Two
   transmons can be capacitively coupled by wiring them together on the
   chip. Photons have no charge, no dipole moment and no wire.

   Being precise: photon-photon scattering is not exactly zero. Quantum
   electrodynamics allows it through a virtual electron-positron loop.
   The cross-section at optical energies is so small that it has never
   been a resource anyone could build a gate from, and treating it as
   "zero for engineering purposes" is the right call. Nonlinear optical
   media can also mediate an effective interaction through a $\chi^{(3)}$
   response, and at high beam intensities that is routine technology. At
   the level of two *single* photons the induced phase shift in ordinary
   materials falls orders of magnitude short of the $\pi$ a controlled-Z
   would need.

   The seductive wrong answer is "but two beams do interact, that is what
   interference is." Interference is precisely the thing linearity
   permits: amplitudes add, and a beamsplitter redistributes them.
   Passing two photons through a beamsplitter and seeing them bunch, the
   Hong-Ou-Mandel effect, is a genuine quantum phenomenon and still pure
   linear optics. Nothing there is a nonlinearity, and linear optics
   alone cannot implement a deterministic entangling gate on two
   independent photonic qubits.

   What linear optics *can* do is give an entangling gate that works
   sometimes. Add photon detection and post-selection, keep the runs
   where the detectors fired in the right pattern and discard the rest,
   and measurement supplies the effective nonlinearity the medium would
   not. This is why the platform's entry in the capstone's comparison
   table is "probabilistic fusion gates: succeed or fail heralded" rather
   than a fidelity percentage. "What fraction succeed" and "how good are
   the survivors" are two separate questions here, and a single number
   would hide one of them.

   </details>

4. Why might a hybrid architecture use photons specifically for
   communication between separate quantum processors, even if those
   processors themselves use superconducting or ion qubits?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a link's job is transmission, and transmission is the one task
   where every property that makes photons awkward computers is either
   irrelevant or an advantage.

   **The carrier is already in motion.** Every other platform's qubit is
   bolted into a trap, a fridge or a chip. Moving one to the next
   building is not an operation the hardware supports. A photon travels
   at $c$ by its nature and needs no environment to live in.

   **The error clock matches the task.** A matter qubit's $T_1$ runs on
   elapsed time whether or not it goes anywhere, so carrying quantum
   information across a campus in a transmon would spend coherence for
   the whole journey. A photon's dominant error grows with distance
   flown. That is exactly the axis a link is measured on, and it is the
   inversion the lesson draws out: photons excel where distance is the
   job, matter qubits excel where time is.

   **The infrastructure exists.** The 0.2 dB/km figure is for the telecom
   wavelength that the world's installed fibre was built around.

   **Failures announce themselves.** Because loss is heralded, a link
   attempt that failed is known to have failed. Entanglement distribution
   can therefore be run as repeat-until-success: try, check, keep the
   successes. That protocol is unavailable for an error you cannot see.

   **The platform's hardest problem is off the critical path.** Photonic
   quantum *computing* is hard because two-photon gates are hard. A link
   does not need a two-photon gate. It needs a photon to arrive, so the
   weakness never comes up.

   The hybrid then reads as each layer doing what it is best at:
   superconducting or ion qubits for computation, where strong couplings
   and fast or high-fidelity gates live, and photons for the links
   between them.

   One honest complication, since a hybrid is not free. Joining the two
   layers requires converting a stationary qubit's excitation into a
   flying photon and back, and for a superconducting processor that means
   moving between microwave and optical frequencies. Microwave-to-optical
   transduction is an active research problem because current
   converters are lossy and noisy, and the whole architecture's link rate
   is set by that step.

   The wrong turn is running the argument one step further into "so
   photons should be the computer too, since their coherence is
   effectively perfect." The absence of interaction that makes a photon
   an excellent courier is the same absence that makes it a poor gate
   partner. It is one physical fact, and it reads as a strength or a
   weakness depending only on which job you hand it.

   </details>

## Summary

- Photonic qubits encode information in a photon's polarization or
  path, the only platform in this course where the qubit travels rather
  than sits still.
- The photon and most of the optics around it run at room temperature,
  the only platform in this course that doesn't need a dilution
  refrigerator or laser-cooled vacuum chamber for the qubit itself.
- Photons barely interact with their environment in transit, giving
  excellent coherence in the usual sense; the dominant error is loss
  (a photon failing to arrive), a qualitatively different failure mode.
- The near-total absence of natural photon-photon interaction makes
  two-qubit gates the platform's defining engineering challenge, without
  a direct analogue among the matter-based platforms.
