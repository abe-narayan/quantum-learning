## Motivation

A modest 50-logical-qubit algorithm, protected the way the threshold
theorem requires, can cost on the order of 50,000 physical qubits, a
thousand-to-one tax paid before a single "useful" qubit does any work.
Every noise source this course covered, sources, T1/T2, crosstalk,
compounding error, feeds directly into that tax. This capstone-style
lesson connects them to the concrete physical-to-logical overhead real
hardware roadmaps target, and is honest about how far current devices
are from paying it off.

## Intuition

Error correction's promise sounds almost free: encode a logical qubit
redundantly across several physical ones, and the logical error rate
drops below the physical error rate. What that promise doesn't
advertise up front is the exchange rate, how many physical qubits it
costs per logical qubit, and that number is the entire roadmap question
this lesson closes the course with. Further down, a slider lets you
sweep across a realistic range of exchange rates and watch the
physical-qubit bill move.

## Synthesis

### From physical error rate to logical qubits

Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's threshold theorem says: below a
critical physical error rate, encoding into logical qubits (using
redundant physical qubits per logical one) can suppress logical error
rate arbitrarily. The catch, which that course's honest-scope framing
already flagged, is that the overhead is large. Depending on the code and
target logical error rate, estimates commonly range from the low hundreds
to several thousand physical qubits per logical qubit for full fault
tolerance at a useful scale, a genuinely large multiplier, not a minor
implementation detail.

Concretely, a surface-code-style logical qubit is built from a lattice of
physical **data qubits** interleaved with **ancilla (syndrome)
qubits** that repeatedly measure error syndromes without disturbing the
encoded information. The small patch below makes the physical-to-logical
overhead visual, though it's illustrative, not to scale: a real
fault-tolerant logical qubit needs the low-hundreds-to-thousands overhead
just described, not the handful of physical qubits pictured here.

### The NISQ era

**NISQ** (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) names the current era
honestly: devices with tens to a few thousand physical qubits, without
full error correction, whose usefulness is bounded by exactly this
course's compounding-error problem (Scaling Challenges). NISQ-era
algorithms (Quantum Algorithms II's VQE and QAOA are exactly this
category) are specifically designed to extract value from shallow
circuits, few enough sequential gates that compounding error stays
tolerable, rather than assuming fault tolerance is already available.

### What "fault tolerant" actually targets

A fault-tolerant quantum computer isn't simply "a quantum computer with
more qubits." It's a device where logical error rates are suppressed
far enough below physical error rates that arbitrarily long, deep
computations become reliable, via the specific error-correction overhead
mechanism above. This is a qualitatively different target from
NISQ-scale devices, not merely a bigger version of one.

## Honest Scope Statement

This course covered noise sources, T1/T2, crosstalk, and compounding
error quantitatively; it did not derive the threshold theorem itself
(Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's scope) or attempt to predict a
timeline for reaching fault tolerance, genuinely active, evolving
engineering questions across the entire field, not settled facts this
platform can state with false precision.

For a sense of what one real, published target currently looks like:
IBM's public hardware roadmap names a specific planned system, Quantum
Starling, targeted for 2029 with 200 logical qubits capable of roughly
100 million quantum operations, built on quantum LDPC codes the company
says can cut physical-to-logical qubit overhead by up to 90% relative to
the surface code this lesson's diagram illustrates. Whether that
timeline holds is exactly the kind of prediction this lesson declines to
make; it's included only to show concretely what one company's current,
stated roadmap target looks like, not as a platform-endorsed forecast.

## Worked Example

**A target logical error rate requires roughly 1000 physical qubits per
logical qubit (a representative, illustrative estimate). How many
physical qubits would a 50-logical-qubit fault-tolerant algorithm need?**

$$
50 \times 1000 = 50{,}000 \text{ physical qubits}
$$

Dramatically more than any current device's qubit count, illustrating
concretely why fault-tolerant quantum computing at a useful scale is
described as a longer-term target rather than an immediate one.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Conflating "large qubit count" with "fault tolerant."** As the worked
example shows, a fault-tolerant algorithm's PHYSICAL qubit requirement
can be orders of magnitude larger than its LOGICAL qubit count. A
1000-physical-qubit NISQ device and a 1000-physical-qubit slice of a
fault-tolerant system are not the same kind of thing.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating NISQ as a permanent limitation rather than a named era.**
NISQ describes the CURRENT state of the field honestly. It's a
description of where hardware is now, not a claim about where it must
remain.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The threshold theorem's error-correction overhead (commonly estimated
  in the hundreds to low thousands of physical qubits per logical qubit)
  is the concrete target real hardware roadmaps are built around.
- NISQ honestly names the current era: noisy, intermediate-scale devices
  without full error correction, whose usefulness is bounded by this
  course's compounding-error problem.
- Fault tolerance is a qualitatively different target from "more
  qubits." It requires suppressing logical error rates far below
  physical ones, at real and substantial physical-qubit overhead cost.
