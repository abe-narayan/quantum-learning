## Beyond the Press-Release Number

Every time a hardware company announces a new chip, the press release
leads with two numbers: <Term id="t1-t2-coherence-times">T1 and T2</Term>, usually in microseconds for
superconducting devices, stretching all the way to seconds for the
hyperfine qubits of trapped ions and neutral atoms alike. Reporters
repeat them, and competitors get
compared by them. Physical Qubit Platforms and Advanced
Topics in Quantum Mechanics already built everything needed to define
both numbers precisely and compute with them directly, so this lesson
turns them from a spec-sheet line into something you can reason about
the moment you see it quoted.

## Two Decay Constants, and the Bound Between Them

### T1: energy relaxation

**T1** is the exponential time constant for a qubit prepared in
$|1\rangle$ to decay toward $|0\rangle$: $P_1(t)=e^{-t/T_1}$. This is
exactly `amplitudeDampingChannel`'s continuous-time limit. Connecting the
discrete channel to this continuous law uses
`decayProbabilityForTimestep(T1,dt)`$=1-e^{-dt/T_1}$ as the per-step
damping probability $\gamma$, chosen so that $N$ repeated applications
over total time $t=N\,dt$ give $(1-\gamma)^N=e^{-N\,dt/T_1}=e^{-t/T_1}$
**exactly**, for any step count $N$ (not merely as a fine-stepping
approximation; this platform's test suite confirms $N=10$, $100$, and
$1000$ all give $P_1(T_1)=e^{-1}$ to 9 decimal places).

### T2: the total coherence time

**T2** is the analogous time constant for off-diagonal <Term id="coherence">coherence</Term> decay:
$|\rho_{01}(t)|=|\rho_{01}(0)|\,e^{-t/T_2}$. Reproducing that curve on
its own takes one channel, `dephasingChannel` fed
`decayProbabilityForTimestep(T2,dt)` as its per-step probability
$\lambda$, since the channel multiplies $\rho_{01}$ by $1-\lambda$ per
step and $(1-\lambda)^N=e^{-t/T_2}$ exactly.

Read that as a phenomenological shortcut rather than a decomposition,
because it is one. `dephasingChannel` models *pure* dephasing, whose own
time constant is $T_\varphi$, and $T_2$ is not $T_\varphi$: the next
section is about the difference. Feeding $T_2$ into the pure-dephasing
channel reproduces the observed coherence envelope only when that
channel is acting alone. Compose it with `amplitudeDampingChannel` at
$T_1$ and the relaxation contributes its own coherence loss on top, so
the pair decays faster than $e^{-t/T_2}$. To build both mechanisms at
once, feed $T_1$ to the damping channel and $T_\varphi$, not $T_2$, to
the dephasing channel.

### The T2 ≤ 2T1 bound

Two separate mechanisms destroy coherence, and it is worth naming them
apart before combining them. T1 processes (energy relaxation) *also*
randomize phase as a side effect: losing populational information
necessarily disturbs coherence too. Pure dephasing, with its own time
constant $T_\varphi$, scrambles the relative phase without any energy
leaving the qubit at all.

$T_2$ is not one of those two mechanisms. It is the rate at which the
coherence actually decays once both are acting, so the two rates add:

$$
\frac{1}{T_2}=\frac{1}{2T_1}+\frac{1}{T_\varphi}.
$$

The factor of 2 on the first term is the asymmetry above, written down:
energy relaxation costs coherence at half the rate it costs population.
Reading the bound off that equation now takes no argument at all. The
$1/T_\varphi$ term cannot be negative, so $1/T_2\geq1/(2T_1)$, which is
$T_2\leq2T_1$. Coherence can never outlast twice the energy-relaxation
time, and it can fall far short of it when $T_\varphi$ is small.

The two limiting cases have names. A device with $T_2$ close to $2T_1$
is "T1-limited": $T_\varphi$ is long enough to contribute almost
nothing, and the only way to improve coherence is to improve $T_1$. One
with $T_2\ll2T_1$ is "T2-limited", meaning pure dephasing dominates the
sum. Watch the wording in the literature here, because "T2 processes" is
a common shorthand for pure dephasing and it is the same word doing two
jobs: $T_2$ is the total, $T_\varphi$ is the one mechanism.

The bound holds under Markovian (Lindblad) dynamics, where both decays
are exponential, which is the regime in which $T_1$ and $T_2$ are
defined as rates at all. On real hardware that caveat has teeth: the
flux noise, charge noise, and TLS defects catalogued in Sources of Noise
are all weighted toward low frequencies, and against a bath the qubit
effectively remembers, the coherence envelope bends away from a pure
exponential toward a Gaussian. A "$T_2$" extracted from such a curve is
a fit parameter belonging to a particular pulse sequence, not a rate,
and setting it against $2T_1$ compares two different kinds of number.

## Not Two Free Parameters

T1 and T2 aren't independent free parameters a device designer can set
arbitrarily. They reflect two physically distinct (if related)
processes: T1 requires an energy-exchanging coupling to the environment
(the qubit must be able to release energy somewhere); T2 requires only a
fluctuating, energy-neutral perturbation (a randomly shifting local
field is enough). This is why realistic devices often report $T_2<2T_1$
substantially; dephasing processes with no direct T1 analogue are
common (Sources of Noise's flux-noise example).

Bloch's definitions carry over without amendment: "T1-weighted" and
"T2-weighted" are still the names of standard clinical scan types.
Nothing about them changed on the way to a superconducting datasheet;
only the system being measured did.

<Callout type="note">
**Reading a T1/T2 number in a press release.** A quoted T1 or T2 is a
time constant, not a "the qubit is dead here" cutoff: at $t=T_1$, 37%
($e^{-1}$) of the population is still there, not zero. Two numbers only
mean something read together: is $T_2$ close to $2T_1$ (T1-limited,
dephasing is well under control) or well below it (T2-limited, some
extra dephasing source is eating coherence)? And it's worth asking how
the number was measured: a single best qubit on the chip, or an average
across all of them, and over what run of the device. A headline T1 from
one cherry-picked qubit is a different claim than a chip-wide median.
</Callout>

## Reading a Device From Its Two Numbers

**A device has $T_1=80\mu s$. What is the absolute maximum possible
$T_2$, and what does it mean if the measured $T_2$ is only $30\mu s$?**

Maximum: $T_2\leq2T_1=160\mu s$. A measured $T_2=30\mu s$, far below
this ceiling, indicates the device is strongly **T2-limited**: some
additional pure-dephasing process (beyond whatever contributes to T1) is
the dominant coherence-loss mechanism, and improving T1 alone would do
very little for overall coherence. The engineering priority should be
identifying and suppressing the extra dephasing source instead.

## Treating T1 and T2 as Unrelated

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming T1 and T2 are independent, unrelated numbers.** They're
physically related via $T_2\leq2T_1$. Where both decays really are
exponential, a reported $T_2$ above that ceiling indicates a measurement
or definition error rather than a better device; where they are not, the
first question to ask is what was fit, and to what.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming decayProbabilityForTimestep's exact match to $e^{-t/T}$ is
merely a good approximation.** It's an *exact* identity, for any step
count. The telescoping product
$(1-\gamma)^N=(e^{-dt/T})^N=e^{-Ndt/T}$ has no approximation error at
all, unlike (for example) the WKB approximation from Approximation
Methods, which is only approximate.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $T_1=100\mu s$, what per-step damping probability $\gamma$
   corresponds to a $dt=1\mu s$ timestep? Then show that applying the
   channel with that $\gamma$ over one full $T_1$ reproduces $e^{-1}$
   exactly, rather than approximately.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \gamma = 1-e^{-dt/T_1} = 1-e^{-1/100} \approx 0.00995017,
   $$

   just under 1% per step. `decayProbabilityForTimestep(100, 1)` returns
   $0.009950166250831893$.

   Note it is *not* $dt/T_1=0.01$. That is the linearization, and it sits
   about $5\times10^{-5}$ high. Small, and using it would break the exact
   identity below, which is the whole reason the formula is written with
   the exponential.

   **The exactness.** Over one full $T_1$ the channel is applied
   $N=T_1/dt=100$ times, and the surviving population is the product of
   100 independent survivals:

   $$
   (1-\gamma)^N = \left(e^{-dt/T_1}\right)^N = e^{-N\,dt/T_1} = e^{-T_1/T_1} = e^{-1}.
   $$

   The exponentials telescope. There is no truncation, no small-$dt$
   assumption and no error term, so the answer does not improve as you
   step more finely because it was never wrong. Running the real Kraus
   channel confirms it: `applyChannelRepeatedly` on $|1\rangle$ with
   amplitude damping gives $P_1$ after one $T_1$ of

   $0.3678794411714424$ at $N=10$,
   $0.367879441171443$ at $N=100$,
   $0.3678794411714576$ at $N=1000$,

   against $e^{-1}=0.36787944117144233$. All three agree to about nine
   decimal places, and the residual differences are floating-point noise
   that grows very slightly with the number of multiplications, not a
   discretization error that shrinks with smaller steps. Notice the
   $N=1000$ result is marginally *further* from $e^{-1}$ than $N=10$,
   which is the signature of accumulated rounding rather than of an
   approximation converging.

   Two wrong turns. The first is treating this as the usual
   "fine-stepping converges to the continuous limit" story, which would
   predict the opposite trend. The second is reading $\gamma$ as a rate.
   It is a probability per step, dimensionless and capped at 1; the rate
   is $1/T_1$, and the two coincide only to first order in $dt/T_1$.

   </details>

2. If a device's measured $T_2$ equals exactly $2T_1$, what does this
   say about its dominant decoherence mechanism?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   That there is no pure dephasing at all: the only thing destroying
   coherence is the energy-relaxation process itself. The device is as
   $T_1$-limited as it is possible to be.

   Within the Markovian picture where both decays really are exponential,
   the two coherence-destroying contributions add as rates:

   $$
   \frac{1}{T_2} = \frac{1}{2T_1} + \frac{1}{T_\varphi},
   $$

   where $T_\varphi$ is the pure-dephasing time. The first term is the
   phase disruption forced by relaxation, which question 4 derives; the
   second is everything else, the fluctuating fields that randomize phase
   without moving energy. Setting $T_2=2T_1$ forces $1/T_\varphi=0$: the
   second channel contributes nothing.

   **The engineering consequence is the reason anyone cares.** On a
   device at the ceiling, effort spent on the usual dephasing remedies is
   wasted. Better magnetic shielding, quieter flux lines, longer
   dynamical-decoupling sequences, all of them attack $T_\varphi$, and
   $T_\varphi$ is already infinite. The only route to longer coherence is
   a longer $T_1$: better materials, fewer two-level-system defects, a
   Purcell filter on the readout path. That is exactly the diagnosis the
   two numbers exist to give you, and it is why the lesson insists they
   only mean something read together.

   Three caveats, because "exactly $2T_1$" is a limit rather than a
   measurement.

   Real devices report $T_2$ at the ceiling *within error bars*. No
   physical device has literally zero pure dephasing.

   A reported $T_2$ *above* $2T_1$ is not a better device. Where both
   decays are genuinely exponential it indicates a measurement or
   definition problem; where they are not, the first question is what
   was fit, and to what. Against the low-frequency-weighted flux and
   charge noise this course catalogues, the coherence envelope bends away
   from exponential toward Gaussian, and a "$T_2$" pulled off such a
   curve is a fit parameter belonging to one pulse sequence, not a rate
   that can be set against $2T_1$ at all.

   Which raises the practical question of *which* $T_2$ is being quoted.
   A Ramsey measurement with no refocusing gives $T_2^*$, which includes
   slow, quasi-static field variation and is typically much shorter. A
   Hahn echo refocuses that slow component and gives a longer $T_2$. A
   device quoted at the $2T_1$ ceiling is almost certainly quoting an
   echo number, and comparing it against someone else's $T_2^*$ compares
   two different experiments.

   </details>

3. Using the worked example's numbers, would doubling $T_1$ (to $160\mu s$)
   necessarily double the measured $T_2$? Why or why not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **No.** On the worked example's device, $T_2$ would go from 30 μs to
   about 33 μs: a 10% improvement, not a 100% one.

   $T_2\leq2T_1$ is a *ceiling*, not a proportionality. The worked example
   has $T_1=80\ \mu$s and a measured $T_2=30\ \mu$s, which sits far below
   the existing $160\ \mu$s ceiling. Raising the ceiling to $320\ \mu$s
   does nothing on its own, because the ceiling was not what was binding.

   Working it out with the rate decomposition makes the size of the
   effect concrete. From the original numbers:

   $$
   \frac{1}{T_\varphi} = \frac{1}{T_2}-\frac{1}{2T_1} = \frac{1}{30}-\frac{1}{160} \approx 0.027083\ \mu\text{s}^{-1}, \qquad T_\varphi \approx 36.9\ \mu\text{s}.
   $$

   The pure-dephasing process is untouched by whatever improved $T_1$, so
   with $T_1=160\ \mu$s:

   $$
   \frac{1}{T_2'} = \frac{1}{320}+0.027083 = 0.030208 \implies T_2' \approx 33.1\ \mu\text{s},
   $$

   a gain of 10.3%.

   The limiting case is the clearest statement of the situation. Push
   $T_1$ to infinity and $T_2$ saturates at $T_\varphi\approx36.9\ \mu$s.
   Perfect energy relaxation buys a 23% improvement in coherence on this
   device and not one microsecond more. The bottleneck is the extra
   dephasing source, and until it is found and suppressed, $T_1$ work is
   the wrong project.

   The wrong turn is reading the inequality as an equality in disguise,
   as though $T_2$ tracked $2T_1$ and simply fell short by some fixed
   inefficiency. It does not track it at all. $T_1$ and $T_2$ come from
   physically different requirements: relaxation needs an
   energy-exchanging coupling to the environment, while pure dephasing
   needs only a fluctuating, energy-neutral field. There is no reason for
   the second to improve when the first does, and on real hardware it
   usually does not.

   One hypothesis this argument leans on, and should not be dropped: the
   rates add like this in the Markovian regime, where both decays are
   exponential and $T_1$ and $T_2$ are rates at all. Against a
   slow, correlated bath the arithmetic above is not the right model, and
   the honest answer becomes "no, and the numbers cannot be combined this
   way either."

   </details>

4. Explain physically why T1 processes necessarily disturb phase
   coherence too, while pure dephasing ($T_\varphi$) processes need not
   involve any energy exchange. Then show how that asymmetry produces
   the factor of 2 in $T_2\leq2T_1$ specifically.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **The direction that is forced.** Coherence is the off-diagonal
   element $\rho_{01}$, and for any legitimate density matrix positivity
   requires

   $$
   |\rho_{01}| \le \sqrt{\rho_{00}\,\rho_{11}} .
   $$

   Coherence between two levels is bounded by the populations of those
   two levels. So as relaxation empties $|1\rangle$ and $\rho_{11}\to0$,
   $\rho_{01}$ is dragged to zero with it whether any phase-randomizing
   process is present or not. You cannot hold a superposition of two
   things when one of them is no longer occupied.

   That inequality also produces the factor of two, which is worth seeing
   rather than memorizing. With $\rho_{11}(t)\propto e^{-t/T_1}$ and
   $\rho_{00}\le1$,

   $$
   |\rho_{01}(t)| \le \sqrt{e^{-t/T_1}} = e^{-t/(2T_1)},
   $$

   so the coherence envelope is capped by a decay with time constant
   $2T_1$. If the coherence really is exponential with constant $T_2$,
   that cap says $T_2\le2T_1$ directly. The square root is the whole
   origin of the 2.

   **Why physically, and not just algebraically.** A $T_1$ event is the
   emission of a real energy quantum into the environment. Something out
   there now holds a record of which branch of the superposition the
   qubit was in, and a which-path record in the environment is
   decoherence, whether or not anyone reads it. Relaxation is, in effect,
   a measurement performed by the surroundings.

   **The direction that is not forced.** Pure dephasing needs nothing of
   the sort. A fluctuating local field, stray flux for a superconducting
   qubit, a wandering magnetic field for an ion, a nuclear-spin bath for
   a spin qubit, shifts the qubit's level splitting slightly up and down.
   The accumulated relative phase between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$
   becomes random. No quantum is exchanged, and the populations
   $\rho_{00}$ and $\rho_{11}$ are untouched, which is exactly what the
   lesson's explorer shows when dephasing leaves $z$ fixed and shrinks
   only $x$ and $y$. Coherence can be destroyed with no energy budget at
   all.

   **So the implication runs one way.** Energy relaxation implies phase
   disturbance; phase disturbance does not imply energy relaxation. A
   one-way implication produces a one-sided inequality, which is why the
   relation is $T_2\le2T_1$ and not $T_2=2T_1$, and why $T_2$ is free to
   fall arbitrarily far below the ceiling while never rising above it.

   The wrong turn is reading $T_2\le2T_1$ as a rough empirical
   observation about typical devices, and therefore expecting $T_2$ to
   sit near $2T_1$ most of the time. It is a bound, its cause is the
   positivity constraint above, and real devices are usually well under
   it because dephasing sources with no $T_1$ counterpart are common.
   Both statements need the Markovian, exponential-decay setting to even
   be comparable, which is the hypothesis this lesson attaches to the
   bound and which is not optional.

   </details>

## Summary

- T1 (energy relaxation) is the continuous-time limit of
  `amplitudeDampingChannel`, and coherence decay is `dephasingChannel`'s,
  connected exactly (not approximately) via
  `decayProbabilityForTimestep`. T2 is the *total* coherence time, not a
  mechanism: the mechanism with its own channel is pure dephasing, at
  $T_\varphi$.
- The bound $T_2\leq2T_1$ reflects that energy relaxation necessarily
  disturbs phase, while pure dephasing needs no energy exchange.
- A device's $T_2/T_1$ ratio diagnoses whether it's T1-limited or
  T2-limited, an actionable engineering distinction.
- A quoted T1 or T2 is a time constant (37% remaining at $t=T$), not a
  hard cutoff, and it's only fully meaningful alongside the other number
  and a sense of how and over how many qubits it was measured.
