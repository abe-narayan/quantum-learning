## What Actually Limits Two-Qubit Fidelity

Ask a hardware team what limits their two-qubit <Term id="gate-fidelity">gate fidelity</Term>. Once the
chip gets past a handful of qubits, <Term id="t1-t2-coherence-times">T1/T2</Term> usually isn't the first
answer; <Term id="crosstalk">crosstalk</Term> is. Every noise source this course has covered so
far, T1 and T2, lives on one qubit coupled to its own environment.
Crosstalk breaks that picture: there's no single-qubit version of that
story to fall back on.

## Spectator Rotations, Quantified

### What crosstalk physically is

A microwave (or laser) pulse aimed at one qubit is never perfectly
localized. Some of its energy inevitably leaks onto physically or
spectrally nearby qubits, especially as devices pack qubits more densely
to scale up (Physical Qubit Platforms' connectivity discussion). This
"leak" acts as an unwanted, unintended small rotation on the
**spectator** qubit, even though only the **target** qubit's gate was
requested.

<CrosstalkDiagram ariaLabel="A chip layout with a target qubit being driven by an intended pulse, a faint dashed coupling line to an adjacent spectator qubit showing unwanted leakage, and other idle qubits elsewhere on the chip unaffected" />

Only the physically (or spectrally) nearby spectator picks up the leak;
the other, more distant qubits on the same chip are unaffected, which is
why crosstalk is a proximity effect rather than a device-wide one.

### Quantifying the fidelity loss

Using this platform's existing gate engine directly (no new machinery
needed): intending an X gate on qubit 0 of a 2-qubit system, but with
crosstalk causing an unwanted rotation of angle $\epsilon$ on qubit 1,
the actual final state differs from the ideal (X⊗I) target state with
fidelity:

$$
F(\epsilon) = |\langle\text{ideal}|\text{actual}\rangle|^2 = \cos^2(\epsilon/2)
$$

Confirmed directly via `applySingleQubitGate` and `rotationAboutAxis`
composed on a 2-qubit `StateVector`, matching this closed form to
machine precision for every tested $\epsilon$ (0.01 through 0.2 radians).

### Why crosstalk gets worse at scale

Unlike T1/T2 (properties of a single qubit's isolation, which don't
inherently worsen just because a device has more qubits), crosstalk
turns on *proximity and density*: pack more qubits into a fixed area, or
route more control lines past each other, and the opportunities for
unwanted leakage multiply. That's a different scaling concern from
decoherence, and it's the one the next lesson develops further.

## An Error That Grows With Neighbors

Crosstalk sits at the intersection of Control Electronics (imperfect
pulse localization) and Physical Qubit Platforms (device geometry/density).
T1/T2 ultimately trace back to unavoidable environmental coupling, a
physical limit. Crosstalk is different in kind: an engineering problem in
pulse shaping, filtering, and qubit layout, one that can be reduced,
though not eliminated, with better design.

## Fidelity Loss From a Small Angle

**A device's crosstalk causes $\epsilon=0.05$ radians of unwanted
rotation on a spectator qubit for every gate on its neighbor. What's the
fidelity loss for a single such gate?**

$$
F(0.05) = \cos^2(0.025) \approx 0.999375
$$

A $0.0625\%$ fidelity loss per gate from crosstalk alone. That looks
negligible in isolation, but it's the same kind of per-operation error
that compounds across a deep circuit (Error Correction & Fault
Tolerance's threshold discussion), which is why real hardware invests
in active crosstalk suppression, pulse shaping, frequency detuning
between neighbors, rather than shrugging off a fraction of a percent.

## Filing Crosstalk Under Decoherence

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating crosstalk as just another form of T1/T2 decoherence.**
It's structurally different: T1/T2 are properties of a single qubit's
coupling to its environment; crosstalk is a multi-qubit effect tied to
device geometry and control precision, requiring different mitigation
strategies (Sources of Noise's coherent/incoherent distinction applies
here too: crosstalk from a fixed, deterministic leakage pattern is
coherent, and can in principle be characterized and partially corrected).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming crosstalk fidelity loss is negligible because $\epsilon$ is
small.** As the worked example shows, even a modest per-gate loss
compounds significantly across many gates in a real circuit; small
per-operation errors are what error correction exists to manage.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the fidelity loss for $\epsilon=0.1$ radians, and compare it
   to the worked example's $\epsilon=0.05$ result.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   F(0.1)=\cos^2(0.05)\approx0.9975021, \qquad 1-F \approx 2.4979\times10^{-3},
   $$

   a loss of about 0.250% per gate, against $6.2487\times10^{-4}$, about
   0.0625%, at $\epsilon=0.05$. Both computed with the lesson's own
   engine path, `applySingleQubitGate` composed with
   `rotationAboutAxis` on a two-qubit `StateVector`, which reproduces
   $\cos^2(\epsilon/2)$ to the last digit at both angles.

   **The comparison is the point: the loss went up by a factor of 3.998,
   not 2.** Doubling the leak angle roughly quadrupled the damage. The
   reason is in the small-angle expansion:

   $$
   1-F = \sin^2(\epsilon/2) \approx \left(\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right)^2,
   $$

   so the loss is quadratic in $\epsilon$, not linear. At
   $\epsilon=0.1$ the exact $2.4979\times10^{-3}$ sits within 0.1% of the
   quadratic estimate $(\epsilon/2)^2=2.5\times10^{-3}$, which is why the
   curve looks almost flat near the origin and then falls away.

   The wrong turn is reading "quadratic" as reassuring, on the grounds
   that a small leak costs disproportionately little. It cuts the other
   way too, and that is the direction hardware actually lives in: a leak
   that gets *worse* costs disproportionately more. Question 4 is about
   exactly that.

   And a per-gate number is not the number that matters. Fidelities
   multiply along a circuit, so at $\epsilon=0.1$ a spectator that sits
   through 100 gates on its neighbour retains $F^{100}\approx0.779$, and
   through 1000 gates $\approx0.082$. At $\epsilon=0.05$ the same two
   depths give $0.939$ and $0.535$. A quarter of a percent per gate is
   not a rounding error once you count the depth of a real circuit, which
   is why devices invest in pulse shaping and in detuning neighbours from
   each other in frequency rather than shrugging it off.

   </details>

2. Why is crosstalk described as a matter of "proximity and density,"
   unlike T1/T2?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because crosstalk is a statement about a *relationship between two
   qubits*, while $T_1$ and $T_2$ are statements about *one qubit and its
   environment*.

   The mechanism makes this concrete. Crosstalk exists because a pulse
   aimed at one qubit is never perfectly localized: some fraction of it
   reaches a neighbour. How large that fraction is depends on how close
   the neighbour sits, both in space, since the coupling falls off with
   distance, and in frequency, since a pulse's spectrum has finite width
   and a neighbour whose transition sits nearby in frequency catches more
   of it. Both of those are geometric facts about the *layout*, not about
   either qubit alone.

   $T_1$ and $T_2$ are different in kind. They describe how strongly one
   qubit couples to an uncontrolled environment: lossy dielectric,
   two-level-system defects, fluctuating flux, thermal photons. Every one
   of those is present for a single qubit sitting alone on a chip, which
   is precisely how they are measured. Put ninety-nine more qubits
   elsewhere on the same die and the isolated qubit's $T_1$ is
   unchanged, because none of those quantities referred to a neighbour.

   The cleanest way to state the difference is by asking how many qubits
   each error needs in order to be defined at all. $T_1$ and $T_2$ need
   one. Crosstalk needs two and a geometry: there is no single-qubit
   version of the story to fall back on.

   Two consequences worth carrying forward.

   The scaling behaviours are opposite in character. Decoherence does not
   inherently worsen when a device grows, because each qubit still faces
   its own environment. Crosstalk does, because growth means more
   neighbours, packed closer, with more control lines routed past one
   another. That is why this lesson sits immediately before Scaling
   Challenges.

   The remedies are different too. $T_1$ and $T_2$ trace back to
   unavoidable environmental coupling and are attacked with materials,
   shielding and cooling. Crosstalk is an engineering problem in pulse
   shaping, filtering, frequency allocation and layout, reducible by
   better design in a way a physical decoherence limit is not.

   One honest qualification, so the contrast is not overdrawn. Packing a
   chip more densely can degrade $T_1$ in practice as well, through more
   nearby lossy material and interfaces. That is a real effect, but it is
   incidental rather than structural. Crosstalk's dependence on density
   is the definition of the error; $T_1$'s is a side effect of how the
   denser chip happened to be built.

   </details>

3. Is crosstalk generally a coherent or incoherent error, per Sources of
   Noise's classification? Explain your reasoning.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Generally coherent,** with a real qualification about what "coherent
   in principle" buys you in practice.

   The reasoning: the leak is a drive field, and a drive field acting on
   a qubit produces a rotation. This lesson's own model is a
   `rotationAboutAxis` by angle $\epsilon$, which is a unitary. It is
   deterministic given the pulse and the layout, it happens the same way
   every time that pulse is applied in that context, and it is
   invertible. That is the definition of a coherent error, and it is why
   the fidelity comes out as an exact closed form $\cos^2(\epsilon/2)$
   rather than as a channel that mixes the state. Nothing has been lost
   to an environment; the state has been moved somewhere wrong, and in
   principle it can be moved back.

   Correcting it is a control-side job, matching that classification.
   Characterize the leaked rotation once, then compensate: add a
   corrective rotation on the spectator, reshape the pulse envelope so
   less power reaches the neighbour's frequency, detune neighbours from
   each other, or schedule the offending pair's gates so they never fire
   together.

   **The qualification.** "Deterministic" is doing conditional work.
   The leak a spectator picks up depends on what *all* of its neighbours
   are doing at that instant: which are being driven, with what pulses,
   simultaneously. So the error is deterministic given the full
   simultaneous-operation context, and the number of contexts grows with
   the device. There is no single leaked angle to calibrate away; there
   is a table indexed by context.

   Two things follow that are worth being precise about.

   First, a coherent error can *look* incoherent when you average over a
   variable you are not tracking. Run many circuits with different
   neighbour activity and never model it, and the spectator's
   accumulated rotations point in effectively random directions, so the
   ensemble average resembles a depolarizing channel. That appearance is
   a fact about your bookkeeping, not about the physics, and it is
   exactly why crosstalk characterization protocols deliberately vary
   what the neighbours are doing rather than benchmarking one qubit in
   isolation.

   Second, coherent does not mean mild. As Calibration showed, coherent
   errors accumulate in the rotation angle, so infidelity grows
   quadratically with depth, while stochastic errors accumulate linearly.
   A deterministic crosstalk rotation repeated identically through a
   structured circuit is more damaging than a random error of the same
   per-gate size, not less.

   In practice a real device shows a mixture: a characterizable coherent
   part, plus a residual that drifts with the same parameters everything
   else on the chip drifts with.

   </details>

4. If a device's qubit count quadruples while keeping the same physical
   chip area (denser packing), would you expect crosstalk to get better,
   worse, or stay the same? Justify using this lesson's discussion.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Worse, on three independent counts at once**, which is what makes
   density a structural scaling problem rather than a tuning problem.

   **More neighbours.** Four times the qubits in the same area means
   roughly four times as many adjacent pairs on the layout, so four times
   as many opportunities for a pulse to land somewhere it was not aimed.
   Every gate now has more spectators listening.

   **Each neighbour is closer.** Four times the density in two dimensions
   halves the typical spacing. Whatever the falloff law of the particular
   coupling mechanism, and it differs between a coplanar capacitance and
   a dipole-dipole interaction, it decreases with distance, so halving
   the spacing increases every pairwise leak. Combine that with question
   1: the loss is quadratic in the leak angle, so a leak angle that
   merely doubles quadruples the per-gate fidelity cost.

   **Frequencies crowd too.** Crosstalk is spectral as well as spatial. A
   device has a finite usable frequency band, and putting four times as
   many qubits in it forces their transition frequencies closer together.
   A pulse's spectrum has finite width, so a neighbour sitting closer in
   frequency catches more of it, and the frequency collisions the
   Superconducting Qubits lesson named become likelier. Denser packing
   crowds both axes.

   An illustrative arithmetic, flagged as illustrative because the
   coupling law is device-specific: if halving the spacing doubles the
   leak angle from 0.05 to 0.1 rad, each gate's spectator loss goes from
   $6.2\times10^{-4}$ to $2.5\times10^{-3}$, a factor of 4, and with
   roughly four times as many spectator pairs the total leaked
   disturbance per layer of gates rises by something like sixteen. From a
   change that was described only as "more qubits in the same box."

   Set that against $T_1$ and $T_2$, which do not inherently degrade for
   this reason at all, since each qubit still faces its own environment.
   That contrast is the whole reason crosstalk gets its own lesson: it is
   the noise source whose severity is a function of how big the device
   is.

   The wrong turn is to expect the tradeoff to net out, on the reasoning
   that shorter distances also mean faster gates, so fewer nanoseconds of
   exposure per operation. Exposure time is not what sets this error.
   The leak angle $\epsilon$ is accumulated over the gate, and a stronger
   coupling driven for a shorter time delivers a comparable rotation.
   Nothing here is bought back by speed.

   Real devices do not simply accept the sixteen. They spend the density
   back: deliberate frequency allocation so neighbours are detuned,
   shaped and filtered pulses, tunable couplers that can be switched off
   between gates, and schedulers that refuse to fire particular pairs
   simultaneously. All of that is engineering effort that a sparser chip
   does not have to spend, which is the real price of density.

   </details>

## Summary

- Crosstalk is a multi-qubit noise source: driving one qubit's gate
  unintentionally rotates a nearby spectator qubit.
- Computed directly from this platform's existing gate engine: fidelity
  loss follows $F(\epsilon)=\cos^2(\epsilon/2)$, verified to machine
  precision, and the loss grows quadratically in $\epsilon$, not linearly.
- Unlike T1/T2, crosstalk worsens with qubit density, a structurally
  different scaling concern, central to the next lesson.
