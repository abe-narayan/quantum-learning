## Motivation

Every noise source this course has covered so far, T1, T2, is a
single-qubit story: one qubit, coupled to its own environment. Crosstalk
is different in kind: driving a gate on one qubit unintentionally
disturbs a *different* qubit nearby, a genuinely multi-qubit effect with
no single-qubit analogue.

## Engineering Development

### What crosstalk physically is

A microwave (or laser) pulse aimed at one qubit is never perfectly
localized. Some of its energy inevitably leaks onto physically or
spectrally nearby qubits, especially as devices pack qubits more densely
to scale up (Physical Qubit Platforms' connectivity discussion). This
"leak" acts as an unwanted, unintended small rotation on the
**spectator** qubit, even though only the **target** qubit's gate was
requested.

<CrosstalkDiagram ariaLabel="A chip layout with a target qubit being driven by an intended pulse, a faint dashed coupling line to an adjacent spectator qubit showing unwanted leakage, and other idle qubits elsewhere on the chip unaffected" />

Only the physically (or spectrally) nearby spectator picks up the leak;
the other, more distant qubits on the same chip are unaffected, which is
exactly why crosstalk is a proximity effect rather than a device-wide one.

### Quantifying the fidelity loss

Using this platform's existing gate engine directly (no new machinery
needed): intending an X gate on qubit 0 of a 2-qubit system, but with
crosstalk causing an unwanted rotation of angle $\epsilon$ on qubit 1,
the actual final state differs from the ideal (X⊗I) target state with
fidelity:

$$
F(\epsilon) = |\langle\text{ideal}|\text{actual}\rangle|^2 = \cos^2(\epsilon/2)
$$

Confirmed directly via `applySingleQubitGate` and `rotationAboutAxis`
composed on a 2-qubit `StateVector`, matching this closed form to
machine precision for every tested $\epsilon$ (0.01 through 0.2 radians).

### Why crosstalk gets worse at scale

Unlike T1/T2 (properties of a single qubit's isolation, which don't
inherently worsen just because a device has more qubits), crosstalk is
fundamentally about *proximity and density*: as more qubits are packed
into a fixed area (or connected via more shared control lines), the
opportunities for unwanted leakage multiply. This is a genuinely
different scaling concern from decoherence, developed further in the
next lesson.

## Physical Interpretation

Crosstalk sits at the intersection of Control Electronics (imperfect
pulse localization) and Physical Qubit Platforms (device geometry/density).
It's not a fundamental physical limit the way T1/T2 ultimately trace
back to unavoidable environmental coupling, but rather an engineering
challenge in pulse shaping, filtering, and qubit layout that can, in
principle, be reduced (though not eliminated) with better design.

## Worked Example

**A device's crosstalk causes $\epsilon=0.05$ radians of unwanted
rotation on a spectator qubit for every gate on its neighbor. What's the
fidelity loss for a single such gate?**

$$
F(0.05) = \cos^2(0.025) \approx 0.999375
$$

A $0.0625\%$ fidelity loss per gate from crosstalk alone. Small for
one gate, but exactly the kind of per-operation error that compounds
across a deep circuit (Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's threshold
discussion), motivating active crosstalk suppression techniques
(pulse shaping, frequency detuning between neighbors) in real hardware.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating crosstalk as just another form of T1/T2 decoherence.**
It's structurally different: T1/T2 are properties of a single qubit's
coupling to its environment; crosstalk is a multi-qubit effect tied to
device geometry and control precision, requiring different mitigation
strategies (Sources of Noise's coherent/incoherent distinction applies
here too: crosstalk from a fixed, deterministic leakage pattern is
coherent, and can in principle be characterized and partially corrected).
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming crosstalk fidelity loss is negligible because $\epsilon$ is
small.** As the worked example shows, even a modest per-gate loss
compounds significantly across many gates in a real circuit; small
per-operation errors are exactly what error correction exists to manage.
</Callout>
