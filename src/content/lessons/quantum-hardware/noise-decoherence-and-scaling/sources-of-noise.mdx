## Motivation

Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics built the mathematics of open-system
decoherence in the abstract; Control & Readout hinted at specific error
sources (readout error, miscalibration) without organizing them. This
lesson catalogs real noise sources and sorts them into two fundamentally
different categories, a classification that determines which mitigation
strategy actually applies.

## Engineering Development

### Coherent vs. incoherent errors

A **coherent error** is a systematic, deterministic deviation: the
device does something slightly wrong, consistently, the same way every
time (Control & Readout's Calibration lesson: a stale $\Omega$ value
implements a systematically wrong rotation angle). This is, in
principle, fully correctable once diagnosed; recalibration fixes it
exactly.

An **incoherent error** is a genuine, irreversible loss of information
to an uncontrolled environment, exactly what Advanced Topics in Quantum
Mechanics' Kraus channels model. This can be *mitigated* (better
isolation, error correction) but not simply "fixed" by recalibrating a
number, since the lost information isn't recoverable from the device
alone.

### A catalog of physical sources

- **Miscalibration drift** (coherent): Control & Readout's Calibration
  lesson; slow parameter drift between calibration runs.
- **Amplitude damping / energy relaxation** (incoherent): the qubit
  spontaneously decays to its ground state, releasing energy to the
  environment; modeled by `amplitudeDampingChannel`.
- **Dephasing** (incoherent): random phase kicks from fluctuating
  fields (e.g., flux noise in superconducting qubits, electric field
  noise for trapped ions); modeled by `dephasingChannel`.
- **Thermal photon population** (incoherent): Cryogenic Systems'
  $\bar n$; residual thermal photons directly perturb the qubit.
- **Control line noise**: imperfections in the classical electronics
  generating drive pulses (Control Electronics), which can be either
  coherent (a systematic pulse-shape distortion) or incoherent (random
  electronic noise on the line), depending on its specific origin.
- **Crosstalk**: unwanted coupling between supposedly-independent
  qubits (next lesson's dedicated topic).

### Why the classification matters for mitigation

A coherent miscalibration error is fixed by better/more frequent
calibration (Control & Readout), with no new hardware needed. An
incoherent decoherence error fundamentally cannot be fixed this way; it
requires either better physical isolation (reducing the coupling to the
environment in the first place) or active error correction (Quantum
Computing's Error Correction & Fault Tolerance course, redundantly
encoding information so it survives some amount of unavoidable loss).
Misidentifying which category a given error falls into leads to
wasted engineering effort: recalibrating against a source that's
actually environmental accomplishes nothing.

## Physical Interpretation

This distinction is the practical, hardware-facing version of a theme
this platform's Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics course already
established: decoherence (this lesson's incoherent errors) is
irreducibly different from a deterministic, correctable mistake. Every
real device's overall error budget is a mix of both categories, and
serious engineering effort goes into correctly attributing observed
errors to the right bucket.

## Worked Example

**A device's gate fidelity drops over the course of a day, but is
restored to its original value immediately after recalibration. Is this
most likely a coherent or incoherent error source?**

Coherent: the fact that recalibration alone restores full fidelity
(with no hardware change) is exactly the signature of a systematic,
deterministic parameter drift (Control & Readout's Calibration lesson).
An incoherent, genuinely environmental error would not be fixable by
simply re-measuring and re-setting a classical control parameter.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming all noise is "decoherence."** Miscalibration and other
coherent errors are a genuinely different (and often more easily fixed)
category. Lumping everything together as "decoherence" obscures which
mitigation strategy actually applies.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming incoherent errors can be "calibrated away."** Recalibration
fixes systematic, deterministic errors; it cannot recover information
genuinely lost to an uncontrolled environment. That requires isolation
improvements or active error correction instead.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Coherent errors are systematic and deterministic (fixable by better
  calibration); incoherent errors are genuine information loss to an
  environment (mitigated by isolation or error correction, not
  recalibration).
- Real noise sources include miscalibration drift, amplitude damping,
  dephasing, thermal photon population, control line noise, and
  crosstalk, spanning both categories.
- Correctly classifying an observed error is a prerequisite for choosing
  an effective mitigation strategy.
