## Coherent Against Incoherent, and Why It Matters

### Coherent vs. incoherent errors

A **coherent error** is a systematic, deterministic deviation: the
device does something slightly wrong, consistently, the same way every
time (Control & Readout's Calibration lesson: a stale $\Omega$ value
implements a systematically wrong rotation angle). This is, in
principle, fully correctable once diagnosed; recalibration fixes it
completely.

An **incoherent error** is an irreversible loss of information to
an uncontrolled environment, the same process Advanced Topics in
Quantum Mechanics' <Term id="kraus-operators-cptp-maps">Kraus channels</Term> model. It can be *mitigated* (better
isolation, error correction) but recalibrating a number won't fix it,
because the lost information was never sitting somewhere on the device
to recover.

### A catalog of physical sources

- **Miscalibration drift** (coherent): Control & Readout's Calibration
  lesson; slow <Term id="calibration-drift">parameter drift</Term> between calibration runs.
- **Amplitude damping / energy relaxation** (incoherent): the qubit
  spontaneously decays to its ground state, releasing energy to the
  environment; modeled by `amplitudeDampingChannel`.
- **Dephasing** (incoherent): random phase kicks from fluctuating
  fields (e.g., flux noise in superconducting qubits, magnetic field
  noise for trapped ions); modeled by `dephasingChannel`.
- **Thermal photon population** (incoherent): Cryogenic Systems'
  $\bar n$; residual thermal photons directly perturb the qubit.
- **Control line noise**: imperfections in the classical electronics
  generating drive pulses (Control Electronics), which can be either
  coherent (a systematic pulse-shape distortion) or incoherent (random
  electronic noise on the line), depending on its specific origin.
- **<Term id="crosstalk">Crosstalk</Term>**: unwanted coupling between supposedly-independent
  qubits (next lesson's dedicated topic).

### Why the classification matters for mitigation

A coherent miscalibration error is fixed by better or more frequent
calibration (Control & Readout), with no new hardware needed. An
incoherent decoherence error can't be fixed this way at all; it needs
either better physical isolation (reducing the coupling to the
environment in the first place) or active error correction (Quantum
Computing's Error Correction & Fault Tolerance course, redundantly
encoding information so it survives some amount of unavoidable loss).
Misidentify which category an error falls into and the engineering
effort is wasted: recalibrating against a source that's actually
environmental accomplishes nothing.

### The catalog, per platform

Several entries above (flux noise, thermal photons) are really
superconducting-specific culprits. Each platform from Physical Qubit
Platforms has its own characteristic short list:

| Platform | Characteristic noise sources |
|---|---|
| Superconducting | Charge and flux noise, thermal photons leaking into the readout resonator, two-level-system (TLS) defects in the circuit's materials |
| Trapped ions | Motional heating of the shared vibrational mode, laser phase and intensity noise on the drive beams |
| Neutral atoms | Atom loss from the optical tweezer, photon scattering off the trap light itself |
| Spin qubits | Charge noise coupling in through the gate electrodes that define the dot, and, on unpurified silicon, the nuclear-spin bath of the host material |
| Photonic | Photon loss (fiber, components, coupling), detector inefficiency |

The photonic row deserves the flag Photonic Qubits promised this course
would deliver: photon loss is an *erasure-type* channel, an error that
announces which qubit vanished (the detector simply never fires) rather
than silently flipping a bit or a phase, and error correction can
exploit that located-ness in ways it cannot for the other rows'
bit-flip and dephasing errors.

## The Hardware Face of a Familiar Split

This distinction is the practical, hardware-facing version of a theme
this platform's Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics course already
established: decoherence (this lesson's incoherent errors) is
irreducibly different from a deterministic, correctable mistake. Every
real device's overall error budget is a mix of both categories, and
serious engineering effort goes into correctly attributing observed
errors to the right bucket.

## Classifying an Error From Its Behavior

**A device's gate fidelity drops over the course of a day, but is
restored to its original value immediately after recalibration. Is this
most likely a coherent or incoherent error source?**

Coherent: recalibration alone restoring full fidelity, with no hardware
change, is the signature of a systematic, deterministic parameter drift
(Control & Readout's Calibration lesson). An environmental error
wouldn't be fixable just by re-measuring and re-setting a classical
control parameter.

## Calling Every Error Decoherence

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming all noise is "decoherence."** Miscalibration and other
coherent errors are a different (and often more easily fixed) category.
Lumping everything together as "decoherence" obscures which mitigation
strategy applies.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming incoherent errors can be "calibrated away."** Recalibration
fixes systematic, deterministic errors; it cannot recover information
already lost to an uncontrolled environment. That requires isolation
improvements or active error correction instead.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Classify each as coherent or incoherent: (a) a stale Rabi frequency
   value from Control & Readout, (b) amplitude damping from spontaneous
   emission, (c) dephasing from fluctuating magnetic flux.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **(a) Coherent. (b) Incoherent. (c) Incoherent.**

   The test to apply, in every case, is: *would remeasuring and resetting
   a classical control parameter undo this?*

   **(a) A stale $\Omega$ is coherent.** Nothing has gone wrong
   physically. The drive strength has a definite value, the hardware is
   faithfully executing the pulse it was asked for, and the only fault is
   that the number used to compute the pulse duration is out of date.
   Every gate is therefore wrong by the same factor, in the same
   direction, every time. Remeasure $\Omega$ and the error is gone
   completely.

   **(b) Amplitude damping is incoherent.** The qubit released a real
   energy quantum into its surroundings, and that quantum is not on the
   device any more. Something in the environment is now holding a record
   of which state the qubit was in, and that record is what destroys the
   superposition. No control parameter on the device is wrong, so there
   is nothing to reset.

   **(c) Dephasing from fluctuating flux is incoherent, and the word
   doing the work is "fluctuating."** This one is worth taking slowly,
   because the same physical mechanism can land in either category. If
   the local flux were offset by a *fixed*, unchanging amount, the qubit
   would simply pick up the same extra $Z$ rotation on every run, which
   is a deterministic unitary and therefore a coherent error you could
   characterize once and compensate. What makes flux *noise* incoherent
   is that the offset is different from shot to shot in a way nobody
   tracks, so averaging over runs washes the phase out rather than
   displacing it. The Bloch vector's $x$ and $y$ components shrink toward
   zero instead of rotating to a new place.

   The wrong turn most people take here is classifying by timescale:
   slow drift feels like decoherence, sudden events feel like faults. The
   timescale is not the axis. A coherent error is free to appear
   gradually over hours, which is precisely what (a) does, and an
   incoherent one can act in nanoseconds. The deciding question is
   whether retuning a number brings the fidelity back.

   </details>

2. Why does recalibration fully fix a coherent error but not an
   incoherent one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because recalibration replaces a classical number, and a coherent
   error *is* a wrong classical number, while an incoherent error is
   quantum information that has physically left the device.

   In the coherent case nothing was lost. The state the qubit ended up in
   is a definite, known, reversible rotation away from the state you
   wanted. Measure the parameter properly, recompute the pulse, and the
   error does not merely shrink; it is gone, with no new hardware and no
   extra qubits.

   In the incoherent case the missing information is not sitting anywhere
   on the device in a form you could correct by adjusting a setting. It
   is spread across an environment you neither control nor measure: a
   photon emitted into a lossy line, a phase correlated with a flux
   fluctuation nobody recorded. There is no number to fix because no
   number is wrong.

   The sharpest way to say this uses purity. A coherent error is a
   unitary, and unitaries are invertible, so some other unitary undoes
   it. Every unitary also *preserves* purity exactly. An incoherent
   channel reduces purity, which means no gate, however perfectly
   calibrated, can undo it: applying a unitary to a mixed state leaves it
   exactly as mixed as it was. The best-calibrated pulse in the world is
   still a unitary.

   That is also what this lesson's explorer is showing. Dephasing pulls
   the Bloch vector straight toward the $z$-axis and amplitude damping
   pulls it toward the north pole. Both are contractions of the sphere,
   and a rotation cannot invert a contraction, because a rotation moves
   points around the sphere and never moves them inward or outward. A
   coherent error moves the vector to the wrong place on the surface;
   an incoherent one moves it off the surface.

   The wrong turn is to conclude from all this that incoherent errors are
   simply hopeless. They are not correctable *by recalibration*, which is
   a much narrower claim. Question 3 is about what does work.

   </details>

3. Name two engineering mitigation strategies for incoherent errors,
   and explain why neither is "recalibration."

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **First: better physical isolation.** Reduce the coupling to the
   environment in the first place, so the information has less
   opportunity to leak. This is a materials and hardware programme, and
   the course is full of specific instances. A Faraday cage inside the
   vacuum chamber, cutting the stray electric field around trapped atoms.
   Isotopically purified silicon-28 for spin qubits, removing the
   nuclear-spin bath entirely rather than fighting its effects. Staged
   attenuation down a dilution refrigerator, stripping thermal photons
   off the control lines at each stage. A Purcell filter, transparent at
   the readout frequency and opaque at the qubit's, closing the path by
   which a qubit decays out through its own readout resonator. Better
   interfaces and oxides, reducing the density of two-level-system
   defects.

   **Second: active quantum error correction.** Encode one logical qubit
   redundantly across many physical qubits, measure syndromes that reveal
   *that* an error occurred without revealing the logical state, and
   correct. The redundancy supplies what isolation could not: a way to
   detect and undo a loss that has already happened.

   A third is worth knowing because it looks deceptively like
   recalibration and is not: **dynamical decoupling**. A train of
   refocusing pulses causes the phase accumulated during one interval to
   be cancelled by the next, so the qubit becomes insensitive to
   environmental fluctuations slower than the pulse spacing. It works by
   reshaping which frequencies of noise the qubit is exposed to, not by
   correcting any parameter, and it is why the millisecond spin-qubit
   coherence figures carry "with dynamical decoupling" attached.

   **Why none of these is recalibration.** Recalibration adjusts a
   classical control parameter and works only when the fault was a wrong
   parameter. These three do something else entirely. Isolation changes
   the physical coupling itself, and costs materials, shielding, cooling
   and design time rather than a measurement. Error correction adds
   redundancy, and costs physical qubits, gate depth and syndrome
   measurement rounds, which is where the thousand-to-one overhead in
   Roadmaps to Fault Tolerance comes from. Dynamical decoupling costs
   pulses and circuit time and only suppresses noise in a particular
   frequency band. Run any of them against a stale $\Omega$ and nothing
   improves; run recalibration against amplitude damping and nothing
   improves. The categories select the tool.

   One thing deliberately not on this list: error *mitigation*, in the
   sense of zero-noise extrapolation. It is a real and valuable
   technique, and it is neither of these, because it never produces a
   less noisy state. It recovers a better estimate of an expectation
   value by running noisy circuits and extrapolating. The Quantum Error
   Mitigation lesson develops that distinction properly.

   </details>

4. If crosstalk between two qubits were purely systematic (the same
   deterministic shift every time), would it belong in the coherent or
   incoherent category? Explain your reasoning.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Coherent.** The number of qubits involved is not what the
   classification is about.

   Read the definition again: a coherent error is a systematic,
   deterministic deviation, the same way every time, correctable in
   principle once diagnosed. A crosstalk term that applies exactly the
   same unwanted rotation to a spectator qubit on every run satisfies
   that word for word. It is a unitary, so it is invertible, so it is
   correctable by control-side means: characterize the leaked rotation
   once, then compensate for it with a corrective pulse, a reshaped
   envelope, or a schedule that avoids the offending pair. This is
   exactly what crosstalk compensation on real devices does.

   The wrong turn is the intuitive one: crosstalk involves a second
   qubit, the second qubit is "outside" the first, so surely the first
   qubit is losing information to something external and this is
   environmental. The distinction that actually matters is whether the
   deviation is a deterministic unitary you could in principle undo, not
   whether its origin is on the chip or off it. A neighbouring qubit you
   control and can model is not an uncontrolled environment.

   So why does this lesson pull crosstalk out and give it its own
   chapter, if it is just another coherent error? Because of the
   conditional in the question. Real crosstalk is deterministic *given
   the full context of what every other qubit is doing at that moment*,
   and that context changes gate by gate and circuit by circuit. A
   spectator's leaked rotation depends on which neighbours are being
   driven, with what pulses, at the same time. There is no single number
   to calibrate; there is a table over simultaneous-operation contexts,
   and the number of contexts grows with the device. Correctable in
   principle, and expensive in practice, which is a different problem
   from being incorrigible.

   Two further notes worth carrying.

   In practice crosstalk is usually not purely systematic. Some of it
   tracks parameters that drift, and some of it depends on neighbours in
   ways that look random from the point of view of a circuit that does
   not model them, so a real device shows a mixture with both a coherent
   and an effectively stochastic component.

   And being coherent is not the same as being harmless. As Calibration
   showed, coherent errors accumulate in the rotation angle and so grow
   quadratically with circuit depth, while stochastic errors accumulate
   linearly. A small deterministic crosstalk rotation is more dangerous
   over a deep circuit than a stochastic error of the same per-gate size,
   not less.

   </details>

## Summary

- Coherent errors are systematic and deterministic (fixable by better
  calibration); incoherent errors are information lost to an
  environment (mitigated by isolation or error correction, not
  recalibration).
- Real noise sources include miscalibration drift, amplitude damping,
  dephasing, thermal photon population, control line noise, and
  crosstalk, spanning both categories.
- Correctly classifying an observed error is a prerequisite for choosing
  an effective mitigation strategy.
