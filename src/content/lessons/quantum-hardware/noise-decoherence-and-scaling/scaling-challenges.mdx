## Motivation

Physical Qubit Platforms and this course's earlier lessons have built up
individual error sources (T1/T2, crosstalk, readout error) in isolation.
This lesson asks the question that actually matters for building a
useful device: what happens when you chain hundreds or thousands of
imperfect operations together?

## Engineering Development

### Errors compound multiplicatively

If each gate succeeds with probability $p$ (independent, per-gate
fidelity), then $N$ sequential gates succeed with probability
$p^N$, not $p$, and not $p\times N$ subtracted from 1, but a genuine
exponential decay in $N$.

| Per-gate fidelity | N=10 | N=100 | N=1000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99.9% | 99.0% | 90.5% | 36.8% |
| 99% | 90.4% | 36.6% | $4.3\times10^{-5}$ |
| 95% | 59.9% | 0.6% | $\approx0$ |

Even a genuinely excellent 99.9% per-gate fidelity, better than most
current real hardware, drops to 36.8% overall success by 1000 gates,
barely better than a coin flip's worth of confidence in the final
answer. This single table is the quantitative core of why "quantum
supremacy"-scale algorithms need either extremely low error rates or
active error correction, not just "more qubits."

### Qubit count is an incomplete metric

A device with 1000 "noisy" qubits (a common marketing framing) is not
automatically more capable than a much smaller device with dramatically
lower error rates. The compounding-error table above applies per
*qubit* too, and a computation using many qubits for many sequential
gates compounds error across both dimensions simultaneously. Metrics
like **quantum volume** (not derived in full here, but conceptually: a
combined measure of qubit count, connectivity, and per-gate error
together) exist specifically because raw qubit count alone is
misleading.

### Where error correction fits in

Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's **threshold theorem** result
(already built in the Quantum Computing pillar) is the direct answer to
this lesson's compounding-error problem: if the *physical* per-gate
error rate is below a certain threshold, encoding logical qubits
redundantly (Quantum Computing's bit-flip and Shor codes) can suppress
the *logical* error rate arbitrarily far below the physical rate, at
the cost of needing many physical qubits per logical qubit. This
lesson's compounding-error table is precisely the motivating problem
that result exists to solve.

## Physical Interpretation

"Scaling" a quantum computer means two different, both-necessary things
simultaneously: increasing qubit count, AND decreasing per-gate error
rate (or adding error correction overhead to compensate). A device that
scales only the first while ignoring the second doesn't become more
useful, per this lesson's own compounding-error numbers.

## Worked Example

**How many sequential 99.9%-fidelity gates can run before overall
success probability drops below 50%?**

$$
0.999^N = 0.5 \implies N = \frac{\ln0.5}{\ln0.999} \approx 693
$$

Fewer than 700 gates, even at a fidelity most current hardware hasn't
reached. This concretely illustrates why useful, deep quantum circuits
(many algorithms need thousands to millions of gates) are out of reach
without either substantially better physical fidelities or active error
correction.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "N qubits" as a complete description of a device's
capability.** As this lesson's table shows, per-gate error rate matters
at least as much as qubit count. A large, noisy device can be less
useful than a smaller, cleaner one for a given task.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming error compounds additively (subtracting N×(1-p) from 1)
rather than multiplicatively (p^N).** These give very different answers
at large N. The multiplicative form is what unitarity/independent-trial
probability actually requires.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Per-gate errors compound multiplicatively: $N$ sequential gates at
  fidelity $p$ succeed with probability $p^N$, an exponential decay in
  circuit depth.
- Even 99.9% per-gate fidelity drops to 36.8% success by 1000 gates,
  the concrete numerical reason deep circuits need either very low error
  rates or active error correction.
- Qubit count alone is an incomplete capability metric; per-gate error
  rate (and connectivity) matter at least as much.
