## Two Different Things Called Scaling

Physical Qubit Platforms and this course's earlier lessons built up
individual error sources, <Term id="t1-t2-coherence-times">T1/T2</Term>, <Term id="crosstalk">crosstalk</Term>, readout error, one at a
time, in isolation. This lesson asks the question that decides
whether a device is useful: what happens when hundreds or thousands of
imperfect operations run back to back?

## Why Errors Compound Multiplicatively

### Errors compound multiplicatively

If each gate succeeds with probability $p$ (independent, <Term id="gate-fidelity">per-gate
fidelity</Term>), then $N$ sequential gates succeed with probability $p^N$.
Not $p$, and not $1-N(1-p)$ either: it's an exponential decay in $N$,
not a linear one.

| Per-gate fidelity | N=10 | N=100 | N=1000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 99.9% | 99.0% | 90.5% | 36.8% |
| 99% | 90.4% | 36.6% | $4.3\times10^{-5}$ |
| 95% | 59.9% | 0.6% | $\approx0$ |

Where does 99.9% sit relative to real hardware? Through the mid-2020s,
single-qubit gates routinely exceeded it across platforms; it is
*two-qubit* gates where most devices still fell short of that mark, with
trapped-ion systems having demonstrated two-qubit fidelities at or above
99.9%. Treat that as a dated snapshot rather than a standing fact: the
two-qubit column is the fastest-moving number in the field, and which
platform leads it has changed hands more than once. And even
granting 99.9% for every gate in the circuit, overall success still
drops to 36.8% by 1000 gates, barely better than a coin flip's worth of
confidence in the final answer. This single table is the quantitative core of why
"quantum-supremacy"-scale algorithms need either extremely low error
rates or active error correction, not just more qubits.

The table samples three values of $N$; the full curve makes the decay's
shape, and how differently each fidelity ages, visible directly:

### Qubit count is an incomplete metric

A device with 1000 "noisy" qubits (a common marketing framing) is not
automatically more capable than a much smaller device with much lower
error rates. The compounding-error table above applies per *qubit*
too, and a computation using many qubits for many sequential gates
compounds error across both dimensions at once. Metrics like **quantum
volume** (not derived in full here, but conceptually: a combined
measure of qubit count, connectivity, and per-gate error together)
exist because raw qubit count alone is misleading.

### Wiring is a scaling problem too

Compounding error is the mathematical scaling wall; there's a plumbing
one right behind it. In a superconducting system, each qubit needs its
own dedicated control lines, roughly 2 to 3 per qubit (microwave
drive, readout, and often a separate flux bias), and each is a
physical coax cable running from room-temperature electronics down
through every stage of the dilution refrigerator. Scale that to 1,000
qubits and the fridge must swallow 2,000 to 3,000 coax lines. Every one
of them conducts heat inward and must be thermally anchored, attenuated,
and filtered at each of the staged cooling plates Cryogenic Systems
described, and those stages have finite cooling power; the coldest ones
have the least of it. Qubit counts can in principle be doubled by
lithography, but the wiring, and the heat it carries, does not
miniaturize the same way, which is why serious scaling roadmaps involve
cryogenic control electronics, multiplexed readout lines, and other
ways to break the one-cable-per-qubit-per-function relationship rather
than simply thicker cable bundles.

### Where error correction fits in

Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's **threshold theorem** result
(already built in the Quantum Computing track) is the direct answer to
this lesson's compounding-error problem: if the *physical* per-gate
error rate is below a certain threshold, encoding logical qubits
redundantly (Quantum Computing's bit-flip and Shor codes) can suppress
the *logical* error rate arbitrarily far below the physical rate, at
the cost of needing many physical qubits per logical qubit. This lesson's
compounding-error table is the problem that result exists to solve.

## Qubit Count Is Not a Capability

"Scaling" a quantum computer means two different, both-necessary things
simultaneously: increasing qubit count, AND decreasing per-gate error
rate (or adding error correction overhead to compensate). A device that
scales only the first while ignoring the second doesn't become more
useful, and this lesson's own compounding-error numbers are how you
check that for any device you are handed.

## Where Success Probability Crosses One Half

**How many sequential 99.9%-fidelity gates can you run before overall
success probability drops below 50%?**

$$
0.999^N = 0.5 \implies N = \frac{\ln0.5}{\ln0.999} \approx 693
$$

Fewer than 700 gates, even at a fidelity most current hardware's
two-qubit gates haven't reached. That is why deep quantum circuits
(many algorithms need thousands to millions of gates) are out of reach
without either substantially better physical fidelities or active error
correction.

## Comparing Devices by Qubit Count Alone

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating "N qubits" as a complete description of a device's
capability.** As this lesson's table shows, per-gate error rate matters
at least as much as qubit count. For the task you actually have, a
large noisy device can be less useful than a smaller, cleaner one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming error compounds additively (subtracting N×(1-p) from 1)
rather than multiplicatively (p^N).** These give very different answers
at large N. The multiplicative form is what independent-trial
probability requires.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using $p^N$, compute the success probability for 500 gates at 99.9%
   per-gate fidelity. Then compute what the additive approximation
   $1-N(1-p)$ would have said, and find roughly where the two stop
   agreeing.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   0.999^{500} \approx 0.6064,
   $$

   so about a 60.6% chance the whole circuit runs clean. Sitting between
   the table's 90.5% at $N=100$ and 36.8% at $N=1000$, as it should.

   **The additive approximation gives 0.500:**
   $1-500\times0.001=0.5$. That is 17.5% low in relative terms, and it is
   wrong in the direction that matters, since it makes the device look
   worse than it is at this depth and then catastrophically wrong further
   out.

   **Where they part company.** Track both:

   at $N=10$, $0.990045$ against $0.990000$, agreeing to five decimals;

   at $N=100$, $0.904792$ against $0.900000$, about 0.5% apart;

   at $N=200$, $0.818649$ against $0.800000$, about 2.3% apart;

   at $N=500$, $0.606379$ against $0.500000$, about 17.5% apart;

   at $N=1000$, $0.367695$ against $0.000000$, where the additive form
   has not merely lost accuracy but has crossed into predicting outright
   impossibility for a circuit that in fact succeeds more than a third of
   the time.

   So the two agree while $N(1-p)$ stays well under 1, which is exactly
   the regime where the first-order expansion
   $p^N=(1-\epsilon)^N\approx1-N\epsilon$ is valid, and they diverge as
   $N(1-p)$ approaches 1. Here $1-p=0.001$, so trouble starts somewhere
   in the low hundreds of gates and is total by a thousand.

   That is precisely the regime the lesson is about. The additive form is
   not a harmless simplification for this material, because the
   interesting depths are exactly the depths where it fails, and its
   particular failure mode, predicting zero and then meaningless negative
   numbers, hides the exponential tail that makes 99.9% and 99.99%
   different devices.

   The structural reason for the multiplicative form is worth restating.
   Overall success requires *every* gate to succeed, and for independent
   events the probability that all of them do is the product of their
   individual probabilities, $p\times p\times\dots\times p=p^N$. The
   additive form is answering a different, linearized question.

   </details>

2. Redo the worked example's calculation for a 99.99% per-gate fidelity
   target: how many gates can run before dropping below 50% success?
   Then compare with the 99.9% case: what factor in depth does a tenfold
   reduction in error rate buy?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   0.9999^N = 0.5 \implies N = \frac{\ln 0.5}{\ln 0.9999} \approx 6931 \text{ gates}.
   $$

   Against 693 at 99.9%. **A tenfold reduction in per-gate error rate
   buys almost exactly a tenfold increase in usable depth**, a ratio of
   $6931/693=10.004$.

   The near-exactness is not a coincidence, and the reason is worth
   extracting as a rule of thumb. For small error rates,
   $\ln p=\ln(1-\epsilon)\approx-\epsilon$, so

   $$
   N_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{-\ln p} \approx \frac{0.693}{1-p}.
   $$

   The half-success depth is inversely proportional to the error rate.
   Check it: $0.693/0.001=693$ and $0.693/0.0001=6931$. Both land on the
   exact answers. The same formula gives 69 gates at 99%, which the exact
   calculation puts at 68.97.

   That linear relation is the piece of good news in an otherwise grim
   lesson, and it is worth being clear about how limited it is. Error
   rates are hard-won: getting a two-qubit gate from 99.9% to 99.99% is a
   major engineering programme, not a firmware update, and most platforms
   have not reached the first of those numbers. What that programme buys
   is ten times the depth. Meanwhile algorithms that matter want
   thousands to millions of gates. Even 99.99% everywhere leaves you
   short of a million-gate circuit by more than two orders of magnitude,
   and there is no error rate short of exactly 1 that fixes this by
   itself, because $p^N$ decays for every $p<1$.

   The wrong turn is treating the improvement as though it compounded,
   reasoning that ten times fewer errors surely buys much more than ten
   times the depth given how violently the exponential behaves. It does
   not. The exponential is violent in $N$ at fixed $p$; the trade between
   $p$ and the half-depth is linear. That asymmetry is the whole argument
   for error correction: pushing $p$ down is a losing race against an
   algorithm's gate count, so the relationship itself has to change.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why quantum volume (or similar combined
   metrics) exist, rather than reporting qubit count alone.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a useful computation needs qubit count, circuit depth and
   connectivity *at the same time*, and any single-axis number can be
   improved without the device becoming able to run anything new, so a
   combined metric asks what a device can actually execute rather than
   what it contains.

   Unpacking that. Qubit count is the easiest axis to grow and the
   easiest to report, which is exactly why it is the one most often
   quoted. But this lesson's table shows that adding qubits does nothing
   for a device already limited by depth, and a chip whose qubits are
   sparsely connected pays SWAP routing overhead that inflates $N$ for
   any circuit needing distant pairs, making the depth problem worse.
   Three axes, and a headline that reports one of them tells you almost
   nothing about the other two.

   Quantum volume's construction is a direct response to that. It asks
   for the largest *square* circuit, width equal to depth, that the
   device can run with the right output distribution more often than
   chance, and reports $2^n$ for that width $n$. Because the circuit is
   square, adding qubits you cannot reach or cannot drive deeply enough
   does not raise the number. The metric cannot be inflated on one axis
   alone, which is the entire design goal.

   Two honest caveats, since a combined metric is not a solved problem
   either. Quantum volume is exponential in the width, so a device with
   $n=20$ reports a "volume" of 1,048,576, a number whose size is a
   property of the exponential rather than of the machine, and comparing
   such figures across vendors invites confusion. And it says nothing
   about speed, which is why separate metrics for circuit-layer
   throughput exist, or about error-corrected performance, which is the
   regime the next lesson is aiming at.

   The wrong turn is to swap one headline number for another and treat
   quantum volume as the true scalar capability of a device. The lesson
   is not that some other single number is the right one; it is that
   capability is multidimensional, and any scalar is a projection that
   discards something. The reason to prefer a combined metric is only
   that it discards less.

   </details>

4. How does this lesson's compounding-error problem directly motivate
   the existence of Error Correction & Fault Tolerance's threshold
   theorem?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because compounding error says that for any fixed $p<1$ there is a
   depth beyond which the answer is worthless, and the threshold theorem
   is the result that changes what "fixed $p$" means.

   Lay the problem out first. Success is $p^N$, which decays
   exponentially in depth for every $p$ short of 1. Question 2 showed
   that improving $p$ buys depth only linearly. Algorithms of real
   interest want thousands to millions of gates. So there are only two
   possible escapes: drive $p$ all the way to 1, which physics does not
   allow, or stop treating the physical error rate as the rate the
   algorithm sees.

   The threshold theorem takes the second route. Encode each logical
   qubit redundantly across many physical qubits and measure syndromes,
   and the *logical* error rate becomes a function of the physical one.
   For a code of distance $d$ it behaves roughly as

   $$
   p_L \sim \left(\frac{p}{p_{\text{th}}}\right)^{\lfloor (d+1)/2\rfloor},
   $$

   so provided $p<p_{\text{th}}$, the logical error rate falls
   exponentially in the code distance while the physical qubit cost grows
   only polynomially in it. Depth stops being rationed and becomes
   something you buy with qubits.

   **The reversal is the point, and it is exactly the reversal this
   lesson sets up.** Before the theorem, the binding constraint is a
   depth ceiling and there is no way to raise it far. After it, the
   binding constraint is an *error-rate* ceiling, the threshold, and
   below that ceiling any depth is purchasable at polynomial cost.

   That also gives the sharpest possible version of the lesson's warning
   about qubit count. The threshold is a genuine sign change, not a soft
   guideline. Below $p_{\text{th}}$, adding physical qubits to a code
   makes the logical qubit better. Above it, adding physical qubits makes
   it *worse*, because each additional component is another chance to
   fail and the code cannot correct faster than errors arrive. "More
   qubits" is not merely an incomplete description of capability; whether
   more qubits help at all depends on which side of the threshold the
   device sits.

   The wrong turn is reading the theorem as a promise that error
   correction makes the compounding problem go away. It does not come
   free, and the bill is the subject of the next lesson: the redundancy
   costs on the order of a thousand physical qubits per logical one, plus
   continuous syndrome measurement, plus the fact that the syndrome
   circuits are themselves built from the same faulty gates. The theorem
   converts an impossibility into an overhead. That is an enormous
   change, and it is not the same as a solution.

   </details>

## Summary

- Per-gate errors compound multiplicatively: $N$ sequential gates at
  fidelity $p$ succeed with probability $p^N$, an exponential decay in
  circuit depth.
- Even 99.9% per-gate fidelity drops to 36.8% success by 1000 gates,
  the concrete numerical reason deep circuits need either very low error
  rates or active error correction.
- Qubit count alone is an incomplete capability metric; per-gate error
  rate (and connectivity) matter at least as much.
