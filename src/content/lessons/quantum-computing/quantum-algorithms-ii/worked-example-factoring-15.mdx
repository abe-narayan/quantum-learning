## Mathematical Development

### Step 1: choose a

Pick $a=7$ (coprime to $15$; checked by $\gcd(7,15)=1$).

### Step 2: the quantum period-finding circuit

With $t=6$ counting qubits, `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution(7, 15,
6)` gives exactly four peaks, at outcomes $0,16,32,48$, each with
probability $0.25$, and (numerically) zero everywhere else. This is the
one genuinely quantum step: the interference pattern from applying the
QFT to the counting register after the modular-exponentiation state was
prepared.

### Step 3: read off the period

A single measurement returns one of the four peak locations at random,
say $32$. Dividing by $2^t=64$ gives the fraction $32/64=1/2$. In general
this fraction approximates $k/r$ for some integer $k$; the classical
continued-fractions algorithm (not implemented here, see the last
lesson's scope note) would recover $r=4$ from it reliably even when the
fraction doesn't reduce as cleanly as this particular outcome does. For
this specific worked example, the reduced fraction's denominator, $2$,
undersells the true period. This is a known subtlety of continued
fractions recovery ($k$ and $r$ can share a common factor, here $k=2,r=4$
both divisible by $2$), and it's exactly why real implementations run the
circuit several times and combine the results rather than trusting one
shot. This
platform's `classicalOrder(7, 15)` independently confirms the true
period directly: $r=4$.

From Lesson 1's reduction: $r=4$ is even, $a^{r/2}=7^2\bmod15=49\bmod15=4$,
not $\equiv-1\equiv14\pmod{15}$, so the reduction applies cleanly.
$\gcd(4-1,15)=\gcd(3,15)=3$, $\gcd(4+1,15)=\gcd(5,15)=5$.

$$
\boxed{15 = 3 \times 5}
$$

## Physical Interpretation

Precisely one step in this pipeline needed a quantum computer: producing
the period-finding measurement distribution's sharp peaks via
interference. Every other step (picking $a$, checking $\gcd(a,N)=1$,
interpreting the measured fraction, computing $a^{r/2}\bmod N$, running
Euclid's algorithm twice) is ordinary classical computation a laptop
performs instantly. This is the concrete, worked version of Lesson 1's
abstract claim: quantum speedup lives in one specific, narrow subroutine,
not throughout the whole algorithm.

## Worked Example

**Confirm $\gcd(7,15)=1$ directly, verifying $a=7$ was a legitimate
choice.**

$15=2(7)+1$, so $\gcd(7,15)=\gcd(7,1)=1$ (Euclid's algorithm, one step).
This confirms $7$ is coprime to $15$, so the whole pipeline above was
valid to run.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "factoring 15" as evidence Shor's algorithm threatens real
RSA keys today.** $15$ has $4$ bits; real RSA keys have thousands. Running
this exact pipeline at cryptographically relevant size needs a
controlled-modular-exponentiation circuit with thousands of logical
qubits and deep circuits, protected by error correction: engineering far
beyond both this course's toy engine and current quantum hardware. This
worked example demonstrates the *mechanism*, not a practical attack.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming a single measurement always gives the exact period directly.**
Here it happened to reduce cleanly to $r/2$ instead of $r$ itself. Real
implementations account for this by running the circuit multiple times
and combining results, not by trusting one shot.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $N=15$, $a=7$: quantum period-finding gives peaks at multiples of $16$;
  the true period $r=4$ is confirmed via `classicalOrder`.
- The classical reduction ($a^{r/2}\bmod N$, two gcd computations) gives
  $15=3\times5$ directly.
- Exactly one step, the period-finding measurement distribution's
  interference pattern, is quantum; everything else is classical
  arithmetic, run instantly on ordinary hardware.
- This toy example demonstrates Shor's algorithm's mechanism honestly; it
  is not, and does not claim to be, a cryptographically relevant
  factorization.
