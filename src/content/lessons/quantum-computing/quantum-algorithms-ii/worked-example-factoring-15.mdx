## Small enough to check every step by hand

$N=15$ is the standard first example for Shor's algorithm because it is
small enough to compute every step by hand and to run through this
platform's engine, which makes this an end-to-end demonstration rather
than a description of one.

Two things are worth watching for as it runs. First, exactly one of the
four steps is quantum. Second, one of the four possible measurement
outcomes gives the wrong period and another gives no information, which is
the kind of detail a clean summary of the algorithm leaves out.

## Four steps, from one choice to two factors

### Step 1: choose a

Pick $a=7$ (coprime to $15$; checked by $\gcd(7,15)=1$).

### Step 2: the quantum period-finding circuit

With $t=6$ counting qubits, `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution(7, 15,
6)` gives four peaks, at outcomes $0,16,32,48$, each with probability
$0.25$, and numerically zero everywhere else. This is the one quantum step
in the whole pipeline: the <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> pattern produced by applying the
QFT to the counting register after the modular-exponentiation state was
prepared.

### Step 3: read off the period

A single measurement returns one of the four peak locations at random,
say $32$. Dividing by $2^t=64$ gives the fraction $32/64=1/2$. In general
this fraction approximates $k/r$ for some integer $k$; the classical
continued-fractions algorithm (not implemented here, see the last
lesson's scope note) would recover $r=4$ from it reliably even when the
fraction doesn't reduce as cleanly as this particular outcome does. For
this specific worked example, the reduced fraction's denominator, $2$,
undersells the true period. This is a known subtlety of continued
fractions recovery ($k$ and $r$ can share a common factor, here $k=2,r=4$
both divisible by $2$), and it is why real implementations run the circuit
several times and combine results rather than trusting one shot. This
platform's `classicalOrder(7, 15)` confirms the true period independently:
$r=4$.

From Lesson 1's reduction: $r=4$ is even, $a^{r/2}=7^2\bmod15=49\bmod15=4$,
not $\equiv-1\equiv14\pmod{15}$, so the reduction applies cleanly.
$\gcd(4-1,15)=\gcd(3,15)=3$, $\gcd(4+1,15)=\gcd(5,15)=5$.

$$
\boxed{15 = 3 \times 5}
$$

## Exactly one step needed a quantum computer

One step in this pipeline needed a quantum computer: producing the
period-finding distribution's sharp peaks through interference. Every other
step (picking $a$, checking $\gcd(a,N)=1$, interpreting the measured
fraction, computing $a^{r/2}\bmod N$, running Euclid's algorithm twice) is
classical computation a laptop performs instantly.

This is the worked version of Lesson 1's claim: the <Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum speedup</Term> lives
in one narrow subroutine rather than throughout the algorithm.

## Confirming the choice was legitimate

**Confirm $\gcd(7,15)=1$ directly, verifying $a=7$ was a legitimate
choice.**

$15=2(7)+1$, so $\gcd(7,15)=\gcd(7,1)=1$ (Euclid's algorithm, one step).
This confirms $7$ is coprime to $15$, so the whole pipeline above was
valid to run.

## Four bits against a few thousand

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "factoring 15" as evidence Shor's algorithm threatens real
RSA keys today.** $15$ has $4$ bits; real RSA keys have thousands. Running
this exact pipeline at cryptographically relevant size needs a
controlled-modular-exponentiation circuit with thousands of logical
qubits and deep circuits, protected by <Term id="quantum-error-correction">error correction</Term>: engineering far
beyond both this course's toy engine and current quantum hardware. This
worked example demonstrates the *mechanism*, not a practical attack.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a single measurement always gives the exact period directly.**
Here it happened to reduce cleanly to $r/2$ instead of $r$ itself. Real
implementations account for this by running the circuit multiple times
and combining results, not by trusting one shot.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Repeat the pipeline for $N=21$, $a=2$ (order $r=6$, from earlier
   practice): predict the period-finding peaks with $t=6$ counting qubits,
   then carry out the classical reduction to find $21$'s factors.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The peaks first, and this case does *not* behave like $N=15$.
   $2^t/r = 64/6 = 10.667$ is not an integer, so amplitude cannot
   concentrate on exact multiples. It concentrates on the nearest integers
   to $k\cdot 64/6$ for $k=0,\dots,5$, which are

   $$
   0,\ 10.67,\ 21.33,\ 32,\ 42.67,\ 53.33 \ \longrightarrow\ 0,\ 11,\ 21,\ 32,\ 43,\ 53 .
   $$

   The engine agrees precisely: those six outcomes are the six largest,
   with probability $0.167$ at $0$ and $32$ (where the multiple is exact)
   and $0.114$ at the four rounded ones, about $79\%$ of the total. Unlike
   the $N=15$ run there is no floor of exact zeros; the remaining $21\%$
   is spread thinly across the other $58$ outcomes.

   Now the classical reduction, which does not care about any of that
   provided the measurement yields $r=6$. $r$ is even, and
   $a^{r/2}=2^3=8$. Check the second condition: $-1\equiv20\pmod{21}$ and
   $8\neq20$, so the reduction applies. Then

   $$
   \gcd(8-1,\,21)=\gcd(7,21)=7, \qquad \gcd(8+1,\,21)=\gcd(9,21)=3,
   $$

   giving $21 = 3\times7$.

   The instructive difference from the lesson's $N=15$ run is entirely in
   step 2. Both reductions are identical arithmetic; the quantum output is
   clean in one case and smeared in the other, purely because $4$ divides
   $64$ and $6$ does not. Since $r$ is unknown before you run, a real
   implementation must assume the smeared case, which is what continued
   fractions and repeated runs are for.

   The wrong turn is predicting six sharp peaks at multiples of $10.667$
   and reporting non-integer outcome labels. A measurement returns an
   integer between $0$ and $63$; the non-integer multiples are where the
   peaks *would* be if they could, and rounding is what actually happens.

   </details>

2. Suppose the measured outcome in Step 3 had been $16$ instead of $32$.
   What fraction would $16/64$ reduce to, and would it still correctly
   identify $r=4$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $16/64$ reduces to $1/4$, whose denominator is $4$, and yes, that is
   the correct $r$.

   Compare all four possible outcomes for this run, which the engine's
   distribution says are equally likely at $0.25$ each:
   $0/64 \to 0/1$, denominator $1$, no information at all;
   $16/64 \to 1/4$, denominator $4$, correct;
   $32/64 \to 1/2$, denominator $2$, an undercount;
   $48/64 \to 3/4$, denominator $4$, correct.

   So two of the four outcomes give $r$ directly, one gives a proper
   divisor of it, and one gives nothing. The mechanism is that the
   measured outcome is $k\cdot2^t/r$ for a uniformly random $k$ in
   $\{0,\dots,r-1\}$, and reducing $k/r$ to lowest terms recovers $r$
   exactly when $\gcd(k,r)=1$. Here $k=1$ and $k=3$ are coprime to $4$ and
   work; $k=2$ shares a factor and undersells; $k=0$ collapses entirely.

   That is the honest reason real implementations repeat the circuit. A
   single shot succeeds with probability $\phi(r)/r$, which is $1/2$ here,
   and combining the denominators from a few shots by least common
   multiple recovers $r$ with high probability. It also explains why the
   lesson's chosen illustration, $x=32$, was the awkward one: it was
   picked to show the failure mode rather than to flatter the algorithm.

   The wrong turn is reading a denominator of $2$ as evidence that the
   period really is $2$ and moving on. It would send you into the
   reduction with $a^{r/2}=7^1=7$, and $\gcd(6,15)=3$, $\gcd(8,15)=1$: one
   real factor and one trivial, so you would still stumble onto $3$ here,
   but only by luck. Verifying the candidate $r$ by checking
   $a^{r}\equiv1\pmod N$ costs one modular exponentiation and catches it.

   </details>

3. Why does checking $\gcd(a,N)=1$ *before* running the quantum circuit
   matter? What would go wrong (conceptually) with modular exponentiation
   if $a$ and $N$ shared a common factor?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Two reasons, and the first is the happy one: if $\gcd(a,N)\neq1$ then
   that gcd is *already* a nontrivial factor of $N$, computed by Euclid's
   algorithm in microseconds. You are done, and running any quantum
   circuit would be wasted effort.

   The second is that order-finding is not defined otherwise. The order
   $r$ is the smallest positive integer with $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$, and such
   an $r$ exists only when $a$ is invertible mod $N$, which is exactly the
   condition $\gcd(a,N)=1$. If $a$ shares a factor $d>1$ with $N$, then
   every power $a^k$ is divisible by $d$, so $a^k\bmod N$ is always
   divisible by $d$ and can never equal $1$. The sequence
   $a^0, a^1, a^2,\dots \bmod N$ still eventually repeats, but it never
   returns to $1$, so it has a pre-period before entering its cycle and is
   not purely periodic in $x$.

   That last point is what breaks the circuit rather than merely the
   definition. The QFT extraction assumes $f(x)=a^x\bmod N$ is periodic
   from $x=0$ onward, so that grouping the sum by residue class mod $r$
   gives equal-sized classes whose phases interfere cleanly. An eventually
   periodic function with a lead-in has classes of unequal size, the
   cancellation is no longer exact, and the peaks blur or shift. The
   engine's `classicalOrder` refuses such an $a$ outright, which is the
   same boundary enforced in code.

   The wrong turn is treating the gcd check as a formality that a
   well-chosen random $a$ will pass anyway. It usually will, since for
   $N=pq$ only a $1/p+1/q$ fraction of bases fail, but the check costs
   almost nothing and its failure is a *win*, not an error. It is the one
   place in Shor's algorithm where bad luck hands you the answer for free.

   </details>

4. List, in order, every step of this worked example that was purely
   classical computation, and the one step that was not. Then say which of
   those classical steps stay cheap at cryptographic scale, and which one
   in this demonstration would not.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In order: (1) pick $a=7$, classical. (2) check $\gcd(7,15)=1$ by
   Euclid, classical. (3) run the period-finding circuit and measure,
   **quantum**, and the only quantum step. (4) reduce the measured
   fraction $32/64$ to identify $r$, classical. (5) compute
   $a^{r/2}=7^2\bmod15=4$, classical. (6) run Euclid twice on
   $\gcd(3,15)$ and $\gcd(5,15)$, classical. Five classical steps around
   one quantum one.

   At cryptographic scale every one of those classical steps stays cheap,
   and that is precisely why the reduction counts as a reduction. Euclid's
   algorithm is $O(\log^2 N)$; modular exponentiation by square-and-multiply
   is $O(\log^3 N)$; continued fractions on a fraction with denominator
   $2^t$ is polynomial in $t$. None of them is the bottleneck at any size.

   The step that would not stay cheap is one this demonstration used
   without listing it: `classicalOrder(7,15)`, the brute-force order check
   that confirmed $r=4$ independently. Brute force costs $O(r)$
   multiplications and $r$ can be about the size of $N$, so it is
   exponential in the digit count. It appears here only as a checking
   tool, and it is exactly the job the quantum step exists to replace. A
   real run has no such oracle and must trust the measured outcome, which
   is why the fraction-reduction subtlety in question 2 matters.

   The wrong turn is describing Shor's algorithm as "a quantum algorithm
   for factoring" and leaving it there. Five of six steps run on a laptop,
   the quantum computer is asked one narrow question, and the honest
   summary is that Shor's algorithm is a classical factoring algorithm
   with one exponentially expensive subroutine offloaded. It is also worth
   saying plainly, as the lesson does, that $15=3\times5$ is a number you
   could factor in your head: this run demonstrates the mechanism and no
   advantage whatsoever.

   </details>

## Summary

- $N=15$, $a=7$: quantum period-finding gives peaks at multiples of $16$;
  the true period $r=4$ is confirmed via `classicalOrder`.
- The classical reduction ($a^{r/2}\bmod N$, two gcd computations) gives
  $15=3\times5$ directly.
- Exactly one step, the period-finding measurement distribution's
  interference pattern, is quantum; everything else is classical
  arithmetic, run instantly on ordinary hardware.
- This toy example demonstrates Shor's algorithm's mechanism honestly; it
  is not, and does not claim to be, a cryptographically relevant
  factorization.
