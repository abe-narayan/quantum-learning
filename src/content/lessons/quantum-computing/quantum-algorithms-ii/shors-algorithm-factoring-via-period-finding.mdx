## Motivation

RSA encryption's security rests on factoring large numbers being hard: no
known *classical* algorithm factors an $n$-digit number in time
polynomial in $n$. Shor's algorithm factors in polynomial time on a
quantum computer, and the surprising part is *where* the quantum speedup
actually enters. It's not in factoring directly, but in a narrower
subroutine, finding the **order** of a number modulo $N$, that the rest
of the algorithm is entirely classical number theory built around.

## Mathematical Development

### The order-finding problem

For integers $a,N$ with $\gcd(a,N)=1$, the **order** of $a$ modulo $N$ is
the smallest positive integer $r$ with $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$. This always
exists (a consequence of Euler's theorem, taken as given here) and is
exactly what `classicalOrder` computes by brute force, useful for
checking answers and for the toy examples this course actually runs, even
though brute force itself doesn't scale.

### The reduction: factoring from an even order

Suppose you know $r$, the order of some $a$ modulo $N$, and $r$ happens to
be **even**. Then $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$ can be rewritten as

$$
(a^{r/2})^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1) \equiv 0 \pmod N
$$

using the difference-of-squares factorization directly. This means $N$
divides the product $(a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)$. If $N$ divides *neither*
factor individually (which happens precisely when $a^{r/2}\not\equiv\pm1
\pmod N$), then $N$ must share a nontrivial common factor with each one
separately. Computing $\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,\,N)$ and $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,\,N)$
therefore reveals genuine, nontrivial factors of $N$, an entirely
classical calculation (Euclid's algorithm) once $r$ is known.

### When the reduction fails, and why that's still fine

Two things can go wrong: $r$ could be odd (no $a^{r/2}$ to work with at
all), or $a^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod N$ (the product's second factor is
divisible by $N$ outright, giving only the trivial factor $N$ itself from
that side). Both failures are handled the same way: pick a different
random $a$ and try again. It's a standard (but nontrivial, and not
re-derived here) result of number theory that for a randomly chosen $a$
coprime to $N$, the order $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$
with probability at least $1/2$, so a handful of random restarts succeeds
with overwhelming probability. This platform doesn't prove that
probability bound; it's cited as a known classical result the rest of the
algorithm depends on, not derived from scratch.

## Physical Interpretation

Every step above (the algebra, the gcd computation, the random restarts)
is completely classical, and completely efficient; a classical computer
does all of it in polynomial time without difficulty. The *only* step that
needs a quantum computer is **finding $r$ itself** for a large $N$, since
brute-force order-finding takes exponentially many trial multiplications
in the worst case. Shor's actual contribution is a quantum subroutine for
that one step, the subject of the next lesson.

## Worked Example

**Given $N=15$, $a=7$: verify $r=4$ (already computed via
`classicalOrder`) leads to genuine factors via the reduction above.**

$r=4$ is even. $a^{r/2}=7^2=49\equiv49-45=4\pmod{15}$ (not $\equiv-1\equiv14$,
so the reduction applies). Compute
$\gcd(4-1,15)=\gcd(3,15)=3$ and $\gcd(4+1,15)=\gcd(5,15)=5$, exactly the
two prime factors of $15$. Both gcd computations and the modular
exponentiation are classical arithmetic; the only piece this worked
example *assumed* rather than computed is $r=4$ itself, which the next
two lessons obtain quantum-mechanically.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing Shor's algorithm is "a quantum computer that factors
directly."** It's a classical reduction (factoring → order-finding) plus
one quantum subroutine (order-finding). The factoring step itself, once
$r$ is known, is entirely classical gcd arithmetic.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting to check $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$.** Skipping this check
can produce $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,N)=N$, the trivial "factor" $N$ itself, not
useful information. This is exactly why a failed attempt calls for
picking a new $a$, not for concluding $N$ is prime.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Order-finding: the smallest $r$ with $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$.
- If $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$,
  $\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1,N)$ give nontrivial factors of $N$, derived directly
  from the difference-of-squares identity.
- Every step of the reduction is classical and efficient; only
  order-finding itself needs a quantum speedup for large $N$.
- A random restart strategy handles the two ways the reduction can fail,
  succeeding with probability at least $1/2$ per attempt (a cited, not
  re-derived, number-theory result).
