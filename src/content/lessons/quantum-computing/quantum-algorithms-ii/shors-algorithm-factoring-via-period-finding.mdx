## One sentence holding up RSA

No known *classical* algorithm factors an $n$-digit number in time
polynomial in $n$. That one sentence carries most of the weight of RSA's
security: make $N$ a few hundred digits long, and every classical attack
known runs out of universe. Shor's algorithm is aimed directly at that
sentence.

<Term id="shors-algorithm">Shor's algorithm</Term> factors in polynomial time on a quantum computer, and
the part worth noticing is *where* the <Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum speedup</Term> enters. Not in
factoring. In a narrower subroutine, finding the **order** of a number
modulo $N$, around which the rest of the algorithm is classical number
theory.

That structure is why this lesson contains no quantum mechanics at all.
The reduction is worth understanding on its own terms first, because it
tells you exactly what the quantum computer is being asked to do, and how
small a job it is. Primary source: P. W. Shor, "Polynomial-Time Algorithms
for Prime Factorization and Discrete Logarithms on a Quantum Computer,"
*SIAM J. Comput.* **26**(5), 1484–1509 (1997), an extended version of the
FOCS 1994 presentation, also available as arXiv:quant-ph/9508027.

## Order-finding, and the reduction to it

### The order-finding problem

For integers $a,N$ with $\gcd(a,N)=1$, the **order** of $a$ modulo $N$ is
the smallest positive integer $r$ with $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$. Such an $r$
always exists, as a consequence of Euler's theorem, taken as given here.
`classicalOrder` finds it by brute force, which is useful for checking
answers and for the toy examples this course runs, and useless at the
sizes that matter: brute force is precisely the thing Shor's quantum
subroutine replaces.

### The reduction: factoring from an even order

Suppose you know $r$, the order of some $a$ modulo $N$, and $r$ happens to
be **even**. Then $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$ can be rewritten as

$$
(a^{r/2})^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1) \equiv 0 \pmod N
$$

using the difference-of-squares factorization directly. This means $N$
divides the product $(a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)$. If $N$ divides *neither*
factor individually (which happens precisely when $a^{r/2}\not\equiv\pm1
\pmod N$), then $N$ must share a nontrivial common factor with each one
separately. Computing $\gcd(a^{r/2}-1,\,N)$ and $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,\,N)$
therefore reveals nontrivial factors of $N$, by a classical calculation
(Euclid's algorithm) once $r$ is known.

### When the reduction fails, and why that's still fine

Two things can go wrong: $r$ could be odd (no $a^{r/2}$ to work with at
all), or $a^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod N$ (the product's second factor is
divisible by $N$ outright, giving only the trivial factor $N$ itself from
that side). Both failures are handled the same way: pick a different
random $a$ and try again. It's a standard (but nontrivial, and not
re-derived here) result of number theory that for a randomly chosen $a$
coprime to $N$, the order $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$
with probability at least $1/2$, so a handful of random restarts succeeds
with overwhelming probability. This platform doesn't prove that
probability bound; it's cited as a known classical result the rest of the
algorithm depends on, not derived from scratch.

## Everything classical, except one step

Every step above (the algebra, the gcd computation, the random restarts) is
classical and efficient. A classical computer does all of it in polynomial
time without difficulty. The one step that needs a quantum computer is
**finding $r$** for a large $N$, since brute-force order-finding takes
exponentially many trial multiplications in the worst case.

Shor's contribution is a quantum subroutine for that single step. Keep the
proportions in mind when you read claims about quantum computers breaking
encryption: the quantum part of breaking RSA is one subroutine, and
everything else is arithmetic a laptop can do.

## Factoring fifteen through the reduction

**Given $N=15$, $a=7$: verify $r=4$ (already computed via
`classicalOrder`) leads to genuine factors via the reduction above.**

$r=4$ is even. $a^{r/2}=7^2=49\equiv49-45=4\pmod{15}$ (not $\equiv-1\equiv14$,
so the reduction applies). Compute
$\gcd(4-1,15)=\gcd(3,15)=3$ and $\gcd(4+1,15)=\gcd(5,15)=5$, exactly the
two prime factors of $15$. Both gcd computations and the modular
exponentiation are classical arithmetic; the only piece this worked
example *assumed* rather than computed is $r=4$ itself, which the next
two lessons obtain quantum-mechanically. Laid out as a pipeline, the same
four numbers make the reduction's shape obvious: three plain arithmetic

## The factoring itself is not the quantum part

<Callout type="warning">
**Believing Shor's algorithm is "a quantum computer that factors
directly."** It's a classical reduction (factoring → order-finding) plus
one quantum subroutine (order-finding). The factoring step itself, once
$r$ is known, is entirely classical gcd arithmetic.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to check $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$.** Skipping this check
can produce $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,N)=N$, the trivial "factor" $N$ itself, not
useful information. This is why a failed attempt calls for
picking a new $a$, not for concluding $N$ is prime.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $N=21$, $a=2$: compute the order $r$ of $2$ mod $21$ (by trial
   multiplication), and check whether $r$ is even. Then say what upper
   bound number theory puts on $r$ *before* you start multiplying, and use
   it to say how many trial multiplications you could have needed at
   worst.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Multiply by $2$ and reduce mod $21$ at each step:

   $2^1=2$, $2^2=4$, $2^3=8$, $2^4=16$, $2^5=32\equiv11$, $2^6=22\equiv1$.

   So $r=6$, which is even. `classicalOrder(2, 21)` returns $6$.

   The bound comes from Lagrange's theorem: $r$ must divide the order of
   the multiplicative group mod $21$, which is
   $\varphi(21)=\varphi(3)\varphi(7)=2\times6=12$. So without multiplying
   anything you already know $r\in\{1,2,3,4,6,12\}$, and twelve
   multiplications is the worst case. The sharper bound is Carmichael's
   $\lambda(21)=\mathrm{lcm}(\lambda(3),\lambda(7))=\mathrm{lcm}(2,6)=6$,
   which every element's order divides, so in fact $r\in\{1,2,3,6\}$ and
   six multiplications suffice. Here $r$ hits that ceiling exactly.

   That bound is worth having because it makes the algorithm's whole point
   visible. Brute-force order-finding costs $O(r)$ multiplications, and $r$
   can be as large as $\lambda(N)$, which for a product of two large primes
   is itself about the size of $N$. So brute force is exponential in the
   number of digits, and it is precisely this step, not the gcd's and not
   the difference-of-squares, that Shor's quantum subroutine replaces.

   The wrong turn is stopping at $2^5\equiv11$ and concluding no small $r$
   exists, or reducing incorrectly at $2^6$: $2^6=64$ and $64-63=1$, so
   $64\equiv1$, not $64\equiv22$. Reducing $32$ to $11$ and then doubling
   to $22$ and forgetting to reduce again is the standard slip, and it
   makes $r$ look larger than it is.

   </details>

2. Using the $r$ found in question 1, compute the two gcd's and confirm
   they give nontrivial factors of $21$. Then check the *other* condition
   the reduction needs before those gcd's are guaranteed to be useful, and
   say what you would have done had it failed.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $r=6$, $a^{r/2}=2^3=8$, and $8\bmod 21=8$. Then

   $$
   \gcd(8-1,\,21)=\gcd(7,21)=7, \qquad \gcd(8+1,\,21)=\gcd(9,21)=3
   $$

   and $21=3\times7$. Both are nontrivial, and the engine reproduces both
   gcd's exactly.

   The other condition is $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$. Here
   $-1\equiv20\pmod{21}$ and $a^{r/2}=8\neq20$, so the reduction applies.
   That check is not decoration. If $a^{r/2}\equiv-1$ then $N$ divides
   $a^{r/2}+1$ outright, so $\gcd(a^{r/2}+1,N)=N$ and the other gcd is
   $1$: both answers trivial, no factorization. The lesson's $a=14$,
   $N=15$ case is exactly this, and the engine confirms it returns
   $\gcd(13,15)=1$ and $\gcd(15,15)=15$.

   Had it failed, the response is the same as for an odd $r$: discard this
   $a$, draw a new random $a$ coprime to $N$, and run the order-finding
   again. A standard number-theoretic result, cited in the lesson rather
   than proved, says a random $a$ succeeds with probability at least
   $1/2$, so a handful of restarts succeeds with overwhelming probability.

   The wrong turn is computing $2^3=8$ and then taking $\gcd(8,21)$
   instead of $\gcd(8\pm1,21)$. That returns $1$, since $8$ and $21$ are
   coprime, and it must: $a$ was chosen coprime to $N$, so every power of
   $a$ is too. The whole trick is that $a^{r/2}\mp1$ need not be, and the
   difference of squares is what puts those two numbers in front of you.

   </details>

3. Explain, using the difference-of-squares identity, why an *odd* $r$
   gives the reduction nothing to work with (there's no integer $r/2$).
   Then give a concrete $(a,N)$ pair with odd order, and say what happens
   if you try the reduction anyway.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The reduction begins by rewriting $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$ as
   $(a^{r/2})^2\equiv1$, then factoring the difference of squares:

   $$
   (a^{r/2}-1)(a^{r/2}+1)\equiv0 \pmod N .
   $$

   Every step after this takes gcd's of the two integers $a^{r/2}\mp1$. If
   $r$ is odd, $r/2$ is not an integer, so $a^{r/2}$ is not an integer
   power of $a$ and there are no such integers to hand to Euclid's
   algorithm. There is nothing to fix downstream; the object the rest of
   the reduction operates on does not exist.

   A concrete case: $a=4$, $N=21$. The engine's `classicalOrder(4,21)`
   returns $3$, since $4^1=4$, $4^2=16$, $4^3=64=3\times21+1\equiv1$. Odd.
   Trying the reduction anyway would ask for $4^{1.5}=8$, which happens to
   be an integer here by coincidence, and $\gcd(7,21)=7$, $\gcd(9,21)=3$
   would even give the right factors. That coincidence survives only
   because $4$ is a perfect square. $a^{r/2}$ is meaningful when $r/2$ is an
   integer and not otherwise, so take $a=11$, $N=35$, whose order is $3$
   ($11^2\equiv16$, $11^3\equiv1$): the reduction would ask for
   $11^{3/2}=11\sqrt{11}$, an irrational number with no gcd to take. The
   honest description is that the case does not arise, not that it arises
   and fails gracefully.

   Worth noticing while you are here: mod $15$, every $a$ coprime to $15$
   has even order ($r\in\{2,4\}$ for all seven of them, confirmed by the
   engine), so the odd-$r$ failure never shows up in this course's usual
   example. Mod $21$ it does, at $a=4$ and $a=16$. Choosing $N=15$ for
   worked examples quietly hides one of the reduction's two failure modes.

   The wrong turn is thinking the fix is to use $r$ itself instead of
   $r/2$, giving $\gcd(a^r-1,N)$. That is always $N$ or close to it, since
   $a^r\equiv1$ means $N$ divides $a^r-1$ exactly, so the gcd is $N$: the
   trivial factor, every time. Halving the exponent is what creates two
   numbers whose product $N$ divides while dividing neither.

   </details>

4. If $a^{r/2}\equiv-1\pmod N$ for your chosen $a$, what should you do
   next, and why does trying a *different* $a$ have a reasonable chance of
   avoiding the same failure?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Discard that $a$, pick a new one uniformly at random from the integers
   coprime to $N$, and run order-finding again. Nothing about $N$ needs to
   change, and no partial progress is lost, since the failure was a
   property of the pair $(a,N)$ rather than of $N$.

   The reason a fresh $a$ helps is that the failure is not a property of
   $N$ at all. The standard result, cited in the lesson rather than proved
   there, is that for $N$ odd with at least two distinct prime factors, a
   uniformly random $a$ coprime to $N$ gives an even $r$ with
   $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1$ with probability at least $1/2$. So each attempt
   is an independent coin flip weighted in your favour, and $k$ attempts
   fail with probability at most $2^{-k}$: ten restarts leave under a one
   in a thousand chance of never succeeding.

   The lesson's own sweep is the concrete version. Among the seven bases
   coprime to $15$, only $a=14$ fails, and it fails for exactly this
   reason: $r=2$ is even, but $14\equiv-1\pmod{15}$, so both gcd's come
   back trivial. Six of seven succeed, comfortably above the guaranteed
   half.

   The wrong turn is treating a failed $a$ as evidence that $N$ is prime
   or otherwise resistant. It is evidence about $a$ only. It is also worth
   noticing what the algorithm does *not* need: no cleverness in choosing
   $a$, no search, no structure. Random choice with a constant success
   probability is enough, which is what keeps the expected number of
   quantum runs constant rather than growing with $N$.

   </details>

## Summary

- Order-finding: the smallest $r$ with $a^r\equiv1\pmod N$.
- If $r$ is even and $a^{r/2}\not\equiv-1\pmod N$,
  $\gcd(a^{r/2}\pm1,N)$ give nontrivial factors of $N$, derived directly
  from the difference-of-squares identity.
- Every step of the reduction is classical and efficient; only
  order-finding itself needs a quantum speedup for large $N$.
- A random restart strategy handles the two ways the reduction can fail,
  succeeding with probability at least $1/2$ per attempt (a cited, not
  re-derived, number-theory result).
