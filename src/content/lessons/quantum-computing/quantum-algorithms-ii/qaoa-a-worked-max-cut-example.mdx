## The mechanism, now pointed at two graphs

The last lesson
built the mechanism (cost phases, then a mixer) that biases the
circuit's measurement distribution toward good splits, starting from a
baseline of no optimization at all (expected cut $=|E|/2$). This lesson
runs it on a triangle and on a
single edge, and checks the result against `bruteForceMaxCut`, the true
answer, computable directly since these graphs are small enough to check
every one of the $2^n$ colorings.

Before any of that, the target has to be pinned down, and the triangle's
target is less obvious than it looks. Three vertices, three edges, two
colors.

## One edge, one triangle, and one grid search

### Single edge (2 nodes, 1 edge)

True maximum: `bruteForceMaxCut(2, [[0,1]])` $=1$ (cutting the one edge is
trivially achievable, color the two nodes differently). A grid search
over $\gamma,\beta\in(0,\pi)$ found $\gamma\approx1.55,\ \beta\approx0.40$
gives `expectedCutSize` $\approx0.9997$, within $0.03\%$ of the true
maximum, using a single layer.

### Triangle (3 nodes, all 3 edges)

True maximum: `bruteForceMaxCut(3, [[0,1],[1,2],[0,2]])` $=2$ (any
single-vertex-vs-two-vertex split cuts exactly 2 of the 3 edges. A
triangle can never have all 3 edges cut simultaneously, since that would
need a proper 2-coloring of an odd cycle, impossible). A grid search found
$\gamma\approx0.6,\ \beta\approx0.3$ gives `expectedCutSize`
$\approx1.999$, within $0.05\%$ of the true maximum $2$, again at
$p=1$.

Every optimal triangle coloring is a 1-vertex-vs-2-vertex split (the
"pigeonhole" argument below explains why 2 is the
ceiling); the grid-searched parameters push the circuit's measured
distribution to land on one of these split colorings almost every time:

### Why grid search, not a closed form

Unlike <Term id="vqe">VQE</Term>'s smooth, exactly-solvable single-qubit landscape, QAOA's
$(\gamma,\beta)$ cost landscape for even a small graph has no simple
closed-form optimum. Real QAOA implementations typically use gradient
descent or Bayesian optimization over $(\gamma,\beta)$, run alongside the
quantum circuit in the classical part of the hybrid loop. A grid search
is the smallest honest substitute for that outer loop. It is not a full
classical optimizer, but it finds good parameters, and it finds them by
evaluating the same `qaoaCircuit` and `expectedCutSize` functions a real
optimizer would call.

has to navigate:

## Close but not exact, the honest signature

Both results land close to, but not exactly at, the true maximum: the
honest, typical signature of a heuristic algorithm at low <Term id="circuit-depth">circuit depth</Term>
($p=1$). Real <Term id="qaoa">QAOA</Term> research studies how the approximation ratio (achieved
cut ÷ true maximum) improves as $p$ grows, and whether it can be pushed
arbitrarily close to $1$ for hard instances, an open, actively studied
question this course's two small worked examples can illustrate the
flavor of but not settle.

## Why no odd cycle can be fully cut

**Confirm the triangle result is consistent with the "no odd cycle can be
fully cut" fact used above.**

A triangle's 3 vertices, 2-colored, must have at least two vertices
sharing a color (pigeonhole: 3 vertices, 2 colors). The edge between those
two same-colored vertices is *not* cut. So at most 2 of the 3 edges can
ever be cut, confirming `bruteForceMaxCut`'s answer of $2$ from a
structural argument, not just by checking all 8 colorings by computer.

## Small and simple does not generalize

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming QAOA's near-100% result here generalizes to larger, harder
graphs.** Both worked examples are small and structurally simple. Real
Max-Cut instances at meaningful scale are NP-hard, and QAOA's
approximation ratio in practice depends heavily on graph structure and
circuit depth $p$, not guaranteed to stay this close to optimal.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating the grid-search parameters as universal.** $\gamma\approx0.6,
\beta\approx0.3$ was good specifically for the triangle;
$\gamma\approx1.55,\beta\approx0.4$ was good specifically for the single
edge. Different graphs need their own optimized parameters, found fresh
each time.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the pigeonhole argument from the worked example, explain why
   *any* odd cycle (not just a triangle) has a Max-Cut strictly less than
   its total edge count. Then state the exact Max-Cut of an odd cycle with
   $2k+1$ edges, and check it against the triangle.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Cutting *every* edge of a graph means giving the two endpoints of each
   edge different colours, which is exactly a proper 2-colouring. Walk
   around a cycle of length $n$ and the colours must alternate, so the
   vertices at even positions share one colour and those at odd positions
   share the other. Returning to the start after $n$ steps demands that
   position $n$ and position $0$ differ, and they differ only when $n$ is
   even. For odd $n$ the walk arrives back at the starting vertex with the
   colour it would have to differ from, a contradiction, so at least one
   edge has matching endpoints and Max-Cut $< n$.

   The pigeonhole version for the triangle is the $n=3$ case: three
   vertices into two colours forces two to share, and the edge between
   that pair is uncut.

   The exact answer is $2k$ for a cycle with $2k+1$ edges: exactly one
   edge must go uncut, and one is achievable, by alternating colours all
   the way around and letting the single closing edge match.
   `bruteForceMaxCut` confirms it at every size checked: $n=3$ gives $2$,
   $n=5$ gives $4$, $n=7$ gives $6$. The triangle is $k=1$, so $2$, which
   is the target the whole lesson measures against.

   Worth noticing what this does to the baseline comparison. An odd cycle
   with $2k+1$ edges has a random-guessing baseline of $(2k+1)/2$ and a
   ceiling of $2k$, so as the cycle grows the ratio ceiling-to-baseline
   approaches $2$ but the *absolute* headroom grows without bound. Small
   odd cycles are the hardest case for the approximation ratio and the
   easiest for absolute error.

   The wrong turn is concluding that odd cycles are hard instances for
   Max-Cut. They are not: the answer is $2k$, computable by inspection at
   any size. What they are is instances where the obvious upper bound
   $|E|$ is not achievable, which is what makes them a useful check on any
   algorithm that would otherwise report the trivial ceiling.

   </details>

2. For a 4-node cycle (square: edges (0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,0)), is a
   perfect cut (all 4 edges) possible? Find the 2-coloring that achieves
   it if so.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. The colouring $z=0101$ cuts all four edges, and
   `bruteForceMaxCut(4, square)` returns $4$, the full edge count.

   Check each edge against $z=0101$, reading the bits as vertices
   $0,1,2,3$ in order. Edge $(0,1)$: bits $0$ and $1$, different, cut.
   Edge $(1,2)$: bits $1$ and $0$, cut. Edge $(2,3)$: bits $0$ and $1$,
   cut. Edge $(3,0)$: bits $1$ and $0$, cut. All four.

   The contrast with question 1 is the point. The square is an *even*
   cycle, so alternating colours closes up consistently when the walk
   returns to the start, and the parity obstruction that costs an odd
   cycle one edge simply is not there. The general statement is that a
   graph admits a perfect cut exactly when it is bipartite, and a cycle is
   bipartite exactly when its length is even.

   Note also that the complement $z=1010$ works identically, as it must:
   swapping the two groups leaves every edge's cut status unchanged. Every
   Max-Cut solution comes in such a pair, which is why the QAOA
   distribution in this lesson always shows optimal colourings in
   symmetric pairs rather than singling one out.

   The wrong turn is generalizing from the triangle that "some edge always
   has to give". That is a fact about odd cycles, not about graphs, and
   the square with the same baseline of $|E|/2=2$ reaches double it. A
   quick check on whether the graph is bipartite tells you the ceiling
   before any circuit is run.

   </details>

3. Why does `expectedCutSize` need to average over *all* measurement
   outcomes (weighted by probability) rather than just checking the most
   likely one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the quantity QAOA optimizes is $\langle C\rangle$, the
   expectation of the cost Hamiltonian, and an expectation is by
   definition $\sum_z P(z)\,\mathrm{cut}(z)$ over every outcome. That is
   also the physically honest number: run the circuit once and you get one
   sampled $z$, so the average cut you achieve over many shots is exactly
   this weighted sum, not the cut of the modal outcome.

   Using only the most likely outcome would be wrong in both directions.
   It ignores the probability mass sitting on everything else, which can
   easily be most of it: the triangle at $\gamma=0.6,\beta=0.3$ scores
   $1.999$, and that number comes from spreading nearly all the weight
   across the *six* optimal $1$-versus-$2$ splits, not from one dominant
   peak. Report only the top outcome and you would be describing a
   distribution the circuit does not have. It can also flatter a bad
   circuit, since a state with a slim plurality on the true optimum and
   the rest on terrible colourings would look perfect while performing
   near the baseline.

   The expectation is also what makes the classical optimizer's job
   well-posed. $\langle C\rangle$ is a smooth function of $\gamma$ and
   $\beta$, so a grid search or gradient method has a continuous landscape
   to move on. "Cut size of the most likely outcome" is a step function
   that jumps whenever the argmax changes and is flat in between, which
   gives an optimizer nothing to follow.

   The wrong turn is thinking that because you only get one sample per
   run, only the best outcome matters. In practice you take many samples
   and can keep the best cut you ever saw, which is a legitimate and
   different figure of merit. But that is a statement about
   post-processing, and the quantity the circuit's parameters are tuned
   against is still the expectation.

   </details>

4. What would need to change about this lesson's approach, not
   necessarily in detail, just in kind, to handle a graph with, say, 20
   nodes, where brute-force checking is no longer practical?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Three things, in increasing order of difficulty.

   First, **the ground truth goes away**. `bruteForceMaxCut` checks all
   $2^n$ colourings, which is a million at $n=20$ and still fine, but
   hopeless by $n=50$. Without it there is no "true maximum" to divide by,
   so the approximation ratio becomes unmeasurable and the honest report
   changes to a comparison against a classical heuristic, typically the
   Goemans-Williamson semidefinite relaxation with its $0.878$ guarantee,
   rather than against the optimum.

   Second, **the grid search goes away**. This lesson found
   $\gamma\approx0.6,\beta\approx0.3$ by sweeping a two-dimensional grid,
   which works because $p=1$ has two parameters. At $p$ layers there are
   $2p$ of them, and a grid over $2p$ dimensions is exponential in $p$. A
   real implementation runs an actual classical optimizer on the
   parameters, which is the outer loop the capstone's accounting table
   lists as scoped out here.

   Third, **the state vector goes away**. This lesson computes
   $\langle C\rangle$ exactly from all $2^n$ amplitudes. On hardware you
   cannot see amplitudes at all: you sample measurement outcomes and
   estimate the expectation from a finite number of shots, which
   introduces statistical error of order $1/\sqrt{\text{shots}}$ into the
   very quantity the optimizer is descending. Optimizing a noisy objective
   is a different problem from optimizing an exact one, and it is where
   much of the practical difficulty in NISQ-era variational algorithms
   actually lives.

   What does *not* change is the circuit. $U_C$ is still one two-qubit
   rotation per edge and $U_B$ is still one single-qubit rotation per
   vertex, both of depth independent of $n$. The quantum part scales
   gracefully; the scaffolding around it is what has to be rebuilt.

   The wrong turn is answering "use more qubits and more layers". More
   layers without re-optimized parameters demonstrably makes things worse,
   as the capstone's depth sweep shows, and more qubits without a
   replacement for brute force means you can no longer tell whether the
   answer is any good.

   </details>

## Summary

- $p=1$ QAOA reaches $\approx99.97\%$ of the true maximum for a single
  edge and $\approx99.95\%$ for a triangle, at grid-searched parameters.
- A grid search stands in for QAOA's real classical outer-loop optimizer,
  honestly scoped as a substitute, not a full implementation.
- Both results are checked against `bruteForceMaxCut`, itself only
  practical because these graphs are small, a real limitation that
  doesn't affect QAOA's own circuit, only this course's ability to verify
  it by brute force.
