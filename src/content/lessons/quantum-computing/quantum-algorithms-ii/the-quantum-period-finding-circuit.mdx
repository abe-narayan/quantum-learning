## Two pieces that fit like a key in a lock

Two pieces are already in hand. The last lesson reduced factoring to
finding the period of $f(x)=a^x\bmod N$, and Quantum Algorithms I built a
<Term id="quantum-fourier-transform">QFT</Term> that turns periodic phase patterns into sharp, measurable peaks.

The two fit together the way a key fits a lock, which is not a coincidence:
Shor found the reduction *because* he knew what the QFT could do. Build the
right state, transform the counting register, and read the period off the
positions where the amplitude concentrates.

## The state, the transform, and where the peaks land

### The period-finding state

Using $t$ "counting" qubits and a second register sized to hold values
mod $N$, prepare

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1}|x\rangle\,|a^x\bmod N\rangle
$$

A real circuit builds this via $H^{\otimes t}$ on the counting register
followed by a sequence of controlled modular multiplications (each
counting qubit $q$ conditionally multiplying the second register by
$a^{2^{t-1-q}}\bmod N$, mirroring phase estimation's controlled-$U^{2^k}$
structure exactly). **This platform constructs this exact state directly
from its closed-form amplitudes instead of building that gate sequence.**
`periodFindingState` places <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> $1/\sqrt{2^t}$ at index $(x,\,a^x\bmod
N)$ for every $x$, which is precisely what the real circuit outputs, just
without implementing the controlled-modular-multiplication circuitry
itself. That circuitry is substantial engineering, a quantum-arithmetic
subroutine in its own right, beyond this course's scope and explicitly not
built here. What *is* built and verified is everything downstream: the
QFT-based extraction from this state, which is the <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> mechanism
the algorithm turns on.

### Why the period shows up after the QFT

For a periodic $f$ with period $r$ (so $a^x\bmod N$ repeats every $r$
steps in $x$), grouping the sum by residue class mod $r$ and applying the
QFT to the $x$ register produces constructive interference exactly at
$x$-register outcomes that are multiples of $2^t/r$. This is the same
"amplitude concentrates at exact multiples" structure phase estimation
produced for an exactly-representable eigenphase, because a periodic
function's Fourier transform is itself concentrated at multiples of the
reciprocal period. `quantumFourierTransformOnQubits` (a QFT restricted to
an explicit list of qubits, rather than an entire register) applies the
QFT to just the counting register, leaving the second register alone,
which is what the real circuit's QFT step does.

That rule, "amplitude concentrates at multiples of $2^t/r$," is enough to
say where the peaks land before running anything. Take the case the rest
of this lesson uses and work it out.

Measuring this
distribution and dividing the observed outcome by $2^t$ gives a fraction
very close to $k/r$ for some integer $k$; a classical algorithm called the
**continued fractions algorithm** recovers $r$ from that fraction exactly.
This platform does not implement continued fractions, which is a
well-understood and entirely classical algorithm. The worked example next
lesson identifies $r$ directly from the observed peak locations instead,
which is sufficient to demonstrate the mechanism honestly without
reimplementing a standard classical number-theory routine.

## Periodicity is the structure being exploited

Nothing new happens physically beyond what phase estimation established.
Modular exponentiation supplies a *periodic* function rather than an
arbitrary one, and periodicity is the structure the QFT exposes as a small
set of sharp peaks instead of a spread across every outcome.

That is the whole reason factoring falls to a quantum computer and most
problems do not. Factoring has a period hiding in it, and the QFT is a
period detector. Take away the periodicity and the algorithm has nothing to
grip.

## Predicting the peaks before computing them

**Predict, before computing, where `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution(7,
15, 6)`'s probability peaks should occur.**

$r=4$ (from the previous lesson), $t=6$, so $2^t=64$. Peaks are predicted
at multiples of $64/4=16$: outcomes $0,16,32,48$, four peaks, each with
probability $1/r=0.25$ (since the total probability, $1$, splits evenly
across exactly $r$ peaks by the symmetry of a period-$r$ function's
Fourier transform). This is exactly what the engine computes, not
approximately, to six decimal places.

## What this engine builds directly, and why

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming this platform runs a literal controlled-modular-multiplication
circuit.** It doesn't. `periodFindingState` constructs the *exact output*
of such a circuit directly from its closed-form amplitudes. That is an
explicit, documented scope choice, and it is written down as one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting exactly one sharp peak.** A periodic function with period $r$
spreads its amplitude across every multiple of $2^t/r$, and there are $r$
of those. Order finding needs at least one of them, and the
continued-fractions post-processing step (not implemented here) is what
turns "a peak location" into "the actual value of $r$" reliably. When
$2^t$ happens to be an exact multiple of $r$ (as in this lesson's $a=7$
example, $64/4=16$), those peaks are perfectly sharp: exactly $r$
outcomes, each with probability exactly $1/r$. When it isn't, the
"peaks" smear across roughly $2r$ nearby outcomes instead of landing
exactly on $r$ of them, still usable by continued fractions, but not the
clean picture the sharp case suggests.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $a=4,N=15$ (order $r=2$, check this by direct computation),
   predict where `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution`'s peaks occur
   using $t=6$ counting qubits, and confirm $2^t/r$ is a whole number
   first. Then say what changes about the prediction when $2^t/r$ is
   *not* a whole number, and name an $(a,N)$ pair from this course where
   it isn't.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Check the order first: $4^1=4$ and $4^2=16\equiv1\pmod{15}$, so $r=2$,
   which `classicalOrder(4,15)` confirms. Then $2^t/r = 64/2 = 32$, a whole
   number, so the clean rule applies and the peaks land at multiples of
   $32$ below $64$: outcomes $0$ and $32$, each with probability
   $1/r = 0.5$. The engine returns exactly $0.5$ at $0$ and at $32$, with
   only two nonzero outcomes in the whole distribution.

   When $2^t/r$ is not a whole number the peaks cannot sit on exact
   multiples, because those multiples are not integers and the measurement
   outcome is. What happens instead is that amplitude concentrates at the
   *nearest integers* to $k\cdot2^t/r$, and the rest leaks into a
   surrounding spread rather than being exactly zero.

   The course's own example is $a=2$, $N=21$, where $r=6$ and
   $2^6/6 = 10.667$. The engine's distribution has all $64$ outcomes
   nonzero, but the six largest sit at $0, 11, 21, 32, 43, 53$, which are
   precisely the nearest integers to $0, 10.67, 21.33, 32, 42.67, 53.33$.
   Their probabilities are $0.167$ at $0$ and $32$ (where the multiple is
   exact) and $0.114$ at the four rounded ones, totalling about $79\%$;
   the remaining $21\%$ is spread thinly over the other $58$ outcomes.

   That contrast is the honest picture of the algorithm. The exact-peak
   case is a coincidence of toy examples where $r$ happens to divide
   $2^t$, and no real factoring run enjoys it, since $r$ is unknown and
   generically shares no factor with $2^t$. The wrong turn is generalizing
   from $N=15$ and expecting exactly $r$ nonzero outcomes always; the
   general guarantee is a concentration bound, and it is what makes
   continued fractions necessary rather than optional.

   </details>

2. Explain why the total probability across all peaks must sum to
   exactly $1$, and why (for this platform's exactly-periodic toy
   examples) it splits evenly across exactly $r$ of them. Then say which
   of those two facts survives when $2^t/r$ is not an integer.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The total is $1$ because the distribution is the Born-rule
   distribution of a normalized state, and every gate that produced it,
   the Hadamards, the modular-exponentiation state preparation, and the
   QFT, is unitary. Unitaries preserve the norm, so the squared amplitudes
   still sum to $1$ no matter what the interference did with them. The
   engine confirms $1.000000000$ for every case tested, including the
   messy $N=21$ one.

   The even split has a different source. When $r$ divides $2^t$, the sum
   over $x$ of $e^{2\pi i x k/ (2^t)}$ within each residue class is a
   complete geometric series: it vanishes exactly unless the outcome is a
   multiple of $2^t/r$, and where it does not vanish, every surviving
   outcome gets the same magnitude by symmetry, since the residue classes
   are all the same size. So the probability is $1/r$ on each of $r$
   outcomes, and exactly zero elsewhere. For $a=7,N=15$ that is $0.25$
   four times; for $a=4,N=15$ it is $0.5$ twice.

   Of the two facts, only the first survives. Normalization is a theorem
   about unitarity and holds for every $(a,N,t)$. The even split over
   exactly $r$ outcomes is a special property of the divisible case, and
   at $a=2,N=21$ the engine shows it failing in both respects at once:
   more than $r$ nonzero outcomes ($64$ of them), and unequal peak heights
   ($0.167$ against $0.114$).

   The wrong turn is treating "probabilities sum to one" as evidence the
   peak structure is right. It is evidence of nothing beyond the code
   being unitary; a completely uniform distribution over all $64$ outcomes
   would also sum to $1$ and would carry no information about $r$ at all.
   The two claims have to be checked separately, which is why the lesson
   states them separately.

   </details>

3. What specifically would need to be built for this platform to run
   period-finding on an $N$ too large for brute-force
   `classicalOrder` to check answers against?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Two things, and they are different in kind.

   First, the **controlled modular-exponentiation circuit**. This platform
   writes the period-finding state directly from its closed-form
   amplitudes, placing $1/\sqrt{2^t}$ at index $(x,\ a^x\bmod N)$. That is
   exactly the state a real circuit outputs, and producing it this way
   requires computing $a^x\bmod N$ classically for every $x$, which is
   $2^t$ classical modular exponentiations. Fine for a toy, and the very
   thing a real machine must not do. The replacement is a gate-level
   quantum-arithmetic subroutine: reversible modular multipliers, each
   controlled on one counting qubit, multiplying the target register by
   $a^{2^{t-1-q}}\bmod N$. That is substantial engineering, and the lesson
   is explicit that it is scoped out.

   Second, **continued fractions**. Without `classicalOrder` to check
   against, the measured outcome $x$ has to be turned into $r$ on its own,
   and the reduced fraction of $x/2^t$ is not reliably $1/r$: the
   factoring-15 example's $x=32$ reduces to $1/2$ and undersells the true
   $r=4$. The continued-fraction expansion of $x/2^t$ finds the best
   rational approximation with denominator below $N$, which is provably
   $k/r$, and a few repetitions plus a least-common-multiple step recovers
   $r$ itself.

   Neither is a limitation of the physics, and that is the point worth
   drawing. The interference mechanism, the part that is genuinely
   quantum, is fully built and fully verified here. What is missing is a
   piece of reversible arithmetic and a piece of classical number theory,
   both entirely conventional, and both large enough that building them
   badly would teach less than saying plainly that they are not built.

   The wrong turn is to assume the missing piece is somehow the quantum
   part, or that simulating it would show something new about
   interference. It would not: the state is identical either way, which is
   exactly why building it directly is an honest shortcut rather than a
   fudge.

   </details>

4. Why is choosing $t$ larger than $\log_2N$ (rather than exactly
   $\log_2N$) generally necessary for reliable period recovery in a real
   implementation? (You don't need the full continued-fractions argument,
   just the resolution intuition from phase estimation.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the counting register has to resolve $1/r$ finely enough to
   tell it from its neighbours, and $r$ can be almost as large as $N$.
   Phase estimation with $t$ qubits divides the unit interval into $2^t$
   bins of width $2^{-t}$, and the measured $x/2^t$ is an approximation of
   $k/r$ to within about one bin. To recover $r$ from that approximation
   you need the bins narrower than the gap between neighbouring fractions
   with denominators up to $N$, and two such fractions can be as close as
   $1/N^2$. That forces $2^{-t}<1/N^2$, so $t \gtrsim 2\log_2 N$: roughly
   double, not equal.

   The engine's $a=2,N=21$ run shows the pressure at small scale. With
   $t=6$, $2^t/r = 10.667$ is not an integer, so the peaks are smeared
   over neighbouring outcomes and only about $79\%$ of the probability
   sits on the six main ones. More counting qubits narrow the bins,
   sharpen each peak, and push more probability into it, which is what
   makes a single shot reliable rather than merely suggestive.

   The comparison with the toy cases is the useful part. At $a=7,N=15$
   with $t=6$, $r=4$ divides $64$ exactly, so the peaks are perfect and
   $t=6$ looks generous. That is a coincidence of a small $r$ dividing a
   power of two. In a real run $r$ is unknown, generically shares no
   factor with $2^t$, and the exactness disappears, so the extra qubits
   are buying the resolution the toy example got for free.

   The wrong turn is reasoning that $t=\log_2N$ suffices because the
   answer $r$ is smaller than $N$ and so "fits" in that many bits. Fitting
   is not the constraint; distinguishing is. The measured value is
   $k/r$ for an unknown $k$, and telling $k/r$ apart from every other
   fraction with denominator under $N$ takes about twice as many bits as
   writing $r$ down.

   </details>

## Summary

- The period-finding state $(1/\sqrt{2^t})\sum_x|x\rangle|a^x\bmod
  N\rangle$ is built directly from its closed-form amplitudes, an
  explicit, documented substitute for a real controlled-modular-arithmetic
  circuit this platform doesn't implement.
- Applying the QFT to the counting register concentrates probability at
  exact multiples of $2^t/r$, verified exactly for the $a=7,N=15$ case.
- Recovering $r$ from an observed peak needs the classical continued
  fractions algorithm, also not implemented here: an honestly scoped
  limitation, not a hidden gap.
