## Motivation

Order-finding needs the period of the function $f(x)=a^x\bmod N$, and
periods are exactly what the QFT (Quantum Algorithms I) is built to
extract from a phase pattern. This lesson connects the two: build the
right state, apply the QFT, and read off the period from where the output
amplitude concentrates.

## Mathematical Development

### The period-finding state

Using $t$ "counting" qubits and a second register sized to hold values
mod $N$, prepare

$$
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^t}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^t-1}|x\rangle\,|a^x\bmod N\rangle
$$

A real circuit builds this via $H^{\otimes t}$ on the counting register
followed by a sequence of controlled modular multiplications (each
counting qubit $q$ conditionally multiplying the second register by
$a^{2^{t-1-q}}\bmod N$, mirroring phase estimation's controlled-$U^{2^k}$
structure exactly). **This platform constructs this exact state directly
from its closed-form amplitudes instead of building that gate sequence.**
`periodFindingState` places amplitude $1/\sqrt{2^t}$ at index $(x,\,a^x\bmod
N)$ for every $x$, which is precisely what the real circuit outputs, just
without implementing the controlled-modular-multiplication circuitry
itself. That circuitry is real, substantial engineering (a genuine
"quantum arithmetic" subroutine), well beyond this course's educational
scope, and explicitly not built here. What *is* built and directly
verified is everything downstream: the QFT-based extraction from this
state, which is the actual interference mechanism the algorithm depends
on.

### Why the period shows up after the QFT

For a periodic $f$ with period $r$ (so $a^x\bmod N$ repeats every $r$
steps in $x$), grouping the sum by residue class mod $r$ and applying the
QFT to the $x$ register produces constructive interference exactly at
$x$-register outcomes that are multiples of $2^t/r$. This is the same
"amplitude concentrates at exact multiples" structure phase estimation
produced for an exactly-representable eigenphase, because a periodic
function's Fourier transform is itself concentrated at multiples of the
reciprocal period. `quantumFourierTransformOnQubits` (a QFT restricted to
an explicit list of qubits, rather than an entire register) applies the
QFT to just the counting register, leaving the second register alone,
exactly what the real circuit's QFT step does.

### Checked, not just claimed

For $a=7$, $N=15$ (order $r=4$, from the last lesson) with $t=6$ counting
qubits, `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution` gives probability exactly
$0.25$ at outcomes $0,16,32,48$, precisely the multiples of
$2^6/4=16$, and numerically zero everywhere else. Sampling every fourth
outcome from the real distribution shows exactly this: four sharp peaks
against a flat floor of genuine zeros, not a smoothly-decaying curve:

Measuring this
distribution and dividing the observed outcome by $2^t$ gives a fraction
very close to $k/r$ for some integer $k$; a classical algorithm called the
**continued fractions algorithm** recovers $r$ from that fraction exactly.
This platform does not implement continued fractions (a well-understood,
purely classical algorithm, not a quantum one). The worked example next
lesson identifies $r$ directly from the observed peak locations instead,
which is sufficient to demonstrate the mechanism honestly without
reimplementing a standard classical number-theory routine.

## Physical Interpretation

Nothing new physically happens here beyond what phase estimation already
established. Modular exponentiation just supplies a *periodic* function
instead of an arbitrary one, and periodicity is exactly the structure the
QFT is built to expose as a small set of sharp peaks rather than a spread
across every outcome.

## Worked Example

**Predict, before computing, where `periodFindingMeasurementDistribution(7,
15, 6)`'s probability peaks should occur.**

$r=4$ (from the previous lesson), $t=6$, so $2^t=64$. Peaks are predicted
at multiples of $64/4=16$: outcomes $0,16,32,48$, four peaks, each with
probability $1/r=0.25$ (since the total probability, $1$, splits evenly
across exactly $r$ peaks by the symmetry of a period-$r$ function's
Fourier transform). This is exactly what the engine computes, not
approximately, to six decimal places.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming this platform runs a literal controlled-modular-multiplication
circuit.** It doesn't. `periodFindingState` constructs the *exact output*
of such a circuit directly from its closed-form amplitudes, an explicit
and documented scope choice, not a hidden shortcut.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting exactly one sharp peak.** A periodic function with period $r$
concentrates its amplitude near multiples of $2^t/r$, not into one single
outcome. Order finding needs at least one of them, and the
continued-fractions post-processing step (not implemented here) is what
turns "a peak location" into "the actual value of $r$" reliably. When
$2^t$ happens to be an exact multiple of $r$ (as in this lesson's $a=7$
example, $64/4=16$), those peaks are perfectly sharp: exactly $r$
outcomes, each with probability exactly $1/r$. When it isn't, the
"peaks" smear across roughly $2r$ nearby outcomes instead of landing
exactly on $r$ of them, still usable by continued fractions, but not the
clean picture the sharp case suggests.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The period-finding state $(1/\sqrt{2^t})\sum_x|x\rangle|a^x\bmod
  N\rangle$ is built directly from its closed-form amplitudes, an
  explicit, documented substitute for a real controlled-modular-arithmetic
  circuit this platform doesn't implement.
- Applying the QFT to the counting register concentrates probability at
  exact multiples of $2^t/r$, verified exactly for the $a=7,N=15$ case.
- Recovering $r$ from an observed peak needs the classical continued
  fractions algorithm, also not implemented here: an honestly scoped
  limitation, not a hidden gap.
