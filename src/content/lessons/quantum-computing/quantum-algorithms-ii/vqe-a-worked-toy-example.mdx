<LessonHook eyebrow="Checking the other direction">
Proving an algorithm can't be wrong in one direction is one thing. Proving it actually lands close to the right answer is another. This lesson checks the other direction directly: run the real optimizer on a specific Hamiltonian and see how close it actually gets.
</LessonHook>

## A guarantee about what cannot happen

The last lesson showed <Term id="vqe">VQE</Term>'s cost function cannot dip below the true
ground energy. That is a guarantee about what the algorithm cannot do, and
a guarantee of that shape is compatible with the algorithm being useless:
a program that always returns $+1000$ also never falls below the floor.

So this lesson checks the other direction. A specific <Term id="hamiltonians-time-evolution">Hamiltonian</Term>, the
real optimizer, its real convergence history, and the exact answer computed
independently for comparison.

## One Hamiltonian, the exact answer, and the descent

### The Hamiltonian and its exact answer

$$
H = 0.6\,Z + 0.8\,X
$$

Both coefficients chosen so $\sqrt{0.6^2+0.8^2}=1$ exactly: for any
Hamiltonian of the form $aX+bY+cZ$, the eigenvalues are exactly
$\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$ (a standard fact about <Term id="pauli-matrices">Pauli</Term> combinations,
consistent with this platform's `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` giving the
closed-form quadratic solution directly). So $E_0=-1$ here, exactly,
computed independently via `exactGroundStateEnergy`, not asserted.

`runVqe(H, 80)` starts at $(\theta,\varphi)=(\pi/2,0)$ (the state
$|+\rangle$) and allows *up to* 80 pattern-search steps, but 80 is only a
ceiling on what may run rather than a count of what does: the loop has a second, real
halting condition, stopping as soon as its step size shrinks to $10^{-6}$
or below. For this Hamiltonian that threshold is hit after just 28
iterations (`toyVqeResult.history.length - 1`), so the run exits fewer
than halfway to its 80-step budget without ever coming close to using it.
The cost history decreases monotonically (proven structurally: the
optimizer only ever accepts a strictly better candidate, checked directly
in this course's tests) and converges to an energy within $10^{-12}$ of
$-1$, far tighter than any practical purpose requires. The shrinking step
size, not the iteration cap, is what ends the run.

Hamiltonian, with nothing smoothed for the picture:

### The landscape underneath that descent

The curve above shows cost against iteration count, one number per step. It
does not show the terrain the pattern search was walking across: the
two-parameter surface $\langle\psi(\theta,\varphi)|H|\psi(\theta,\varphi)\rangle$
it was descending. Unlike the last lesson's $H=Z$, whose energy ignores
$\varphi$ altogether, this Hamiltonian has an off-diagonal $X$ term, so
both parameters move the cost and the landscape is a genuine surface
rather than a curve extruded sideways:

The grid's own darkest-green cell sits at $\theta\approx$
{toyLandscapeBestCell.theta.toFixed(2)}, $\varphi\approx$
{toyLandscapeBestCell.phi.toFixed(2)}, giving $\langle H\rangle\approx$
{toyLandscapeBestCell.value.toFixed(4)} (close to, but not exactly on,
the true floor $-1$, since a $17\times17$ grid can't land on the exact
continuous optimum). That is a genuinely *different* point from where
`runVqe` actually converged, $\theta\approx$
{toyVqeResult.params.theta.toFixed(2)}, $\varphi\approx$
{toyVqeResult.params.phi.toFixed(2)}, itself reaching energy
{toyVqeResult.energy.toFixed(4)}, which is $-1$ to four decimal places.
Two different search strategies, an exhaustive grid and an adaptive local
one, landing in two different corners of the same surface, both close to
the same floor: the multiple-minimizers point the second note below makes
concrete, not a contradiction between them.

### Reading the convergence

The optimizer's step size started at $0.5$ radians and halved every time
no candidate direction improved the cost, a direct, visible signal of
approaching a local (here, also global, since the ansatz is fully
expressive) minimum: a smooth cost function's gradient shrinks toward zero
near a minimum, so smaller and smaller steps are needed before any
direction shows further improvement.

## The whole idea at its smallest working scale

This is the whole VQE idea at its smallest working scale. A quantum circuit
measures an energy, a classical loop nudges the circuit's parameters toward
a lower one, and the variational principle means the search always
approaches the answer from above.

Scaling it up changes the difficulty but not the shape. The two things that
get harder are building an ansatz expressive enough to contain a good state
(here trivial, since two angles cover the whole Bloch sphere) and
optimizing over many more parameters without stalling. Both are open
research problems; neither shows up in a single qubit.

## The eigenvalues, by quadratic formula

**Verify $H$'s eigenvalues directly via the quadratic formula, without
citing the general $\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$ shortcut.**

$H=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.8\\0.8&-0.6\end{pmatrix}$ (since
$Z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$).
Trace $=0$, determinant $=(0.6)(-0.6)-(0.8)(0.8)=-0.36-0.64=-1$.
Eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2-0\lambda-1=0$, i.e. $\lambda^2=1$, giving
$\lambda=\pm1$, exactly $E_0=-1$, matching both the shortcut formula and
`exactGroundStateEnergy`'s direct output.

## How many steps convergence actually took

<Callout type="note">
**Expecting VQE to converge in one or two iterations.** Even for this
simple, fully expressive single-qubit case, meaningful convergence took
dozens of pattern-search steps. Larger, more realistic ansätze typically
need many more, and are far more prone to getting stuck in poor local
regions of the cost landscape (an active, ongoing challenge in real VQE
research this toy example is too simple to exhibit).
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming the found parameters $(\theta,\varphi)$ are unique.** Multiple
parameter settings can give the same ground state (global phase, or
different $\varphi$ values when $\theta=0$ or $\pi$ make the state
insensitive to $\varphi$). The *energy* converges reliably; the exact
parameter values found can vary between runs with different starting
points.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $H=0.6Z+0.8X$'s *maximum* eigenvalue (the highest-energy
   state) directly from the quadratic formula. Then say which
   $(\theta,\varphi)$ reaches it, and why the variational principle says
   nothing whatsoever about an optimizer's ability to find it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   As a matrix, $H=\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.8\\0.8&-0.6\end{pmatrix}$, with
   trace $0$ and determinant $-0.36-0.64=-1$. The characteristic equation
   is $\lambda^2-(\text{trace})\lambda+\det=0$, that is
   $\lambda^2-1=0$, so

   $$
   \lambda = \frac{0 \pm \sqrt{0-4(-1)}}{2} = \pm 1 .
   $$

   The maximum eigenvalue is $+1$. This matches the Pauli shortcut
   $\pm\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}=\pm\sqrt{0.36+0.64}=\pm1$, and
   `exactGroundStateEnergy` returns $-1$ for the other root.

   The maximizing parameters follow from the Bloch picture. The ansatz
   state has Bloch vector $(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta)$
   and $\langle H\rangle = 0.6\cos\theta + 0.8\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, which
   is maximized by pointing along $(0.8, 0, 0.6)$: that is $\varphi=0$ and
   $\theta=\arccos(0.6)=0.9273$. `costFunction` at those parameters returns
   $+1.000000000$. The minimum sits antipodally at
   $\theta=\arccos(-0.6)=2.2143$, $\varphi=\pi$, where the engine returns
   $-1.000000000$.

   The variational principle says nothing about reaching the maximum
   because it is a statement about a *lower* bound only:
   $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\geq E_0$ for every state. It provides no
   ceiling, no guarantee of convergence toward anything, and no
   information about the top of the spectrum. If you wanted the maximum
   you would minimize $-H$ instead, and then the principle would apply to
   $-H$'s ground energy, which is $-1$ times this $+1$.

   The wrong turn is inferring from "the optimizer converged to $-1$, the
   exact ground energy" that it would converge equally well to the top. It
   would, for this Hamiltonian, but only because the landscape here is a
   single smooth hill with one maximum and one minimum. Nothing in the
   variational principle underwrites that, and on a landscape with local
   minima the guarantee still holds while convergence fails.

   </details>

2. Explain why starting the optimizer at $(\theta,\varphi)=(0,0)$ (i.e.
   $|0\rangle$) instead of $(\pi/2,0)$ might converge differently, using
   the pattern-search algorithm's local, greedy structure.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Pattern search only ever looks one step in each parameter direction,
   accepts a move if it strictly improves the cost, and shrinks the step
   when nothing does. It has no gradient, no memory, and no model of the
   landscape: every decision is made from the immediate neighbourhood of
   where it currently stands. So the starting point sets which
   neighbourhood it explores first, and therefore the entire path it
   takes.

   The two starting points differ in cost and in direction. `costFunction`
   gives $0.6$ at $(0,0)$, the state $|0\rangle$, and $0.8$ at
   $(\pi/2,0)$, the state $|+\rangle$, so the default start is actually
   the *worse* of the two by $0.2$. It also sits at a different place on
   the sphere relative to the target at $\theta=2.214$, $\varphi=\pi$, so
   the sequence of accepted steps and the number of step-size reductions
   both change. The engine's default run halts after $28$ iterations with
   energy $-0.9999999999993878$.

   For this Hamiltonian the destination is the same either way, and it is
   worth being clear about why: $\langle H\rangle = 0.6\cos\theta+0.8\sin\theta\cos\varphi$
   has exactly one minimum and one maximum on the sphere, with no local
   traps anywhere between them. A greedy method that only ever moves
   downhill cannot get stuck on a landscape with a single basin, so the
   starting point changes the route and not the endpoint.

   The general case is the opposite, and that is what the question is
   pointing at. Multi-qubit ansätze produce landscapes with many local
   minima, and a greedy local search converges to whichever basin it
   started in. That is why real VQE runs use multiple random restarts and
   why the reported energy is an upper bound on the achievable one rather
   than an estimate of $E_0$.

   The wrong turn is expecting a lower starting cost to guarantee a better
   final answer. Here the *higher* start, $|+\rangle$, is the platform's
   default and converges fine, and on a rugged landscape a promising start
   can sit inside a shallow local basin while a worse one sits in the
   global basin. Starting cost predicts nothing about final cost.

   </details>

3. For $H=X$ alone (no $Z$ component), what is $E_0$, and which
   $(\theta,\varphi)$ achieves it?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $E_0=-1$, achieved at $(\theta,\varphi)=(\pi/2,\ \pi)$, the state
   $|-\rangle$.

   $X$'s eigenvalues are $\pm1$ with eigenstates $|\pm\rangle$, so the
   ground state is $|-\rangle$ with energy $-1$;
   `exactGroundStateEnergy(X)` returns $-1$. For the parameters, the
   ansatz is $R_z(\varphi)R_y(\theta)|0\rangle$: $\theta=\pi/2$ produces
   $|+\rangle$, and $R_z(\pi)=\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\pi/2},e^{i\pi/2})=\mathrm{diag}(-i,i)$
   turns it into $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(-i|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)=-i\,|-\rangle$,
   which is $|-\rangle$ up to a global phase. The engine's amplitudes at
   those parameters are $(-0.7071i,\ +0.7071i)$ and `costFunction` returns
   $-1$ exactly.

   In Bloch terms this is simpler: $\langle X\rangle = \sin\theta\cos\varphi$,
   minimized at $-1$ when $\sin\theta=1$ and $\cos\varphi=-1$, that is
   $\theta=\pi/2$ and $\varphi=\pi$, the $-x$ pole. Note that $\varphi$
   matters here, unlike the $H=Z$ case where the energy $\cos\theta$
   ignores $\varphi$ entirely. A Hamiltonian with an off-diagonal term is
   what makes the second parameter do any work at all, which is exactly
   why the lesson's landscape picture for $0.6Z+0.8X$ is two-dimensional
   and the previous lesson's for $H=Z$ was effectively one-dimensional.

   The wrong turn is answering $\theta=\pi/2,\varphi=0$, which gives
   $|+\rangle$ and energy $+1$: the *maximum*, off by the full width of the
   spectrum. The sign of $\cos\varphi$ is the entire difference between the
   best and worst states here, and it is invisible if you reason only about
   $\theta$.

   </details>

4. The lesson's optimizer accepted a candidate only if it strictly
   improved the cost (by more than $10^{-12}$). Why is a small positive
   threshold like this necessary, rather than requiring *any* improvement,
   however tiny?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because "any improvement" is not a well-defined test in floating-point
   arithmetic. Two evaluations of the same cost can differ by a few units
   in the last place purely from rounding in the matrix multiplication and
   the sum, so a candidate that is mathematically identical, or very
   slightly worse, can come out looking $10^{-17}$ better. Accepting on
   that basis lets the optimizer wander on noise: it keeps taking steps,
   never triggers the step-size reduction, and never satisfies its
   termination condition, so the loop runs to its iteration cap while
   making no real progress.

   A threshold comfortably above the noise floor turns "improved" into a
   decision the arithmetic can actually support. Double precision carries
   about $16$ significant digits, so differences below roughly $10^{-15}$
   on order-one quantities are indistinguishable from rounding; $10^{-12}$
   sits a few orders above that, small enough not to stop genuine progress
   and large enough that anything accepted is real.

   The threshold also gives the algorithm its guarantee. Because every
   accepted move improves the cost by at least $10^{-12}$ and the cost is
   bounded below by $E_0$, the number of acceptances is finite, so the
   loop terminates. The lesson's run bears this out: it stops after $28$
   iterations when the step size falls below $10^{-6}$, well short of the
   $80$-iteration ceiling, and reaches $-0.9999999999993878$, within about
   $6\times10^{-13}$ of exact.

   The wrong turn is reading the threshold as a tolerance on the *answer*.
   It is a tolerance on whether a step counts as progress, and it is much
   tighter than the accuracy anyone needs from the final energy. Confusing
   the two leads people to loosen it in the hope of faster convergence,
   which instead makes the optimizer stop early at a visibly worse point.

   </details>

## Summary

- $H=0.6Z+0.8X$ has exact ground energy $-1$, confirmed independently via
  both the quadratic formula and this platform's closed-form eigensolver.
- `runVqe` converges to within $10^{-12}$ of this exact value after just 28
  pattern-search iterations, halted early by its step-size shrinking below
  $10^{-6}$, well short of the 80-iteration budget it was allowed, with a
  monotonically decreasing cost history throughout.
- Shrinking step sizes are a direct signal of approaching a local (here,
  global) minimum of a smooth cost landscape.
