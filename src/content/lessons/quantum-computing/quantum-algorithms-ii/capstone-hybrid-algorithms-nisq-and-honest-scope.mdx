## Motivation

This course built two structurally different families of quantum
algorithm. They differ in more than technique: they assume different
machines, tolerate different failures, and offer different kinds of
guarantee.

This capstone puts them side by side on those three axes, and then does
something a course rarely does to itself. It lists, algorithm by
algorithm, which pieces this platform builds and runs, which it computes
by shortcut, and which it declines to build at all.

## Mathematical Development

### Circuit depth and error tolerance

Shor's algorithm needs a circuit whose depth grows with $N$ (more counting
qubits, more controlled modular multiplications) and whose *entire*
output must be correct. A single bit-flip anywhere in a deep circuit can
corrupt the period-finding measurement beyond recovery, which is why
factoring cryptographically relevant numbers is believed to require
**fault-tolerant** hardware (physical qubits protected by <Term id="quantum-error-correction">quantum error
correction</Term>, the next course's subject) that doesn't exist at scale yet.

VQE and QAOA use comparatively shallow circuits (one ansatz layer, or a
few QAOA layers) run *many times*, with the classical optimizer able to
partially average out or route around some kinds of <Term id="noise-decoherence">noise</Term>. They aren't
immune to it, but they're structurally more tolerant of imperfection than
a single long, must-be-exactly-right circuit.

### The NISQ era, defined

**<Term id="nisq">NISQ</Term>** (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) names the current hardware
era: tens to a few thousand physical qubits, no error correction, limited
circuit depth before noise dominates the signal. VQE and QAOA are the
leading NISQ-era algorithm candidates precisely because their hybrid
structure was designed around these constraints. Shor's algorithm, at
cryptographically relevant scale, is understood to need
**fault-tolerant** hardware, a different, more demanding hardware
regime this platform's `error-correction-and-fault-tolerance` course
covers next.

### A complete accounting

Table of exactly what this course built:

| Algorithm | What was derived | What the engine simulates directly | What was scoped out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deutsch-Jozsa (prior course) | Full circuit and amplitude formula | Full circuit, exactly | Nothing omitted |
| QFT / phase estimation | Full circuit and derivation | Full circuit, exactly | Multi-qubit controlled-$U$ (restricted to $2\times2$ $U$) |
| Grover's | Full circuit and rotation formula | Full circuit, exactly | Nothing omitted |
| Shor's (period finding) | Full number-theory reduction | The period-finding *state* directly (not gate-by-gate) and its QFT analysis, exactly | The controlled-modular-exponentiation circuit itself; continued fractions |
| VQE | Variational principle, ansatz | Full hybrid loop, exactly, for 1 qubit | Multi-qubit ansätze and Hamiltonians |
| QAOA | Cost/mixer derivation | Full circuit, exactly, for small graphs | A real classical optimizer (grid search substitutes); large-graph scaling |

Every "simulates directly, exactly" entry is backed by this course's
tests, not just its lessons. Every "scoped out" entry is a real,
documented limitation, not a claim that this platform secretly does
more than it does.

## Physical Interpretation

The accurate version of "quantum computers will change everything" is
narrower and more interesting than the slogan. Different algorithms need
different hardware regimes, NISQ-tolerant or fault-tolerant. Different
algorithms come with different kinds of guarantee: proven optimal,
promise-dependent, or heuristic with no guarantee at all. A claim that
skips those distinctions is not a claim about anything.

This platform's engine mirrors that. It builds the pieces that demonstrate
each mechanism and says which pieces it does not build.

## Worked Example

**Classify VQE's error tolerance relative to Shor's, using the circuit
depth argument above.**

VQE's ansatz circuit here is exactly 2 gates deep ($R_y$ then $R_z$) run
once per cost evaluation, repeated across many optimizer iterations. A
single bad measurement barely perturbs the overall optimization
trajectory, since the classical loop tries again with adjusted
parameters. Shor's period-finding circuit, by contrast, is one long
circuit (Hadamards, many controlled modular multiplications, a QFT) that
must produce a *single* usable measurement. An error partway through
directly corrupts that one shot, with no built-in mechanism to average it
away. This structural difference, not just historical accident, is why
VQE is considered NISQ-friendly and Shor's algorithm is not. The contrast
in how many independent chances each algorithm gets is drawn from this
course's own worked examples rather than invented for the comparison. What
follows is `runVqe` on the same $H=0.6Z+0.8X$ as the last lesson, and every
candidate direction the pattern search evaluates (one initial evaluation
plus four per-iteration candidates, across every accepted-or-tried
iteration) is a real circuit execution, counted here by
`capstoneVqeCircuitExecutions`:

Shor's algorithm gets one shot at the whole answer. VQE's toy example got
113: one initial evaluation plus four candidate directions tried across
each of the 28 pattern-search iterations, with each execution a fresh
opportunity for the classical loop to route around whatever noise the
previous one left behind. That is many more chances than the iteration
count alone suggests, because the pattern search runs the circuit for every
direction it tries, not only for the ones it keeps.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Believing NISQ-era hardware can already run useful VQE/QAOA at scales
that beat classical computers.** Whether NISQ-era VQE/QAOA achieves a
genuine, practically useful quantum advantage on any real-world problem
remains an open, actively debated research question. This course's toy
examples demonstrate the *mechanism*, not a proven practical advantage at
scale.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "hybrid quantum-classical" as a stopgap until fault-tolerant
hardware arrives.** Even in a future fault-tolerant era, classical
computers remain far cheaper for many subroutines (the gcd computations in
Shor's algorithm, the parameter updates in VQE). Hybrid architectures are
a lasting design pattern, not just a NISQ-era workaround.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Shor's algorithm needs one long, precise, fault-tolerant-scale circuit;
  VQE/QAOA use short, repeated, comparatively noise-tolerant circuits, a
  structural difference driving which hardware era each targets.
- NISQ (today's hardware) favors hybrid algorithms specifically because
  their classical loop can partially compensate for imperfect quantum
  execution.
- This course's full accounting: every genuinely simulated piece is
  tested exactly; every scoped-out piece (modular-exponentiation
  circuitry, continued fractions, multi-qubit ansätze, a real classical
  optimizer for QAOA) is named explicitly, not glossed over.
