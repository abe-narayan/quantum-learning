## Two families that assume different machines

This course built two structurally different families of quantum
algorithm. They differ in more than technique: they assume different
machines, tolerate different failures, and offer different kinds of
guarantee.

This capstone puts them side by side on those three axes, and then does
something a course rarely does to itself. It lists, algorithm by
algorithm, which pieces this platform builds and runs, which it computes
by shortcut, and which it declines to build at all.

## Depth, error tolerance, and the NISQ definition

### Circuit depth and error tolerance

Shor's algorithm needs a circuit whose depth grows with $N$ (more counting
qubits, more controlled modular multiplications) and whose *entire*
output must be correct. A single bit-flip anywhere in a deep circuit can
corrupt the period-finding measurement beyond recovery, which is why
factoring cryptographically relevant numbers is believed to require
**fault-tolerant** hardware (physical qubits protected by <Term id="quantum-error-correction">quantum error
correction</Term>, the next course's subject) that doesn't exist at scale yet.

VQE and QAOA use comparatively shallow circuits (one ansatz layer, or a
few QAOA layers) run *many times*, with the classical optimizer able to
partially average out or route around some kinds of <Term id="noise-decoherence">noise</Term>. They aren't
immune to it, but they're structurally more tolerant of imperfection than
a single long, must-be-exactly-right circuit.

### The NISQ era, defined

**<Term id="nisq">NISQ</Term>** (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) names the current hardware
era: tens to a few thousand physical qubits, no error correction, limited
circuit depth before noise dominates the signal. VQE and QAOA are the
leading NISQ-era algorithm candidates precisely because their hybrid
structure was designed around these constraints. Shor's algorithm, at
cryptographically relevant scale, is understood to need
**fault-tolerant** hardware, a different, more demanding hardware
regime this platform's `error-correction-and-fault-tolerance` course
covers next.

Table of exactly what this course built:

| Shor's (period finding) | Full number-theory reduction | The period-finding *state* directly (not gate-by-gate) and its QFT analysis, exactly | The controlled-modular-exponentiation circuit itself; continued fractions |
| VQE | Variational principle, ansatz | Full hybrid loop, exactly, for 1 qubit | Multi-qubit ansätze and Hamiltonians |
| QAOA | Cost/mixer derivation | Full circuit, exactly, for small graphs | A real classical optimizer (grid search substitutes); large-graph scaling |

Every "simulates directly, exactly" entry is backed by this course's
tests, which is a stronger claim than being backed by its lessons. Every
"scoped out" entry names a real, documented limitation, and none of them
quietly means the opposite.

## The accurate version of the slogan

The accurate version of "quantum computers will change everything" is
narrower and more interesting than the slogan. Different algorithms need
different hardware regimes, NISQ-tolerant or fault-tolerant. Different
algorithms come with different kinds of guarantee: proven optimal,
promise-dependent, or heuristic with no guarantee at all. A claim that
skips those distinctions is not a claim about anything.

This platform's engine mirrors that. It builds the pieces that demonstrate
each mechanism and says which pieces it does not build.

## Classifying VQE's tolerance against Shor's

**Classify VQE's error tolerance relative to Shor's, using the circuit
depth argument above.**

VQE's ansatz circuit here is exactly 2 gates deep ($R_y$ then $R_z$) run
once per cost evaluation, repeated across many optimizer iterations. A
single bad measurement barely perturbs the overall optimization
trajectory, since the classical loop tries again with adjusted
parameters. Shor's period-finding circuit, by contrast, is one long
circuit (Hadamards, many controlled modular multiplications, a QFT) that
must produce a *single* usable measurement. An error partway through
directly corrupts that one shot, with no built-in mechanism to average it
away. That structural difference, rather than any historical accident, is why
VQE is considered NISQ-friendly and Shor's algorithm is not. The contrast
in how many independent chances each algorithm gets is drawn from this
course's own worked examples rather than invented for the comparison. What
follows is `runVqe` on the same $H=0.6Z+0.8X$ as the last lesson, and every
candidate direction the pattern search evaluates (one initial evaluation
plus four per-iteration candidates, across every accepted-or-tried
iteration) is a real circuit execution, counted here by
`capstoneVqeCircuitExecutions`:

direction it tries, including every direction it then discards.

## Useful NISQ advantage is still an open question

<Callout type="note">
**Believing NISQ-era hardware can already run useful VQE/QAOA at scales
that beat classical computers.** Whether NISQ-era VQE/QAOA achieves a
genuine, practically useful quantum advantage on any real-world problem
remains an open, actively debated research question. This course's toy
examples demonstrate the *mechanism*. Whether that mechanism pays off at
scale is precisely what remains unsettled.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "hybrid quantum-classical" as a stopgap until fault-tolerant
hardware arrives.** Even in a future fault-tolerant era, classical
computers remain far cheaper for many subroutines (the gcd computations in
Shor's algorithm, the parameter updates in VQE). Hybrid architectures are
a lasting design pattern that will outlive the NISQ era that prompted it.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the depth argument, explain why increasing QAOA's layer count
   $p$ (making the circuit deeper) buys accuracy at a cost on NISQ-era
   hardware, and say what that cost is.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Each extra layer adds one cost unitary and one mixer, so the circuit
   grows by one two-qubit rotation per edge plus one single-qubit rotation
   per vertex. On a device without error correction, every gate carries a
   failure probability, and those probabilities compound: a circuit of
   depth $d$ with per-gate error $\varepsilon$ retains roughly
   $(1-\varepsilon)^{d}$ of its signal, which decays exponentially in $d$.
   Past a depth set by the hardware's coherence time, the measured
   distribution is dominated by noise and the expectation you are
   estimating stops being the circuit's.

   The accuracy side of the trade is real: at $p\to\infty$ with optimal
   parameters QAOA provably reaches the exact optimum, by an adiabatic
   argument. So depth genuinely buys quality *in principle*, and noise is
   what stops you spending it.

   There is a second cost, and this lesson's own numbers make it the
   sharper one. Repeating the $p=1$-tuned pair $\gamma=0.60,\beta=0.30$
   unchanged at every layer, the engine gives expected cuts of
   $1.999,\ 1.544,\ 1.460,\ 1.992,\ 1.592,\ 1.424$ for $p=1$ through $6$.
   That is not a climb, it is a swing, and at $p=3$ and $p=6$ it falls
   *below* the $1.5$ baseline that random guessing achieves. Extra layers
   help only with parameters re-optimized for that depth, and the
   parameter space grows to $2p$ dimensions, so the classical outer loop
   gets exponentially harder to search at the same time the quantum
   circuit gets noisier.

   The wrong turn is treating $p$ as an accuracy dial you turn until
   satisfied. Depth alone buys nothing at all, as the swing shows, and on
   hardware it actively costs. The honest statement is that $p$ trades
   classical optimization effort and circuit fidelity for a better
   achievable optimum, and on current devices the binding constraint is
   usually the fidelity.

   </details>

2. Why does Shor's algorithm's period-finding step specifically need many
   *coherent* qubits (no measurement until the very end), while VQE can
   measure after every single ansatz execution?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the two algorithms locate their answer in different places.
   Shor's answer lives in an interference pattern across the whole
   counting register: the QFT's cancellation depends on the relative
   phases of all $2^t$ branches being intact simultaneously, and measuring
   any counting qubit early collapses the superposition and destroys the
   pattern before it can form. Every gate from the initial Hadamards
   through the modular exponentiation to the QFT has to preserve coherence
   across the entire register, so the coherence requirement is the full
   circuit depth times the full register width.

   VQE's answer is an expectation value, $\langle\psi(\vec\theta)|H|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$,
   which is a statistical average over many independent runs. Each run
   prepares a shallow ansatz state, measures, and throws the state away.
   Coherence is needed only for the length of one ansatz circuit, and
   nothing has to survive from one shot to the next, because the
   classical optimizer, not the quantum state, is what carries information
   between iterations.

   That difference is the entire NISQ argument in one sentence: Shor's
   algorithm needs one long thing to go right, VQE needs many short things
   to go right on average. Averaging is also why VQE tolerates some noise
   structurally rather than accidentally: a noise process that shifts the
   measured expectation slightly still leaves the optimizer a landscape to
   descend, whereas a single bit flip mid-circuit in Shor's period-finding
   corrupts the measured outcome with no way to detect it from that shot
   alone.

   The wrong turn is describing VQE as noise-immune. It is not. Noise
   biases the estimated energy, and because the variational principle
   bounds only the *exact* expectation, a noisy estimate can and does dip
   below $E_0$, which is exactly the symptom you would otherwise read as a
   bug. What VQE has is a structure that tolerates *some* noise, not
   protection from it, and that distinction is what error correction
   exists to close.

   </details>

3. From this capstone's table, name one algorithm this course implements
   with zero scope limitations, and one with the most scope limitations.
   What's different about their underlying mathematical structure that
   explains the difference?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Zero limitations: Grover's algorithm, and Deutsch-Jozsa alongside it,
   both marked "Nothing omitted". Most limitations: Shor's period finding,
   with the controlled-modular-exponentiation circuit and continued
   fractions both scoped out.

   The structural difference is what each algorithm needs from its
   function. Grover and Deutsch-Jozsa are *black-box* algorithms: they
   assume an oracle exists and their entire analysis is in terms of how
   many times it is queried, never how it is built. A simulator can
   therefore implement the oracle as a sign flip on marked indices,
   `applyPhaseOracle`, which is honest because the algorithm genuinely
   does not care what is inside. Query complexity is the whole subject,
   and query complexity is fully implemented here.

   Shor's algorithm is not a black-box algorithm. Its function is a
   *specific* one, $f(x)=a^x\bmod N$, and the speedup depends on that
   function being computable by a polynomial-size reversible circuit.
   There is no oracle to postulate, because the thing being asserted is
   precisely that this arithmetic can be done quantum-mechanically at
   scale. Building the state directly from its closed-form amplitudes, as
   this platform does, reproduces the state exactly and skips the claim
   that makes the algorithm work. Continued fractions is scoped out for a
   different and lesser reason: it is ordinary classical number theory,
   omitted for effort rather than for difficulty of principle.

   The general lesson is that "scoped out" means different things. A
   black-box algorithm has nothing hidden in its oracle by construction; a
   structured algorithm has its entire advantage hidden there. The honest
   version of this course's Shor's implementation is that it demonstrates
   the interference mechanism completely and demonstrates the speedup not
   at all.

   The wrong turn is ranking by the number of table entries rather than by
   what the entries mean. QAOA also lists two omissions, a real classical
   optimizer and large-graph scaling, but both are engineering an
   interested reader could add without new physics. Shor's missing piece
   is the part the whole claim rests on.

   </details>

4. In your own words, summarize the difference between "provably optimal"
   (Grover's), "promise-dependent exponential" (Deutsch-Jozsa), and
   "heuristic, no guarantee" (QAOA) as three distinct *kinds* of quantum
   advantage claim. Then say which of the three this course's own QAOA
   results demonstrate, and what would have to be shown to upgrade it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>A defensible answer</summary>

   Marked on the distinctions drawn, not on wording. One version in full,
   then alternatives, then what fails.

   A worked version. **Provably optimal** is a two-sided claim: Grover
   achieves $O(\sqrt N)$ queries, and a matching lower bound says no
   quantum algorithm can do better for unstructured search. Both halves
   are theorems, they hold against every algorithm that will ever be
   written, and nothing about the claim is waiting on future work.
   **Promise-dependent exponential** is one-sided and conditional:
   Deutsch-Jozsa beats every *deterministic* classical algorithm
   exponentially, but only on inputs satisfying a promise nobody
   encounters, and the gap collapses to nothing once the classical side is
   allowed randomness and a one-in-a-billion error rate. **Heuristic, no
   guarantee** is neither: QAOA has no proof it beats classical methods on
   any problem, no lower bound protecting it, and its quality depends on
   the graph, the depth, and how well a classical optimizer happens to
   find parameters. It is a candidate, not a result.

   This course's QAOA results are firmly in the third category, and it is
   worth being precise about how narrowly. The triangle at
   $\gamma=0.6,\beta=0.3$ reaches an expected cut of $1.999$ against a
   true maximum of $2$, which is excellent and proves nothing: the
   parameters were found by grid search over a two-dimensional space on a
   three-vertex graph whose answer was already computed by brute force. No
   classical algorithm was outperformed, and Max-Cut on a triangle is not
   a hard problem.

   Upgrading it would take a comparison the course never makes: QAOA at
   some depth $p$, on a family of graphs large enough that brute force is
   impossible, beating the best classical approximation algorithm, which
   for Max-Cut means beating Goemans-Williamson's $0.878$ guarantee, with
   the parameter-finding cost counted honestly on the classical side. That
   comparison is an open research question, and no version of it has been
   settled in QAOA's favour.

   Equally defensible framings. You can organize the three by what varies:
   optimality quantifies over all algorithms, promise-dependence
   quantifies over all inputs satisfying a condition, heuristics quantify
   over nothing. You can organize by what would refute each: a faster
   quantum search algorithm would refute Grover's optimality and cannot
   exist; a better classical algorithm would refute Deutsch-Jozsa's gap
   and already does; and QAOA has nothing to refute because it claims
   nothing. You can also note that only the first is robust to the
   classical side improving, which is the property that matters for
   long-range planning.

   What a bad answer looks like: ranking them by speedup size, so
   exponential beats quadratic beats heuristic. That gets the ordering
   backwards on the axis that matters, since Grover's smaller speedup is
   the only one that is guaranteed. Also wrong is calling QAOA's lack of a
   guarantee a temporary gap awaiting a proof; it may equally await a
   disproof, and treating an open question as a pending confirmation is
   the specific error the capstone's Sycamore note warns about.

   </details>

## Summary

- Shor's algorithm needs one long, precise, fault-tolerant-scale circuit;
  VQE/QAOA use short, repeated, comparatively noise-tolerant circuits, a
  structural difference driving which hardware era each targets.
- NISQ (today's hardware) favors hybrid algorithms specifically because
  their classical loop can partially compensate for imperfect quantum
  execution.
- This course's full accounting: every genuinely simulated piece is
  tested exactly; every scoped-out piece (modular-exponentiation
  circuitry, continued fractions, multi-qubit ansätze, a real classical
  optimizer for QAOA) is named explicitly, not glossed over.
