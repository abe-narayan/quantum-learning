Max-Cut asks you to split a graph's vertices into two groups to maximize the number of edges crossing between them — an NP-hard problem QAOA attacks with the same hybrid loop VQE uses for ground energies.

## Motivation

**QAOA** — the quantum approximate optimization algorithm — is that
same hybrid loop, a parameterized circuit and a classically-optimized
cost, retargeted from finding a ground energy to combinatorial
optimization. Max-Cut is the first, standard example.

## Mathematical Development

### Max-Cut as a Hamiltonian

Represent a 2-coloring of $n$ vertices as a computational basis state
$|z\rangle$, $z\in\{0,1\}^n$. For each edge $(i,j)$, define
$Z_i,Z_j$'s eigenvalues as $+1$ (bit 0) or $-1$ (bit 1). Then
$\frac{1-Z_iZ_j}{2}$ equals $1$ exactly when $i,j$ have *different* bits
(the edge is cut) and $0$ when they match. Summing over every edge:

$$
C = \sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1-Z_iZ_j}{2}
$$

is a Hamiltonian whose eigenvalue on $|z\rangle$ is exactly the number of
edges cut by that particular 2-coloring. Maximizing $\langle C\rangle$ is
literally Max-Cut.

For a 3-vertex triangle graph (every pair connected, 3 edges total), the
coloring $z=100$ (vertex 0 in one group, vertices 1 and 2 in the other)
cuts exactly 2 of the 3 edges:

Edges 0-1 and 0-2 cross between groups (dashed, cut); edge 1-2 sits

dashed edges.

### The cost unitary is a pure phase

$C$ is diagonal in the computational basis (built entirely from $Z$
operators), so $U_C(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma C}$ acts on each basis state by a
phase equal to its own eigenvalue:

$$
U_C(\gamma)|z\rangle = e^{-i\gamma\cdot(\text{cut count of }z)}|z\rangle
$$

`applyCostUnitary` implements exactly this, computing each basis index's
cut count directly (summing $1$ for every edge whose two endpoint bits
differ) and multiplying by the corresponding phase, with **no change to
any measurement probability** (a pure phase operator, checked directly:
probabilities before and after `applyCostUnitary` match to $10^{-9}$).

### The mixer

$U_C$ alone can't change which outcomes are likely. It only adds phases.
The **mixer** $U_B(\beta)=e^{-i\beta B}$, $B=\sum_iX_i$, is what actually
moves amplitude between basis states. Because $B$ is a sum of single-qubit
$X$ terms acting on different qubits, those terms commute and $e^{-i\beta
B}$ factors into a tensor product of single-qubit exponentials
$e^{-i\beta X_i}$, one per qubit. With this platform's rotation
convention $R_x(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2}$, each factor $e^{-i\beta X_i}$
is exactly $R_x(2\beta)$ on qubit $i$, so $U_B(\beta)$ is implemented as
$R_x(2\beta)$ applied independently to every qubit (`applyMixerUnitary`),
reusing the ordinary single-qubit rotation gate, not a new primitive. Alternating $U_C$ and
$U_B$ for $p$ layers, starting from the uniform superposition
(`qaoaCircuit`), is the full QAOA circuit; $p=1$ is the smallest
nontrivial case this course's worked example uses.

### A checkable baseline

Before running any optimization, check the *starting point*: the uniform
superposition itself, with no cost/mixer layers applied at all, gives
every basis state equal probability $1/2^n$, so the expected cut size is
just the average over *all* $2^n$ colorings. For any single edge, exactly
half of all colorings cut it (matching the endpoints' bits differently)
and half don't, so the expected cut count from the uniform superposition
alone is exactly $|E|/2$, half the edge count, for *any* graph. For a
3-edge triangle: `expectedCutSize` on the plain uniform superposition
gives exactly $1.5$, confirmed directly, not just argued.

## Physical Interpretation

QAOA's cost unitary "tags" each candidate coloring with a phase
proportional to its quality, and the mixer lets those phases interfere.
This is structurally the same idea as Grover's oracle-then-diffusion
pattern, now applied to a continuous-valued cost instead of a single
marked item, and tuned by a classical optimizer choosing $\gamma,\beta$
rather than a fixed formula.

## Worked Example

**Confirm the uniform-superposition baseline for a single edge (2 nodes,
1 edge): expected cut size should be exactly $0.5$.**

Two colorings cut the edge ($|01\rangle,|10\rangle$), two don't
($|00\rangle,|11\rangle$), each with probability $0.25$ in the uniform
superposition. Expected cut size
$=0.25(0)+0.25(1)+0.25(1)+0.25(0)=0.5$, exactly half of the single edge,
matching the general $|E|/2$ rule and the engine's direct output.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the cost unitary alone to improve the measured cut size.**
It's a pure phase operator. Measurement probabilities (and hence the
expected cut size) are completely unchanged by $U_C$ on its own.
Improvement only happens once the mixer lets those phases turn into
amplitude changes.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming QAOA always finds the exact maximum cut.** It's a heuristic,
approximate algorithm. The next lesson's worked example shows $p=1$
QAOA reaching *close to* but not exactly the true maximum, and more
layers ($p>1$) generally improve, but don't guarantee, further
convergence.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Max-Cut's cost Hamiltonian $C=\sum(1-Z_iZ_j)/2$ has eigenvalue equal to
  the cut count, for any coloring.
- The cost unitary is a pure phase operator (verified: leaves every
  probability unchanged); the mixer is what actually moves amplitude.
- The uniform superposition's expected cut size is exactly $|E|/2$ for
  any graph, a checkable baseline before any optimization runs.
