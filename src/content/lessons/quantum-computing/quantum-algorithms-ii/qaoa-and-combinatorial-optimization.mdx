<LessonHook eyebrow="Splitting a graph, one phase at a time">
Max-Cut asks you to split a graph's vertices into two groups so that as many edges as possible cross between them. It is NP-hard, and QAOA attacks it with the same hybrid loop VQE uses for ground energies.
</LessonHook>

## VQE's loop, aimed at a graph

**<Term id="qaoa">QAOA</Term>** (the quantum approximate optimization algorithm) is VQE's hybrid
loop, a parameterized circuit with a classically-optimized cost, pointed at
combinatorial optimization instead of ground-state energy.

The retargeting takes one idea: write the thing you want to maximize as a
Hamiltonian whose eigenvalue on each candidate answer *is* that answer's
score. Once you have that, "find the best coloring" and "find the lowest
energy" are the same problem, and VQE's machinery transfers unchanged.
Max-Cut is the standard first example, and the encoding takes two lines.

## A cost Hamiltonian, a phase, and a mixer

### Max-Cut as a Hamiltonian

Represent a 2-coloring of $n$ vertices as a computational basis state
$|z\rangle$, $z\in\{0,1\}^n$. For each edge $(i,j)$, define
$Z_i,Z_j$'s eigenvalues as $+1$ (bit 0) or $-1$ (bit 1). Then
$\frac{1-Z_iZ_j}{2}$ equals $1$ exactly when $i,j$ have *different* bits
(the edge is cut) and $0$ when they match. Summing over every edge:

$$
C = \sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1-Z_iZ_j}{2}
$$

is a <Term id="hamiltonians-time-evolution">Hamiltonian</Term> whose eigenvalue on $|z\rangle$ is exactly the number of
edges cut by that particular 2-coloring. Maximizing $\langle C\rangle$ is
literally Max-Cut.

For a 3-vertex triangle graph (every pair connected, 3 edges total), the
coloring $z=100$ (vertex 0 in one group, vertices 1 and 2 in the other)
cuts exactly 2 of the 3 edges:

Before building any circuit at all, check the *starting point* QAOA begins
from: the uniform superposition gives every basis state equal probability
$1/2^n$, so the expected cut size is just the average of $C$ over *all*
$2^n$ colorings. For any single edge, exactly half of all colorings cut it
(matching the endpoints' bits differently) and half don't, so the expected
cut count from the uniform superposition alone is exactly $|E|/2$, half
the edge count, for *any* graph. For a 3-edge triangle: `expectedCutSize`
on the plain uniform superposition gives exactly $1.5$, confirmed
directly, not just argued.

### The cost unitary is a pure phase

$C$ is diagonal in the computational basis (built entirely from $Z$
operators), so $U_C(\gamma)=e^{-i\gamma C}$ acts on each basis state by a
phase equal to its own eigenvalue:

$$
U_C(\gamma)|z\rangle = e^{-i\gamma\cdot(\text{cut count of }z)}|z\rangle
$$

`applyCostUnitary` implements exactly this, computing each basis index's
cut count directly (summing $1$ for every edge whose two endpoint bits
differ) and multiplying by the corresponding phase, with **no change to
any measurement probability** (a pure phase operator, checked directly:
probabilities before and after `applyCostUnitary` match to $10^{-9}$).

### The mixer

$U_C$ alone can't change which outcomes are likely. It only adds phases.
The **mixer** $U_B(\beta)=e^{-i\beta B}$, $B=\sum_iX_i$, is what actually
moves amplitude between basis states. Because $B$ is a sum of single-qubit
$X$ terms acting on different qubits, those terms commute and $e^{-i\beta
B}$ factors into a tensor product of single-qubit exponentials
$e^{-i\beta X_i}$, one per qubit. With this platform's rotation
convention $R_x(\theta)=e^{-i\theta X/2}$, each factor $e^{-i\beta X_i}$
is exactly $R_x(2\beta)$ on qubit $i$, so $U_B(\beta)$ is implemented as
$R_x(2\beta)$ applied independently to every qubit (`applyMixerUnitary`),
reusing the ordinary single-qubit rotation gate, not a new primitive. Alternating $U_C$ and
$U_B$ for $p$ layers, starting from the uniform superposition
(`qaoaCircuit`), is the full QAOA circuit; $p=1$ is the smallest
nontrivial case this course's worked example uses.

## Tagging colorings, then letting them interfere

QAOA's cost unitary "tags" each candidate coloring with a phase
proportional to its quality, and the mixer lets those phases <Term id="phase-interference">interfere</Term>.
This is structurally the same idea as Grover's oracle-then-diffusion
pattern, now applied to a continuous-valued cost instead of a single
marked item, and tuned by a classical optimizer choosing $\gamma,\beta$
rather than a fixed formula.

## The baseline for a single edge

**Confirm the uniform-superposition baseline for a single edge (2 nodes,
1 edge): expected cut size should be exactly $0.5$.**

Two colorings cut the edge ($|01\rangle,|10\rangle$), two don't
($|00\rangle,|11\rangle$), each with probability $0.25$ in the uniform
superposition. Expected cut size
$=0.25(0)+0.25(1)+0.25(1)+0.25(0)=0.5$, exactly half of the single edge,
matching the general $|E|/2$ rule and the engine's direct output.

## A pure phase changes no probability

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the cost unitary alone to improve the measured cut size.**
It is a pure phase operator, so measurement probabilities, and with them
the expected cut size, are unchanged by $U_C$ alone. Improvement happens
only once the mixer converts those phases into amplitude differences.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming QAOA always finds the exact maximum cut.** It is a heuristic,
approximate algorithm with no optimality guarantee. The next lesson's
worked example shows $p=1$ QAOA landing close to but not on the true
maximum, and adding layers ($p>1$) usually helps without promising to.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the expected cut size of the uniform superposition for a
   4-edge graph, using the $|E|/2$ rule. Then say what the rule does and
   does not depend on about the graph, and give one 4-edge graph whose
   true Max-Cut is $4$ and one whose true Max-Cut is $3$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|E|/2 = 4/2 = 2$. The engine's `expectedCutSize` on
   `uniformSuperposition(4)` for the 4-cycle returns $2.0000000000000004$.

   The rule depends on the edge count and on nothing else. The derivation
   is one line of linearity of expectation: for any single edge, the two
   endpoint bits are independent and uniform under the uniform
   superposition, so they differ with probability exactly $\tfrac12$, and
   the edge contributes $\tfrac12$ to the expected cut. Summing over $|E|$
   edges gives $|E|/2$. Nothing in that argument mentions how the edges
   are arranged, whether the graph is connected, or how many vertices
   there are.

   The true Max-Cut is a completely different story, and the two examples
   show it. The 4-cycle with edges $(0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,0)$ has Max-Cut
   $4$: colour it alternately, $z=0101$, and every edge crosses.
   `bruteForceMaxCut(4, square)` returns $4$. A triangle with a pendant
   edge, $(0,1),(1,2),(0,2),(2,3)$, also has four edges and the same
   baseline of $2$, but its Max-Cut is $3$: the triangle can never give up
   more than $2$ of its edges, and the pendant edge is always cuttable.
   `bruteForceMaxCut` returns $3$.

   That gap is exactly what QAOA is being asked to close, and its size
   varies by graph even at fixed edge count. The baseline is free and
   uninformative; the ceiling requires knowing the graph's structure, and
   finding it is the NP-hard part.

   The wrong turn is reading $|E|/2$ as a property of the *algorithm*
   rather than of random guessing. It is what you get with no circuit at
   all, which is why the lesson insists it is the number every QAOA result
   has to beat before it has shown anything. A run reporting an expected
   cut of $2$ on a four-edge graph has demonstrated precisely nothing.

   </details>

2. Explain why $U_C(\gamma)$'s action can be computed directly from a
   basis state's index alone (no matrix multiplication needed), using its
   diagonal structure.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $C$ is built entirely out of $Z$ operators, and $Z$ is diagonal in the
   computational basis, so products and sums of $Z$'s are diagonal too.
   A diagonal $C$ has every computational basis state as an eigenstate,
   with eigenvalue equal to that state's cut count. Exponentiating a
   diagonal matrix exponentiates its entries, so

   $$
   U_C(\gamma)|z\rangle = e^{-i\gamma\,C}|z\rangle = e^{-i\gamma\,\mathrm{cut}(z)}|z\rangle .
   $$

   Computationally that means: take the basis index $z$, read its bits,
   count the edges whose two endpoint bits differ, and multiply that one
   amplitude by a phase. No row of a matrix is ever touched, and no
   amplitude is ever mixed with another. `applyCostUnitary` does exactly
   this, one index at a time.

   The cost saving is the reason it matters. A general $2^n\times2^n$
   matrix-vector product is $O(4^n)$ multiply-adds. Here the work is
   $O(2^n\cdot|E|)$: one pass over the amplitudes, counting edges for
   each. And on real hardware the same structure means $U_C$ compiles into
   one two-qubit $R_{zz}$ rotation per edge, a circuit whose depth grows
   with the graph rather than with the state space.

   The consequence worth carrying to the next question is that $U_C$
   changes no measurement probability at all, since multiplying an
   amplitude by a unit-modulus phase leaves $|c_z|^2$ alone. The engine
   confirms this directly: probabilities before and after
   `applyCostUnitary` match to machine precision. All $U_C$ does is write
   information into phases, and something else has to convert it into
   amplitude.

   The wrong turn is expecting a "cost" operator to make good colourings
   more likely by itself. It cannot, for the reason just given, and a QAOA
   circuit consisting of cost layers alone would return the uniform
   distribution however many layers you stacked. The mixer is not a
   refinement; it is the half that does anything visible.

   </details>

3. For a single edge, write out $U_C(\gamma)$'s phase on each of the 4
   basis states explicitly ($|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The cut count of a single edge $(0,1)$ is $1$ when the two bits differ
   and $0$ when they match:

   $|00\rangle \to e^{0} = 1$, unchanged.
   $|01\rangle \to e^{-i\gamma}$.
   $|10\rangle \to e^{-i\gamma}$.
   $|11\rangle \to e^{0} = 1$, unchanged.

   So $U_C(\gamma)=\mathrm{diag}\left(1,\ e^{-i\gamma},\ e^{-i\gamma},\ 1\right)$.
   The engine bears this out at $\gamma=0.7$ on the uniform superposition:
   the $|00\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$ amplitudes stay at $0.5$, while
   $|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ become $0.382421-0.322109i$, which is
   exactly $0.5\,e^{-0.7i}$ since $e^{-0.7i}=0.764842-0.644218i$.

   Two features are worth reading off. The phase pattern is symmetric
   under flipping both bits, because $z$ and its complement cut the same
   edges, so QAOA can never distinguish a colouring from its mirror image.
   That degeneracy is harmless for Max-Cut, where both are equally good
   answers, and it means the measured distribution always comes in
   complementary pairs. And the two cut colourings receive the *same*
   phase as each other, so no coloring is singled out: what the cost layer
   creates is a phase *difference* between the cut group and the uncut
   group, of size $\gamma$.

   That difference is the raw material for interference, and $\gamma$ is
   the dial controlling how much of it there is. At $\gamma=0$ there is
   none and QAOA reduces to random guessing; at $\gamma=2\pi$ the phases
   wrap around to identical again, which is why the parameter is periodic
   rather than monotonic and why the grid search in the next lesson
   searches over a bounded interval.

   The wrong turn is writing $e^{-i\gamma}$ on $|11\rangle$ on the
   reasoning that both bits are set so "something happens". The cut count
   asks whether the endpoints *differ*, not whether they are $1$, and
   $|11\rangle$ leaves the edge entirely inside one group.

   </details>

4. Why is $B=\sum_iX_i$ (independent single-qubit rotations) a reasonable
   choice of mixer, given that the goal is to explore *different*
   colorings rather than stay fixed at one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $X$ is precisely the operator that flips a bit, so $e^{-i\beta X_i}$
   partially flips vertex $i$'s colour: it moves amplitude between
   $|{\ldots}0_i{\ldots}\rangle$ and $|{\ldots}1_i{\ldots}\rangle$ in a
   controlled amount set by $\beta$. Summed over all vertices, $B$
   generates moves between every colouring and each of its
   single-vertex-flip neighbours, which is exactly the local-search
   neighbourhood a classical heuristic for Max-Cut would use.

   Three properties make it a good choice rather than merely a workable
   one. It is off-diagonal, so unlike $U_C$ it genuinely redistributes
   probability rather than only phases. Its terms act on different qubits
   and therefore commute, so $e^{-i\beta B}$ factorizes into a tensor
   product of single-qubit exponentials with no Trotter error, and
   compiles to one $R_x(2\beta)$ per qubit: depth one, whatever the graph
   looks like. And the uniform superposition QAOA starts from is $B$'s
   own highest eigenstate, which is what makes the standard adiabatic
   story about interpolating from $B$ to $C$ available as motivation.

   The interference is what turns local moves into optimization. $U_C$ has
   already stamped each colouring with a phase proportional to its
   quality, so when the mixer brings neighbouring colourings' amplitudes
   together they add or cancel according to those phases, and good
   colourings accumulate weight. This is the oracle-then-diffusion pattern
   from Grover with a continuous score in place of a single marked item.

   The wrong turn is treating $\beta$ as a dial where more is better. The
   lesson's own sweep shows the expected cut climbing to $1.875$ at
   $\beta=\pi/8$, then falling to $0.375$ at $\beta=3\pi/8$, below the
   $1.5$ baseline, and returning to the baseline at $\pi/2$. It is a
   rotation angle, so overshooting undoes the work, exactly as running
   Grover past its optimum does.

   </details>

## Summary

- Max-Cut's cost Hamiltonian $C=\sum(1-Z_iZ_j)/2$ has eigenvalue equal to
  the cut count, for any coloring.
- The cost unitary is a pure phase operator (verified: leaves every
  probability unchanged); the mixer is what actually moves amplitude.
- The uniform superposition's expected cut size is exactly $|E|/2$ for
  any graph, a checkable baseline before any optimization runs.
