<LessonHook eyebrow="Run, measure, adjust, run again">
VQE and QAOA do not run a fixed circuit once and stop, the way Shor's algorithm does. They run a parameterized circuit again and again, with a classical optimizer steering the parameters between runs.
</LessonHook>

## Run once, or run and steer

Shor's algorithm is a fixed sequence of gates, run once, with classical
post-processing at the end. **<Term id="vqe">VQE (the variational quantum eigensolver)</Term>**
and **QAOA** work differently: a parameterized circuit runs many times,
with a *classical optimizer* adjusting its parameters between runs to
improve a measured quantity.

Handing the steering wheel to an optimizer raises an obvious worry. What
stops it from wandering somewhere meaningless and reporting a number that
looks good? The answer is a theorem, proved below in four lines, which says
the quantity being minimized has a hard floor at the true ground energy. An
optimizer can fail to reach the floor. It cannot fall through it, and a
reported value below it means a bug rather than a discovery.

## The principle, the ansatz, and the loop between them

### The variational principle, proved

Let $H$ be a Hermitian <Term id="hamiltonians-time-evolution">Hamiltonian</Term> with eigenvalues
$E_0\le E_1\le\cdots$ and corresponding orthonormal eigenstates
$|\phi_0\rangle,|\phi_1\rangle,\dots$ ($E_0$ the ground-state energy). Any
normalized state $|\psi\rangle$ can be written in this eigenbasis:
$|\psi\rangle=\sum_ic_i|\phi_i\rangle$, with $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$. Then:

$$
\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle = \sum_i|c_i|^2E_i \geq \sum_i|c_i|^2E_0 = E_0\sum_i|c_i|^2 = E_0
$$

using $E_i\geq E_0$ for every $i$ in the inequality step. So, for $H$ with
a discrete spectrum bounded below,
**$\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\geq E_0$ for every normalized $|\psi\rangle$,
with equality exactly when $|\psi\rangle$ lies in $H$'s ground eigenspace**
(any weight on a state of strictly higher energy pushes the expectation
value up, while weight spread among degenerate ground states costs
nothing). This is the
**variational principle**: the ground energy is a lower bound on every
state's energy expectation value, so minimizing that expectation over any
family of states approaches $E_0$ from above and cannot overshoot below.

### The ansatz and the hybrid loop

An **<Term id="ansatz">ansatz</Term>** is a parameterized circuit $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$. This
platform's single-qubit VQE uses
$|\psi(\theta,\varphi)\rangle=R_z(\varphi)R_y(\theta)|0\rangle$, which
reaches *every* point on the Bloch sphere as $\theta,\varphi$ vary, so it
can represent the exact ground state of *any* single-qubit Hamiltonian,
not merely approximate it. The **hybrid loop**: measure
$\langle\psi(\vec\theta)|H|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ on the quantum device
(via `costFunction`, reusing `expectationValue` directly), feed that
number to a classical optimizer, get updated parameters, repeat.
`runVqe` implements this with pattern search: try a step in each parameter
direction, keep whichever improves the cost, shrink the step when nothing
does. Simple, but a real optimizer rather than a hardcoded answer.

### Why the variational principle matters for an imperfect optimizer

The variational principle guarantees `runVqe`'s output energy is at least
$E_0$, however badly the optimizer performs and however few iterations it
runs. That is a safety property, and this course's tests assert it directly:
after running the optimizer on $H=0.3Z+0.9X$ they require
`result.energy >= exact - 1e-6`.

The asymmetry is worth stating plainly. The optimizer can fail to *reach*
$E_0$, and often does. It cannot report a value *below* $E_0$, so a number
under the floor is evidence of a bug in the ansatz, the cost function, or
the optimizer, and never of a better answer.

## Built for a different hardware regime

VQE and QAOA are built for a different regime than Shor's algorithm. They
accept imperfect answers, an approximate ground energy or a near-optimal
cut, and buy much shallower circuits with that concession. Shallow circuits
are what present-day hardware can actually finish before decoherence sets
in, which is why this family is the leading candidate for near-term
("<Term id="nisq">NISQ</Term>", covered in the capstone) devices that cannot yet run Shor's deep
circuits reliably.

## Checking the bound on one explicit state

**For $H=Z$ (eigenvalues $+1,-1$), confirm the variational principle holds
for the ansatz state at $\theta=\pi/2,\varphi=0$ (i.e. $|+\rangle$).**

$Z|+\rangle=|-\rangle$, and
$\langle+|Z|+\rangle=\langle+|-\rangle=0$ (an even, $50/50$ mix of the two
eigenvalues under this particular state). $E_0=-1$ for $H=Z$, and indeed
$0\geq-1$: the variational principle holds, though this particular
$\theta,\varphi$ is far from optimal (the true minimizer is
$\theta=\pi,\varphi=0$, giving $|1\rangle$, energy exactly $-1$). Placed
on the actual energy spectrum, the inequality is a picture, not just an
arithmetic check: the trial value has nowhere to go but at or above the
floor $E_0$ sets. That isn't special to $\theta=\pi/2$.

## A lower reading is not automatically better

<Callout type="warning">
**Believing a lower measured energy is always more trustworthy.** The
variational principle says the *true* minimum is a hard floor. If an
implementation ever reports an energy below the Hamiltonian's actual
smallest eigenvalue, that is evidence of a bug rather than a discovery.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any ansatz can represent any Hamiltonian's ground state.**
This platform's specific $R_z R_y$ single-qubit ansatz can (it spans the
whole Bloch sphere), but a poorly chosen or insufficiently expressive
ansatz for a larger system can systematically fail to reach $E_0$ no
matter how well the classical optimizer runs, a real and active
limitation of VQE in practice.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Prove the variational principle's equality case: show that if
   $|\psi\rangle=|\phi_0\rangle$ exactly, $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle=E_0$.
   Then say why the *converse* (equality forces $|\psi\rangle=|\phi_0\rangle$)
   needs the extra hypothesis that $E_0$ is non-degenerate, with an
   example where it fails.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Expand in the eigenbasis. If $|\psi\rangle=|\phi_0\rangle$ then
   $c_0=1$ and every other $c_i=0$, so

   $$
   \langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle = \sum_i|c_i|^2E_i = 1\cdot E_0 + 0 = E_0 .
   $$

   Equivalently and more directly, $H|\phi_0\rangle=E_0|\phi_0\rangle$ by
   definition of an eigenstate, so
   $\langle\phi_0|H|\phi_0\rangle=E_0\langle\phi_0|\phi_0\rangle=E_0$ using
   normalization. Either route is two lines.

   The converse needs non-degeneracy because the inequality step in the
   proof, $\sum_i|c_i|^2E_i \geq \sum_i|c_i|^2E_0$, is tight for any term
   with $E_i = E_0$, not only for $i=0$. Weight sitting on a *different*
   eigenstate that happens to share the ground energy costs nothing, so
   equality no longer pins down which state you are in.

   A concrete failure: take $H=-I$ on a single qubit, whose eigenvalues
   are $-1$ and $-1$. Every normalized $|\psi\rangle$ gives
   $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle=-1=E_0$, so equality holds universally while
   $|\psi\rangle$ is completely unconstrained. The correct general
   statement, which the lesson gives, is that equality holds exactly when
   $|\psi\rangle$ lies in $H$'s ground *eigenspace*, and that eigenspace is
   one-dimensional only when $E_0$ is non-degenerate.

   This matters for VQE rather than being a pedantic footnote. An
   optimizer that reaches the floor has found *a* ground state, and with
   degeneracy there is no guarantee about which one, which is exactly the
   situation in molecular problems with symmetry. The energy is still
   right; any property computed from the returned state may not be.

   The wrong turn is proving only the direction the question opens with
   and treating the biconditional as obvious. The forward direction is the
   easy half and says nothing about whether a low energy certifies a good
   state, which is the half an optimizer's user actually needs.

   </details>

2. For $H=-Z$ (eigenvalues $-1,+1$, so $E_0=-1$ still, but now belonging
   to $|0\rangle$ rather than $|1\rangle$), which $(\theta,\varphi)$
   minimizes the ansatz energy?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $(\theta,\varphi)=(0,0)$, giving the ansatz state $|0\rangle$ and energy
   exactly $-1$. Any $\varphi$ works, since $\theta=0$ makes
   $R_y(0)|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $R_z(\varphi)$ then contributes only a
   global phase, so $\varphi$ is unconstrained.

   Work it out from the matrix. $Z=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, so
   $-Z=\mathrm{diag}(-1,1)$. Applying it to $|0\rangle$ gives
   $-|0\rangle$, eigenvalue $-1$; applying it to $|1\rangle$ gives
   $+|1\rangle$, eigenvalue $+1$. So the ground state of $-Z$ is
   $|0\rangle$. `costFunction` confirms it: at $(\theta,\varphi)=(0,0)$ the
   energy is $-1.000000$, and at $(\pi,0)$, the state $|1\rangle$, it is
   $+1.000000$. `exactGroundStateEnergy(-Z)` returns $-1$.

   The contrast with the lesson's worked example is the whole point of the
   question. For $H=Z$ the minimizer is $\theta=\pi$, the state
   $|1\rangle$, and flipping the Hamiltonian's sign flips which pole is
   the ground state without changing $E_0$ at all. In Bloch terms,
   $\langle\psi|(\pm Z)|\psi\rangle=\pm\cos\theta$, minimized at
   $\theta=\pi$ for $+Z$ and at $\theta=0$ for $-Z$: the energy landscape
   is reflected, not shifted.

   The wrong turn is assuming the ground state is always $|1\rangle$
   because it was for $H=Z$ in the lesson. The eigenvalue $-1$ is a
   property of the Hamiltonian; which state carries it is a separate
   question that has to be read off the matrix each time. Checking is one
   line, $H|0\rangle$ versus $H|1\rangle$, and it is worth doing before
   trusting any intuition about "the lower state".

   </details>

3. Explain, using the eigenbasis expansion in the derivation, why a state
   with *any* nonzero overlap with an excited eigenstate has strictly
   higher energy than $E_0$ (assuming no degeneracy at $E_0$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Start from the expansion and subtract the floor term by term:

   $$
   \langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle - E_0 = \sum_i|c_i|^2E_i - E_0\sum_i|c_i|^2 = \sum_i|c_i|^2\,(E_i-E_0)
   $$

   using $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$ to rewrite $E_0$ as a weighted sum. Every term
   in that final sum is non-negative, since $|c_i|^2\geq0$ and
   $E_i\geq E_0$. So the whole thing is zero only if every term is zero,
   which requires $|c_j|^2(E_j-E_0)=0$ for each $j$. With no degeneracy,
   $E_j-E_0>0$ strictly for every $j\neq0$, so each such term forces
   $c_j=0$. A single nonzero $c_j$ with $j\neq0$ contributes
   $|c_j|^2(E_j-E_0)>0$ and the total is strictly positive.

   The quantitative version is more useful than the yes-or-no one. The
   excess energy above the floor is exactly
   $\sum_{i\neq0}|c_i|^2(E_i-E_0)$: a weighted average of the excitation
   gaps, weighted by how much of the state sits in each excited level. So
   a VQE run reporting an energy $10^{-3}$ above $E_0$ is telling you the
   state has at most $10^{-3}/\Delta$ of its probability outside the ground
   state, where $\Delta$ is the first gap. Energy error bounds state error,
   and the gap is the conversion factor.

   That relationship is also why VQE is more forgiving than it looks.
   Energy is *quadratic* in the amplitude error near the minimum: an
   overlap error of $\varepsilon$ in amplitude gives an energy error of
   order $\varepsilon^2$, so a state that is $99\%$ correct reports an
   energy far closer than $1\%$ off. The flip side is the one to worry
   about: a good energy is weak evidence of a good state.

   The wrong turn is arguing from a single example, say $|+\rangle$ for
   $H=Z$ giving $0>-1$, and calling it done. That shows the inequality
   holds once; the sum above shows why it cannot fail, and identifies
   degeneracy as the exact circumstance under which the strictness is lost.

   </details>

4. Why does this platform's ansatz needing only 2 real parameters
   ($\theta,\varphi$) to span every single-qubit state not generalize
   automatically to larger systems? (Hint: how many real parameters does a
   general $n$-qubit pure state need?)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the parameter count explodes. An $n$-qubit pure state has $2^n$
   complex amplitudes, so $2^{n+1}$ real numbers, minus one for
   normalization and one for global phase: $2^{n+1}-2$ real parameters. At
   $n=1$ that is $2$, which is why $(\theta,\varphi)$ suffices and the
   Bloch sphere is the whole state space. At $n=10$ it is $2046$; at
   $n=50$ it is about $2\times10^{15}$.

   A fully expressive ansatz would therefore need at least that many
   parameters, and a circuit with exponentially many gates to carry them.
   That is not a practical circuit, and it would also hand the classical
   optimizer an exponentially large search space, so the quantum speedup
   would be spent on the outer loop instead.

   Real ansätze give up expressivity deliberately. A hardware-efficient
   ansatz uses a fixed number of layers of single-qubit rotations and
   entangling gates, with parameter count polynomial in $n$, and covers a
   tiny sliver of state space chosen to be reachable on the device. A
   chemistry-motivated ansatz such as UCCSD instead restricts to states
   with the right particle number and symmetry. Both are bets that the
   true ground state lies near the sliver, and neither can promise it
   does. So the guarantee changes character: the variational principle
   still says the reported energy is at least $E_0$, but it no longer says
   the minimum over the ansatz family is anywhere near $E_0$.

   There is a second, less obvious cost worth naming. Many expressive
   randomly-initialized ansätze suffer *barren plateaus*, where the cost
   landscape's gradients vanish exponentially in $n$, so the optimizer has
   nothing to follow. Expressivity and trainability pull against each
   other, and choosing an ansatz is choosing where to sit between them.

   The wrong turn is reading the single-qubit case as a proof of concept
   that scales. It is a proof that the machinery is correct, verified end
   to end, and it is silent on the one question that decides whether VQE
   is useful: whether a polynomially-sized ansatz can reach a chemically
   accurate state for a molecule anyone cares about.

   </details>

## Summary

- The variational principle, $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle\geq E_0$ for any
  normalized $|\psi\rangle$, follows directly from expanding $|\psi\rangle$
  in $H$'s eigenbasis.
- An ansatz circuit parameterizes a family of trial states; the hybrid
  loop alternates quantum expectation-value measurement with classical
  parameter updates.
- This platform's single-qubit ansatz is exactly expressive enough to
  reach any single-qubit ground state, verified directly instead of merely
  argued.
- The variational principle guarantees the optimizer's output energy is
  never below the true ground energy, a genuine safety check built into
  this course's own tests.
