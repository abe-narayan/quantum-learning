Can two people agree on a secret string of bits over a channel anyone might be listening to, in a way that makes listening detectable? BB84 says yes, and the reason is the no-cloning theorem.

## What no-cloning forbids an eavesdropper

The last lesson proved that an unknown qubit state cannot be copied, which
reads like a mathematical curiosity until you notice what it forbids an
eavesdropper from doing. Eve cannot make a quiet copy of each qubit, keep
one, and pass the other along undisturbed. She has to interact with the
only copy there is.

BB84, the first <Term id="qkd-bb84">quantum key distribution</Term> protocol, turns that into a
security guarantee you can put a number on. Every primitive it needs
(encode a bit in a basis, change basis with $H$, measure) is already in
this course's engine, so the whole protocol can be computed here rather
than described.

## Two conjugate bases, and no quiet copy

A classical bit sent in the open can always be copied and read without
leaving a trace. A qubit is different: it can be encoded in one of two
*conjugate* bases, the <Term id="computational-basis">computational (Z) basis</Term> $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ or
the Hadamard (X) basis $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$, and if you don't know which
basis was used, measuring in the wrong one destroys the information you were
trying to read while giving you a coin flip instead. Alice exploits this:
she picks a random bit and a random basis for every qubit she sends. Bob
picks his own random basis to measure in. Neither of them knows in advance
whether their bases will match, but afterward they can compare *which
basis* they each used, in public, for every qubit, without ever saying what
the bit values were. Wherever the bases matched, Bob's measurement recovered
Alice's bit exactly; wherever they didn't, the result is discarded. What's
left is a shared, secret string: the key.

## Encode, measure, sift, and count the errors

### Encoding a bit in a basis

Encoding bit $b$ in the Z basis is just the basis state itself. Encoding it
in the X basis means applying $H$ first:

$$
\text{Z-basis encode}(b) = |b\rangle \qquad \text{X-basis encode}(b) = H|b\rangle = \begin{cases}|+\rangle & b=0\\|-\rangle & b=1\end{cases}
$$

### Measuring in a basis

Measuring in the Z basis is an ordinary computational-basis measurement:
read off `state.probabilities()` directly. Measuring in the X basis means
first applying $H$ (which sends $|+\rangle\to|0\rangle$ and
$|-\rangle\to|1\rangle$, undoing the encoding step above) and *then* reading
off computational-basis probabilities, the same basis-change trick every
earlier lesson in this course has used.

### What happens when Bob's basis matches Alice's

Alice encodes bit $1$; suppose Bob happens to measure in the *same* basis
she encoded in. In the Z/Z case, Bob measures $|1\rangle$ directly:
certainty. In the X/X case, Bob applies $H$ to $|-\rangle$, getting back
$|1\rangle$ exactly, so again certainty. Matching bases always reproduce
Alice's bit with probability 1, for any bit value, since the basis-change
gate is applied and then exactly undone.

### What happens when Bob's basis doesn't match

Now suppose Bob measures in the *other* basis. Z-encoded $|1\rangle$
measured in the X basis: apply $H$, getting $H|1\rangle=|-\rangle$, whose
computational-basis probabilities are exactly $50/50$. X-encoded
$|-\rangle$ measured directly in the Z basis is already $50/50$ by
inspection. Either mismatch throws away all information about the original
bit: Bob's outcome is a fair coin flip, uncorrelated with what Alice sent.

### Sifting

After every qubit has been sent and measured, Alice and Bob publicly
announce *only their basis choices* for each one, never the bit values.
Since the basis each of them picked was independent of the bit encoded,
revealing it leaks nothing about the key. They keep exactly the positions
where their bases agree (on average, half of them) and discard the rest.
What remains, the **sifted key**, is identical on both sides with
certainty, by the matching-basis case derived above, and unknown to anyone
who wasn't measuring the actual qubits.

### Eve, and why intercepting is not free

Suppose an eavesdropper Eve intercepts each qubit in transit, measures it
in a randomly guessed basis, and resends whatever she <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapsed</Term> it to,
hoping neither Alice nor Bob notices. <Term id="no-cloning-theorem">No-cloning</Term> is exactly why she can't
do better: she cannot duplicate the qubit and forward an untouched original
while keeping a copy for herself, so she is forced to measure the real
thing and pass along only what her own measurement leaves behind.

Concretely: Alice sends bit $1$ in the Z basis. Eve guesses the X basis:

Eve's own outcome is $50/50$ (the mismatch case derived above), so half the
time she collapses onto $|+\rangle$. Not knowing she guessed wrong, she
resends exactly that: a fresh $|+\rangle$, prepared in the X basis.

<QuantumStateDisplay state={eveResendState} label="Eve resends |+⟩" showProbabilities={false} />

Bob, using the basis he and Alice will later discover *does* match Alice's
original Z basis, measures the qubit Eve actually sent him:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={eveResendState} label={`Bob measures Eve's resent qubit in Z: P(0)=${bobAfterEveProbabilities[0].toFixed(2)}, P(1)=${bobAfterEveProbabilities[1].toFixed(2)}`} />

Alice's and Bob's bases agree here, an event they would normally treat as
reliable, and yet Bob has only a $50\%$ chance of reading back Alice's
original bit. That is the whole mechanism. Eve's wrong-basis guess forces
her to resend a state in the wrong basis relative to the one Alice and Bob
agreed on, and measuring a wrong-basis state is the $50/50$ case derived
above.

### The resulting error rate

Combine two independent probabilities: Eve guesses the wrong basis with
probability $1/2$; conditioned on that, Bob's measurement (in the basis
Alice actually used) disagrees with Alice's bit with probability $1/2$, as
just computed. So on any given sifted-key bit (one where Alice's and Bob's
bases matched), the probability Eve's interference introduced an error is

$$
P(\text{error on a sifted bit}) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 = 25\%
$$

Alice and Bob don't need to inspect every bit to catch this: they sacrifice
a random public sample of their sifted key, compare it bit by bit, and
count disagreements. An honest channel gives $0\%$ mismatches (the
matching-basis case is exact, probability 1); a full intercept-resend
attack gives a $25\%$ mismatch rate on the sample, unmistakably above
noise. They discard the sample and, if the error rate is too high, discard
the whole key and try again over a cleaner channel.

The basis-combination sweep above is where this $25\%$ comes from. Those
bars are `HADAMARD` and `.probabilities()` computing Bob's outcome
distribution for all four basis combinations, not an illustration of it.
Revisit it with the derivation in mind: the mismatch bars (Alice Z/Bob X
and Alice X/Bob Z) are the $50/50$ distribution Eve inflicts on a sifted
bit whenever she guesses her interception basis wrong.

## No entanglement anywhere in this protocol

Nothing about BB84 requires entanglement or anything exotic. Every qubit
Alice sends is in a definite, unentangled state, and the security comes
from one fact: the $Z$ and $X$ bases are *conjugate*, non-commuting
observables, in the same sense position and momentum are. Measuring one
disturbs the other, which is the physics the <Term id="heisenberg-uncertainty-principle">uncertainty principle</Term>
expresses elsewhere in this curriculum.

No-cloning closes the one loophole that would otherwise defeat all of it.
Without it, Eve could copy each qubit as many times as she liked, measure
the copies in every basis, reconstruct the state, and never touch the qubit
Alice and Bob use. Because copying is impossible, she has to gamble on a
basis for the one real qubit in transit, and gambling wrong is what makes
her visible.

## BB84 needs no entangled pairs

<Callout type="warning">
**"BB84 requires entanglement."** It doesn't. Every qubit Alice sends is a
single, unentangled state in one of four fixed preparations
($|0\rangle,|1\rangle,|+\rangle,|-\rangle$). Entanglement-based variants of
QKD exist, but BB84 itself is a prepare-and-measure protocol.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Revealing which basis was used leaks the bit."** It doesn't. The basis
Alice or Bob picked was chosen independently of the bit value (a separate
random coin flip), so announcing "I used Z" or "I used X" carries no
information about whether the underlying bit was $0$ or $1$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Eve can just copy the qubit, keep one, and resend the other
untouched."** This is exactly what the no-cloning theorem forbids. Eve has
no way to duplicate an unknown state; she must measure the one real qubit
in transit, and measuring in the wrong basis is what introduces the
detectable disturbance.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Alice encodes bit $0$ in the Z basis. Using the same method as the
   single-bit walkthrough above, compute Bob's measurement probabilities
   for each of his two possible bases, and identify which one is the
   matching case.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Alice's state is $\text{Z-basis encode}(0) = |0\rangle$.

   Bob measuring in Z reads the computational-basis probabilities directly:
   $[1, 0]$. He gets $0$ with certainty, so Z is the matching case.

   Bob measuring in X applies $H$ first, giving $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$,
   whose computational-basis probabilities are $[\tfrac12, \tfrac12]$. A
   fair coin, carrying nothing about Alice's bit. The engine returns
   exactly these two distributions.

   The structure is the same on every one of the four preparations, and it
   is worth stating in the general form. Matching bases are certain because
   Bob's basis change is literally the inverse of Alice's encoding gate:
   $H$ applied and then $H$ applied again is $H^2 = I$, so nothing happened
   at all. Mismatched bases are exactly $50/50$ because $|\langle 0|+\rangle|^2 = |\langle 1|+\rangle|^2 = \tfrac12$,
   which is what makes Z and X *conjugate* bases: every state of one is
   maximally undecided in the other. Neither result is a near-miss that
   better hardware could improve.

   The wrong turn is reporting the mismatched case as "Bob usually gets 0,
   since Alice sent 0". There is no residual bias. All of the information
   is gone, not most of it, and that total loss is what the protocol is
   built on: it means announcing bases in public is safe, and it means Eve
   in the wrong basis learns exactly nothing.

   </details>

2. Suppose Eve always guesses the Z basis (never randomizes). For a key
   where Alice picks her own basis uniformly at random, what fraction of
   *all* qubits does Eve guess correctly? Does this change the $25\%$
   sifted-key error rate derived above? Then say *where in the key* the
   errors now sit, and how Alice and Bob could use that pattern to tell
   this Eve from the randomizing one.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Eve guesses correctly on exactly half of all qubits: her basis is always
   Z, Alice's is Z half the time, so they match with probability
   $\tfrac12$, the same fraction a randomizing Eve gets.

   The sifted-key error rate is also unchanged, at $25\%$, but it is now
   built out of two very different halves. Split the sifted key by which
   basis Alice used.

   On Z positions, Eve measures in the basis Alice actually prepared in, so
   she reads the bit without disturbing the state and resends an identical
   qubit. Bob, also in Z, recovers Alice's bit with certainty. Error rate
   $0\%$, and Eve knows those bits perfectly.

   On X positions, Alice sent $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$ and Eve measures
   in Z, collapsing to $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ at $50/50$ and resending
   that. Bob applies $H$ and gets $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$ or
   $H|1\rangle = |-\rangle$, either of which is $50/50$ in his readout, so
   he disagrees with Alice half the time. Error rate $50\%$, and Eve learns
   nothing about those bits.

   Alice's basis is uniform, and sifting does not bias it, so the sifted
   key is half of each:

   $$
   \tfrac12 \times 0 + \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14 = 25\%.
   $$

   Identical to the randomizing attack, engine confirmed. Eve's information
   is identical too: half the sifted bits known exactly and half unknown,
   which is $\tfrac12$ a bit per sifted bit either way. So this strategy
   buys her nothing.

   What it costs her is stealth, and that is the part the averaged number
   hides. The errors are no longer scattered uniformly; they are perfectly
   sorted by Alice's basis. If Alice and Bob bin their public sample by the
   basis they announced anyway and count separately, a randomizing Eve
   shows roughly $25\%$ in both bins, while this Eve shows about $0\%$ in
   the Z bin and about $50\%$ in the X bin. Same headline number,
   completely different fingerprint, and the bins cost nothing to compute
   since the bases are already public.

   The wrong turn is concluding that committing to one basis must be
   better, because "at least she is right half the time for sure" instead
   of gambling. The gamble was never the problem. The quantity that
   matters is the probability her measurement leaves the qubit in a state
   Bob will misread, and averaging $0\%$ and $50\%$ lands in exactly the
   same place as $25\%$ everywhere. Removing the randomness only removed
   the camouflage.

   </details>

3. Alice and Bob sacrifice a random sample of $m$ bits from their sifted
   key and find zero disagreements. If Eve had in fact been running the
   full intercept-resend attack, what is the probability they would see
   that? How large must $m$ be to push it below one in a million, and why
   does the sample have to be chosen at random and then discarded?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Each sampled bit independently disagrees with probability $\tfrac14$
   under the attack, so it agrees with probability $\tfrac34$, and a clean
   sample of $m$ bits has probability

   $$
   \left(\tfrac34\right)^{m}.
   $$

   That falls off geometrically: about $5.6\%$ at $m = 10$, $3.2\times10^{-3}$
   at $m = 20$, $1.0\times10^{-5}$ at $m = 40$. Solving
   $(3/4)^m < 10^{-6}$ gives $m > \log(10^{-6})/\log(3/4) \approx 48.02$, so
   $m = 49$ suffices, where the value is $7.6\times10^{-7}$. Forty-eight
   bits is just short, at $1.007\times10^{-6}$.

   Fifty sacrificed bits is nothing. That is the practical point: detection
   confidence grows exponentially in the sample size while the cost grows
   linearly, so a key of ten thousand sifted bits can buy near-certainty
   against a full intercept-resend attack for half a percent of its length.

   The sample has to be random because Eve chooses which qubits to attack.
   If Alice and Bob always compared, say, the first hundred sifted
   positions, Eve would pass those through untouched and attack the rest,
   and the test would certify a key she holds half of. Randomizing the
   sample after the fact means any attack on a fraction $f$ of the qubits
   is caught with probability $1 - (1 - f/4)^m$, and Eve has no way to
   arrange to be in the unsampled part.

   The sample has to be discarded because comparing it made it public.
   Announcing those bit values over the classical channel hands them to
   anyone listening, so they can no longer be key. Keeping them would be
   the one flaw large enough to defeat everything else in the protocol.

   Two wrong turns are worth naming. The first is treating a clean sample
   as proof of no eavesdropping. It is a bound on the probability of having
   been fooled, not a certificate, which is why the size of $m$ has to be
   chosen against the confidence you want. The second is expecting an
   honest channel to give exactly $0\%$. Real hardware has loss and noise,
   so practical QKD sets a threshold error rate and runs privacy
   amplification below it, rather than aborting on a single disagreement.
   The derivation here is the idealized case that sets where that threshold
   can sit.

   </details>

4. Eve tries a third strategy. Instead of choosing Z or X, she measures
   every qubit in the *Breidbart basis*, the one exactly halfway between
   them, spanned by $\cos\frac{\pi}{8}|0\rangle+\sin\frac{\pi}{8}|1\rangle$
   and $-\sin\frac{\pi}{8}|0\rangle+\cos\frac{\pi}{8}|1\rangle$, and
   resends whichever of those two she collapsed onto. Find the error rate
   she causes on the sifted key and the probability her own outcome
   matches Alice's bit, and compare both with the random-basis attack.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The error rate is $25\%$, exactly the same as the random-basis attack,
   but Eve's own guess is right $\cos^2\frac{\pi}{8} \approx 85.4\%$ of the
   time instead of $75\%$. Same disturbance, better per-bit accuracy, and
   the last paragraph of this answer explains why "better" is not the same
   as "more information".

   Take Alice sending bit $0$ in the Z basis, so $|0\rangle$, with Bob in
   the matching Z basis. Write $b_0$ and $b_1$ for Eve's two basis states.
   Her outcome probabilities are the overlaps
   $|\langle b_0|0\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\pi}{8} \approx 0.8536$ and
   $|\langle b_1|0\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\frac{\pi}{8} \approx 0.1464$.
   Whichever she gets, she resends that state, and Bob then measures it in
   Z. From $b_0$ he misreads with probability
   $\sin^2\frac{\pi}{8}$; from $b_1$ he misreads with probability
   $\cos^2\frac{\pi}{8}$. So

   $$
   P(\text{error}) = 2\cos^2\tfrac{\pi}{8}\sin^2\tfrac{\pi}{8} = \tfrac12\sin^2\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac14 .
   $$

   The engine runs this for all four of Alice's preparations and returns
   $0.25$ every time, along with $0.8536$ for Eve's guess. The symmetry is
   the reason: the Breidbart basis sits at the same angle from Z as from X,
   so it treats all four preparations identically, which is also why Eve
   has no basis choice left to get wrong.

   Compare the random-basis attack. There Eve is right with certainty on
   the half where she guessed Alice's basis and at chance on the other
   half, so $\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = 75\%$. The
   headline lesson is that the $25\%$ figure is not the signature of one
   naive attack. Two quite different intercept-resend strategies pay
   exactly the same price, and Eve cannot buy her way under it by being
   cleverer about her basis.

   The wrong turn is expecting the halfway basis to be a compromise that
   halves the damage, on the reasoning that it is "half right" on every
   qubit rather than fully wrong on half of them. Disturbance does not
   average that way. What decides it is the overlap between Bob's basis and
   whatever Eve resent, and Eve's resent state is always tilted $\pi/8$
   away from what Alice prepared, which costs the same $25\%$ in
   expectation as being at $\pi/2$ half the time.

   One further subtlety is worth the extra minute, because $85.4\%$ against
   $75\%$ makes the Breidbart attack look like a free upgrade and it is
   not. Higher guess accuracy is not the same as more information. Eve's
   knowledge of a sifted bit is a binary symmetric channel, and its
   capacity is $1 - H(p)$ where $H$ is the binary entropy and $p$ the error
   rate. Breidbart gives $1 - H(0.1464) = 1 - 0.601 = 0.399$ bits per
   sifted bit. The random-basis attack gives certainty on half the bits and
   nothing on the other half, which is $\tfrac12 \times 1 + \tfrac12 \times 0 = 0.5$
   bits. So the strategy with the better hit rate carries *less* usable
   information, because a uniform small edge on every bit is worth less
   than perfect knowledge of half of them. Which strategy Eve should prefer
   depends on what she plans to do next, and that is a real question in
   QKD security proofs rather than a quibble.

   </details>

## Summary

- Alice encodes each random bit in a randomly chosen basis (Z or X); Bob
  measures each qubit in his own randomly chosen basis.
- Matching bases recover Alice's bit with certainty (probability 1);
  mismatched bases give a uniformly random result (probability $1/2$ each
  way), both computed directly from `HADAMARD` and `.probabilities()`.
- Sifting (publicly comparing bases, never bits) keeps only the
  matching-basis positions, producing an identical shared key on both
  sides.
- An intercept-resend eavesdropper, unable to clone the qubit (no-cloning),
  must guess a basis; guessing wrong introduces a $25\%$ error rate on the
  sifted key, detectable by sacrificing and comparing a public sample.
