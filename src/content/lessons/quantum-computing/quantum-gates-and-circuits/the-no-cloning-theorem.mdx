## Motivation

Copying data is the most ordinary operation in classical computing — you
do it constantly, without thinking about it. It's worth asking directly:
can a quantum computer copy an unknown qubit's state onto a second,
blank qubit? The answer is a flat no, and the proof is short enough to
walk through completely, using nothing more than linearity — a property
every quantum gate has been guaranteed to have since the moment you
learned gates are matrices.

## Intuition

A "cloning machine" would need to be a single, fixed device that works
for *any* input state, since the whole point is you don't know the state
in advance (if you already knew it, you could just prepare a second copy
yourself with no machine needed). But a fixed quantum device is a fixed
unitary matrix — and matrices are linear. Linear operations are
extremely constrained: what they do to a superposition is *completely
determined* by what they do to the pieces of that superposition
separately. That single fact is enough to break cloning.

## Mathematical Development

### Setting up the claim

Suppose a cloning unitary $U$ existed: for *any* single-qubit state
$|\psi\rangle$, acting on $|\psi\rangle$ together with a fixed "blank"
qubit $|0\rangle$ produces two copies:

$$
U(|\psi\rangle\otimes|0\rangle) = |\psi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle \qquad \text{for every } |\psi\rangle
$$

This has to hold for *every* single-qubit state, since $U$ is one fixed
device with no way to know in advance which $|\psi\rangle$ it's been
handed.

### Testing it on two specific inputs

Apply the assumption to $|0\rangle$ and to $|1\rangle$ individually:

$$
U(|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle) = |0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle, \qquad U(|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle) = |1\rangle\otimes|1\rangle
$$

Nothing wrong yet — a device that measures the input in the computational
basis and prepares a matching copy could achieve exactly this much.

### The contradiction: apply it to a superposition

Now apply the *same* assumption to $|+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$.
If cloning works for every state, it must work for this one too, giving
$U(|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)=|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$. But $U$ is
linear, and $|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\otimes|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|10\rangle)$,
so linearity forces:

$$
U(|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(U(|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)+U(|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)\Big) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)
$$

That's the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$ from two lessons ago. Compare it to
what cloning was supposed to produce:

$$
|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)
$$

These are not the same state — $|\Phi^+\rangle$ has zero amplitude on
$|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$; $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ doesn't.
**Contradiction.** A $U$ that correctly clones $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$
individually is *forced by linearity* to fail at cloning $|+\rangle$.
Since $U$ was assumed to work for every state, no such $U$ can exist.

## Physical Interpretation

This isn't a technological limitation waiting on better engineering — the
proof only used linearity, a property that holds for *every* possible
quantum gate, so no future device of any kind can evade it. No-cloning is
also not an arbitrary restriction bolted onto the theory: if cloning were
possible, you could make unlimited copies of one unknown qubit and
measure each copy in a different basis, reconstructing the full state
exactly — directly violating the measurement-disturbs-the-state structure
this course has built lesson by lesson. Worse, a device that clones one
half of an entangled pair many times over would let its holder detect,
instantaneously, which basis someone light-years away chose to measure
their half in — a faster-than-light signal. No-cloning isn't a
restriction on quantum mechanics; it's a requirement for quantum
mechanics to be a consistent theory at all.

## Worked Example

**Someone claims their device correctly clones $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$,
*and* $|+\rangle$. Show this is still impossible using the same method.**

If the device clones all three, it must also (by the same linearity
argument, since $|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$ is
another superposition of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$) satisfy:

$$
U(|-\rangle\otimes|0\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big(U(|0\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)-U(|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)\Big) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|11\rangle) = |\Phi^-\rangle
$$

But correct cloning of $|-\rangle$ requires
$U(|-\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)=|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle=\frac12(|00\rangle-|01\rangle-|10\rangle+|11\rangle)$
— again not equal to $|\Phi^-\rangle$. Cloning $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$,
and $|+\rangle$ correctly already forces incorrect behavior on
$|-\rangle$; you can't patch the theorem by cloning "just a few more"
states without breaking somewhere else.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**"CNOT copies bits, so cloning must be possible."** $\text{CNOT}(|b\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)=|b\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$
for $b=0$ or $1$ individually — but apply linearity to a superposition
input, exactly as the proof above does, and CNOT produces the Bell state,
not two copies of the superposition. CNOT copies *known classical
basis states*; it does not clone unknown quantum states.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking no-cloning forbids preparing many copies of a *known* state.**
If you know $|\psi\rangle$ in advance, you can prepare as many copies as
you like using ordinary gates — the theorem only forbids a single fixed
device correctly copying an *arbitrary, unknown* input.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

2. A device is claimed to clone $|0\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ correctly.
   Using the same method as the worked example, find the state it must
   produce for $|1\rangle$'s input, and show it can't equal
   $|1\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$.
3. Explain, in a sentence or two, the connection between no-cloning and
   the measurement postulate: why would perfect cloning let you learn an
   unknown state's exact amplitudes, something a single measurement
   cannot do?

## Summary

- No fixed unitary can map $|\psi\rangle\otimes|0\rangle\to|\psi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle$
  for *every* single-qubit $|\psi\rangle$.
- The proof: assume it works for $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, apply
  linearity to $|+\rangle$, and show the required output disagrees with
  actual cloning of $|+\rangle$.
- Copying *known* classical states (via CNOT) is unaffected — the theorem
  is specifically about arbitrary, unknown quantum states.
- No-cloning is required for the theory's consistency, not an incidental
  restriction.
