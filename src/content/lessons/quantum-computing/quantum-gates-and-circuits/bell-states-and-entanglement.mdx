## Motivation

The last lesson ended with a promise: put CNOT's control qubit into
<Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> first, and something new happens. This lesson delivers on
it. The result is the most famous state in quantum computing, and the
question of what makes it different from ordinary correlated randomness
is one every serious treatment of the subject has to answer precisely,
not just gesture at.

## Intuition

Imagine two coins prepared so that they always land the same way, but you
don't know which way until you look: heads-heads or tails-tails, 50/50,
decided the moment they were set up. That's *classical* correlation,
shared but predetermined. A Bell pair looks identical if you only ever
check one measurement, but as this lesson shows with an actual
calculation, it keeps agreeing even when you check in a *different* way
of measuring, which no predetermined classical coin pair can do.

## Mathematical Development

### The derivation

The circuit for this whole derivation is just two columns: $H$ on qubit 0,
then $\text{CNOT}$ with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target.

    { gate: "CNOT", targets: [0, 1] },

/>

Start from $|00\rangle$. Apply $H$ to qubit 0 only:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterH} label="H on qubit 0: (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2" />

Qubit 0 is now in superposition; qubit 1 is untouched. Apply
$\text{CNOT}(0,1)$: the $|00\rangle$ term (control 0) is left alone; the
$|10\rangle$ term (control 1) has its target flipped, $|10\rangle\to|11\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={bellState} label="CNOT(0,1): the Bell state |Φ+⟩" />

$$
|00\rangle \xrightarrow{H \text{ on } q_0} \frac{|00\rangle+|10\rangle}{\sqrt2} \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}(0,1)} \frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} \equiv |\Phi^+\rangle
$$

The two `QuantumStateDisplay` panels above are two fixed snapshots; here
is the same circuit as one steppable run, with the amplitude bars
recomputed live after each gate instead of shown as separate panels:

This is the <Term id="bell-states">Bell state</Term> $|\Phi^+\rangle$, one of four (the other three,
$|\Phi^-\rangle,|\Psi^+\rangle,|\Psi^-\rangle$, come from the same
recipe starting at $|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ instead; you'll
derive one yourself in the practice questions).

### Proving it's entangled

Suppose $|\Phi^+\rangle = |a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ for some
single-qubit $|a\rangle=a_0|0\rangle+a_1|1\rangle$,
$|b\rangle=b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle$. Matching coefficients against
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|00\rangle+0|01\rangle+0|10\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|11\rangle$:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Equate each product-state coefficient a_ib_j to the corresponding Bell-state amplitude.">
$$
a_0b_0=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_0b_1=0, \qquad a_1b_0=0, \qquad a_1b_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="a_0b_1=0 forces a_0=0 or b_1=0 — a product of complex numbers is zero only if one factor is.">
If $a_0=0$: then $a_0b_0=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, contradiction.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Check the other branch of the same either/or.">
If $b_1=0$: then $a_1b_1=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction.
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

No values of
$a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$ satisfy all four equations simultaneously, so
$|\Phi^+\rangle$ is **not** a product state. This is a complete proof, not
an assertion: <Term id="entanglement">entanglement</Term> isn't a label we attach to this state, it's a
provable mathematical fact about it.

### Is this different from classical correlation?

Measuring each qubit of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ in the <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> gives
0 or 1 with 50/50 probability individually, and the two outcomes always
match, exactly what a pair of classically-correlated coins would give
too. So far, no difference. The gap shows up when you measure in a
*different* basis. Apply $H$ to **both** qubits of $|\Phi^+\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterHBoth} label="H on both qubits of |Φ+⟩" />

$$
(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle = \frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} = |\Phi^+\rangle
$$

Applying $H$ to both qubits (which, recall, is exactly how you measure in
the X-basis) reproduces the *same* perfectly-correlated pattern:
$|00\rangle$ or $|11\rangle$ only, still 50/50, still perfectly matching.
A classical hidden-variable model, two coins with some fixed, if hidden,
predetermined outcome for *every* possible way of checking them, cannot
reproduce this. A predetermined pair can be rigged to match in one
specific basis, or shown (with more work than this course covers) to
fail some combination of bases when checked in several at once; a full,
airtight version of this argument is Bell's theorem, covered in the
Quantum Mechanics pillar's "EPR & Bell Inequalities." What you've just
computed is the concrete phenomenon that theorem is built to explain:
correlation that survives a change of measurement basis in a way no
predetermined classical description can match.

**What entanglement is not:** it does not let the two qubit-holders send
information to each other faster than light. Whoever holds qubit 1, on
their own, sees only a random 50/50 sequence no matter what the other
person does to their qubit. The correlation is only visible by later
comparing notes over an ordinary (light-speed-limited) classical channel.

## Physical Interpretation

Entanglement is real, provable structure in the state itself, not a
statement about anyone's ignorance. Two entangled qubits behave as one
system with no independently well-defined parts, and that fact is what
every later lesson in this course (measurement, teleportation, and the
no-cloning theorem) builds on directly.

## Worked Example

**Run the same recipe starting from $|10\rangle$ instead of $|00\rangle$.
Which Bell state comes out?**

$H$ on qubit 0 of $|10\rangle$: recall $H|1\rangle=|-\rangle$, so this
gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|10\rangle)$. Apply
$\text{CNOT}(0,1)$: $|00\rangle\to|00\rangle$ (control 0),
$-|10\rangle\to-|11\rangle$ (control 1, target flips). Result:
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|11\rangle) = |\Phi^-\rangle$, the same
"both qubits match" pattern as $|\Phi^+\rangle$, but with a <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative
phase</Term> of $-1$ between the two terms instead of $+1$. Two of the four
starting points ($|00\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$) give the two
$|00\rangle,|11\rangle$-type Bell states; the other two give the
$|01\rangle,|10\rangle$-type states, one of which is $|\Psi^+\rangle$,
worked out in the practice questions below.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**"Entangled" and "correlated" used as synonyms.** Classical variables can
be correlated; only quantum states can be entangled in the sense proven
above: correlated in every measurement basis simultaneously, not just
one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing entanglement enables faster-than-light signaling.** It
doesn't. Local statistics on either qubit alone are always uniformly
random; only comparing results afterward (at ordinary speed) reveals the
correlation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $H$ on qubit 0 then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$, from $|00\rangle$, produces
  $|\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$.
- $|\Phi^+\rangle$ provably cannot be written as any
  $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, a direct proof by contradiction.
- The correlation survives a change of measurement basis
  ($(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle=|\Phi^+\rangle$), which distinguishes it
  from any classical predetermined (hidden-variable) correlation.
- No signal is transmitted by entanglement alone: each qubit's local
  statistics are always uniformly random.
