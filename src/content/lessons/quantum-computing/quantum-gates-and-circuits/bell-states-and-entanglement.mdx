## Easy to write down, easy to mistake

The last lesson ended with a promise: put CNOT's control qubit into
<Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> first, and something new happens. Two columns of circuit
produce it.

The state that comes out is easy to write down and easy to mistake for
something ordinary. Two coins that always land the same way would give the
same 50/50-and-always-matching statistics. The work of this lesson is
finding the measurement that tells the two situations apart, and it takes
one calculation you can do by hand.

## Two coins glued together

Imagine two coins prepared so that they always land the same way, but you
don't know which way until you look: heads-heads or tails-tails, 50/50,
decided the moment they were set up. That's *classical* correlation:
shared, but predetermined. A Bell pair is indistinguishable from this if
you only ever measure one way. Change the measurement and the two stories
come apart. Working out how they come apart is the calculation below, and
it is short enough to do by hand.

## Deriving it, then proving it cannot factor

### The derivation

The circuit for this whole derivation is just two columns: $H$ on qubit 0,
then $\text{CNOT}$ with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target.

Start from $|00\rangle$. Apply $H$ to qubit 0 only:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterH} label="H on qubit 0: (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2" />

Qubit 0 is now in superposition; qubit 1 is untouched. Apply
$\text{CNOT}(0,1)$: the $|00\rangle$ term (control 0) is left alone; the
$|10\rangle$ term (control 1) has its target flipped, $|10\rangle\to|11\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={bellState} label="CNOT(0,1): the Bell state |Φ+⟩" />

$$
|00\rangle \xrightarrow{H \text{ on } q_0} \frac{|00\rangle+|10\rangle}{\sqrt2} \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}(0,1)} \frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} \equiv |\Phi^+\rangle
$$

is the same circuit as one steppable run, with the amplitude bars

This is the <Term id="bell-states">Bell state</Term> $|\Phi^+\rangle$, one of four (the other three,
$|\Phi^-\rangle,|\Psi^+\rangle,|\Psi^-\rangle$, come from the same
recipe starting at $|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$ instead; you'll
derive one yourself in the practice questions).

### Proving it's entangled

Suppose $|\Phi^+\rangle = |a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ for some
single-qubit $|a\rangle=a_0|0\rangle+a_1|1\rangle$,
$|b\rangle=b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle$. Matching coefficients against
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|00\rangle+0|01\rangle+0|10\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|11\rangle$:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Equate each product-state coefficient a_ib_j to the corresponding Bell-state amplitude.">
$$
a_0b_0=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_0b_1=0, \qquad a_1b_0=0, \qquad a_1b_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="a_0b_1=0 forces a_0=0 or b_1=0, since a product of complex numbers is zero only if one factor is.">
If $a_0=0$: then $a_0b_0=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, contradiction.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Check the other branch of the same either/or.">
If $b_1=0$: then $a_1b_1=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction.
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

No values of
$a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$ satisfy all four equations simultaneously, so
$|\Phi^+\rangle$ is **not** a product state. Notice what the proof rules
out: not one clever factorization you failed to find, but all of them at
once. <Term id="entanglement">Entanglement</Term> is not a label attached to this state; it is a theorem
about it.

### Is this different from classical correlation?

Measuring each qubit of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ in the <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> gives
0 or 1 with 50/50 probability individually, and the two outcomes always
match, which is what a pair of classically-correlated coins would give
too. So far, no difference. The gap shows up when you measure in a
*different* basis. Applying $H$ to **both** qubits, then measuring, is how
you measure both in the X basis, so $(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle$ is the
state whose amplitudes decide the answer. It is a four-term expansion you
can do on paper; commit to a guess first.

Applying $H$ to both qubits (which, recall, is how you measure in
the X-basis) reproduces the *same* perfectly-correlated pattern:
$|00\rangle$ or $|11\rangle$ only, still 50/50, still perfectly matching.
A classical hidden-variable model, two coins carrying a fixed, if hidden,
predetermined outcome for *every* possible way of checking them, can still
reproduce this much, which is why Bell's theorem needs more than two bases.
Perfect agreement in a pair of bases stays within reach of a predetermined
pair; the airtight version of the argument has to compare correlations
across several intermediate measurement angles at once, and it is covered
in the Quantum Mechanics track's "EPR & Bell Inequalities." What you've just
computed is the concrete phenomenon that theorem is built to sharpen into a
proof: correlation that survives a change of measurement basis.

**What entanglement is not:** it does not let the two qubit-holders send
information to each other faster than light. Whoever holds qubit 1, on
their own, sees only a random 50/50 sequence no matter what the other
person does to their qubit. The correlation is only visible by later
comparing notes over an ordinary (light-speed-limited) classical channel.

## Structure in the state, not in your ignorance

Entanglement is structure in the state itself, not a statement about
anyone's ignorance. Two entangled qubits behave as one system with no
independently well-defined parts, and that fact is what the rest of this
course (measurement, teleportation, no-cloning) is built on.

## The same recipe from a different start

**Run the same recipe starting from $|10\rangle$ instead of $|00\rangle$.
Which Bell state comes out?**

$H$ on qubit 0 of $|10\rangle$: recall $H|1\rangle=|-\rangle$, so this
gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|10\rangle)$. Apply
$\text{CNOT}(0,1)$: $|00\rangle\to|00\rangle$ (control 0),
$-|10\rangle\to-|11\rangle$ (control 1, target flips). Result:
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|11\rangle) = |\Phi^-\rangle$, the same
"both qubits match" pattern as $|\Phi^+\rangle$, but with a <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative
phase</Term> of $-1$ between the two terms instead of $+1$. Two of the four
starting points ($|00\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$) give the two
$|00\rangle,|11\rangle$-type Bell states; the other two give the
$|01\rangle,|10\rangle$-type states, one of which is $|\Psi^+\rangle$,
worked out in the practice questions below.

## Correlated and entangled are different words

<Callout type="warning">
**"Entangled" and "correlated" used as synonyms.** Classical variables can
be correlated; only quantum states can be entangled in the sense proven
above: correlated in every measurement basis simultaneously, not just
one.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing entanglement enables faster-than-light signaling.** It
doesn't. Local statistics on either qubit alone are always uniformly
random; only comparing results afterward (at ordinary speed) reveals the
correlation.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Derive $|\Psi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)$ using
   the H-then-CNOT recipe, starting from the correct one of
   $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Start from $|01\rangle$.

   $H$ acts on qubit 0, which is $|0\rangle$, so $H|0\rangle = |+\rangle$
   while qubit 1 stays $|1\rangle$:

   $$
   (H\otimes I)|01\rangle = \frac{|01\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   Then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ term by term. The $|01\rangle$ term has control
   0 and is left alone; the $|11\rangle$ term has control 1, so its target
   flips from 1 to 0:

   $$
   \text{CNOT}(0,1)\frac{|01\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|01\rangle+|10\rangle}{\sqrt2} = |\Psi^+\rangle
   $$

   Two features of the input pick it out, and you can read both off before
   doing any algebra. Qubit 0 must start at $|0\rangle$, because starting
   at $|1\rangle$ makes $H$ produce $|-\rangle$ and leaves a minus sign in
   the answer, which $|\Psi^+\rangle$ does not have. Qubit 1 must start at
   $|1\rangle$, because CNOT flips it in exactly one of the two branches,
   so the branch labels end up disagreeing, which is what "$\Psi$" means as
   against "$\Phi$". Running the recipe on all four inputs gives
   $|\Phi^+\rangle, |\Psi^+\rangle, |\Phi^-\rangle, |\Psi^-\rangle$ in the
   order $|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle$, and the engine
   confirms all four, each one entangled.

   The wrong turn is starting from $|10\rangle$ on the grounds that
   $|\Psi^+\rangle$'s first term is $|01\rangle$ and "the qubits should
   start out disagreeing". Qubit 1 is what the input specifies and CNOT is
   what changes it, so the input's second digit says nothing directly about
   the output's second digit. $|10\rangle$ in fact yields $|\Phi^-\rangle$,
   a state whose two branches agree, which is the exact opposite of the
   guess.

   </details>

2. Prove $|\Psi^+\rangle$ is entangled, using the same coefficient-matching
   contradiction method as the $|\Phi^+\rangle$ proof above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Suppose $|\Psi^+\rangle = |a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$. Matching against
   $0|00\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|01\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|10\rangle + 0|11\rangle$:

   $$
   a_0b_0 = 0, \qquad a_0b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_1b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_1b_1=0
   $$

   From $a_0b_0 = 0$, either $a_0 = 0$ or $b_0 = 0$. If $a_0 = 0$ then
   $a_0b_1 = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, a contradiction. If $b_0 = 0$ then
   $a_1b_0 = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction. Every branch
   fails, so no product state equals $|\Psi^+\rangle$.

   Notice the structure is the $|\Phi^+\rangle$ proof with the two
   diagonals exchanged. There, the zeros sat on $|01\rangle$ and
   $|10\rangle$; here they sit on $|00\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$. The
   argument never cared which diagonal was which, only that two amplitudes
   on one diagonal vanish while both on the other do not. That is the same
   thing the determinant test says in one line:
   $c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10} = 0 - \tfrac12 = -\tfrac12$, nonzero, so
   the amplitude array does not factor as row times column. The engine's
   separability routine returns exactly $\tfrac12$ for the magnitude, with
   entanglement entropy $1$ bit, the maximum.

   The wrong turn here is a proof that stops at "I tried several
   $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ and none worked". Failing to find a factorization
   is not the same as there being none, and the contradiction structure is
   what upgrades a search into a theorem: it starts by *granting* that a
   factorization exists and then derives something false, which rules out
   every candidate at once rather than one at a time.

   </details>

3. What are $P(0)$ and $P(1)$ for measuring *just* qubit 0 of
   $|\Phi^+\rangle$, on its own? (You computed this kind of quantity
   before; reconcile it with the claim that the pair is still perfectly
   correlated.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $P(0) = P(1) = \tfrac12$. Sum $|c_i|^2$ over the terms whose qubit-0
   digit is 0, which is just $|00\rangle$, giving $\tfrac12$; likewise for
   the terms whose qubit-0 digit is 1, which is just $|11\rangle$. The
   engine returns $[0.5, 0.5]$ for qubit 0 and the identical pair for
   qubit 1.

   The reconciliation is that "perfectly correlated" is a statement about
   the *pair*, and $P(0)$ and $P(1)$ for one qubit are deliberately blind
   to the pair. Correlation lives in the joint distribution: $P(00) = P(11) = \tfrac12$
   and $P(01) = P(10) = 0$. Each qubit's own marginal is obtained by
   summing that joint distribution over the other qubit's outcomes, and
   summing is exactly the operation that throws correlation away. Two
   different states can have identical marginals and completely different
   joint behaviour, which is why the marginals cannot possibly show you the
   correlation.

   The sharpest way to say it is with the reduced description of qubit 0
   alone, which the engine computes as $\tfrac12 I$, the maximally mixed
   state. Qubit 0 on its own is not in *some* pure state you have yet to
   determine. It is in no pure state at all, which is what "the pair has no
   independently well-defined parts" means concretely, and it is exactly
   why no local experiment on qubit 0 can reveal anything at all about what
   was done to qubit 1. That is the no-signaling guarantee, in one matrix.

   The wrong turn is reading $\tfrac12$ each as "qubit 0 is secretly in
   $|+\rangle$", since $|+\rangle$ also gives $\tfrac12$ each. It is not:
   $|+\rangle$ would give $P(+) = 1$ in the X basis, while qubit 0 of
   $|\Phi^+\rangle$ gives $\tfrac12$ there too. A maximally mixed qubit is
   $50/50$ in *every* basis, and a superposition is $50/50$ in only one.

   </details>

4. A friend argues that since each qubit alone looks perfectly random,
   entanglement can't be "doing" anything physical. What's the flaw in
   this reasoning?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The flaw is that "look at each qubit alone" is not a complete experiment
   on a two-qubit system, so it cannot be the test of whether anything
   physical is happening. The randomness the friend observes is real, and
   it is not the whole of what there is to observe.

   Make the point with two states that pass the friend's test identically.
   In $|\Phi^+\rangle$ each qubit alone is $50/50$; in $|\Psi^+\rangle$
   each qubit alone is $50/50$; in the product state $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$
   each qubit alone is $50/50$. Three states, indistinguishable by the
   friend's method, wildly different in every joint measurement: the first
   always agrees, the second always disagrees, the third agrees exactly
   half the time. The information is entirely in the correlations, and the
   friend's procedure discards precisely that.

   There is a second layer, and it is the one this lesson was built to
   show. Even the joint measurement in a single basis is not enough,
   because two classically correlated coins reproduce $|\Phi^+\rangle$'s
   computational-basis statistics exactly. What the coins cannot reproduce
   is agreement that *survives changing the basis on both sides at once*,
   which is what $(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle = |\Phi^+\rangle$ says, and
   Bell's theorem turns into a bound no predetermined model can meet.

   The reasoning does contain one true and important thing, worth keeping
   rather than correcting. Local randomness really is complete, in the
   sense that nothing you do to your half changes the statistics on the
   other half by even a little. That is not a limitation of your equipment;
   it is what forbids faster-than-light signaling, and it is the same fact
   as qubit 0's reduced description being $\tfrac12 I$ no matter what
   happens to qubit 1. So the correct conclusion is not "entanglement does
   nothing", but "entanglement does nothing *locally*", which is a much
   sharper and much stranger claim: the effect is entirely in the
   relationship, and reading it out requires bringing both halves together.

   </details>

## Summary

- $H$ on qubit 0 then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$, from $|00\rangle$, produces
  $|\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$.
- $|\Phi^+\rangle$ provably cannot be written as any
  $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, a direct proof by contradiction.
- The correlation survives a change of measurement basis
  ($(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle=|\Phi^+\rangle$), the phenomenon Bell's
  theorem sharpens into a proof; two bases alone do not yet rule out
  hidden variables.
- No signal is transmitted by entanglement alone: each qubit's local
  statistics are always uniformly random.
