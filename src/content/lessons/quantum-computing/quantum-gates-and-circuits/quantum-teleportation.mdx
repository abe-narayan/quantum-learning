## Not a trick, an approximation, or a loophole

Moving a qubit's state to Bob without the qubit traveling, and without
anyone learning its amplitudes, sounds like it has to be a trick, an
approximation, or a loophole in no-cloning. It is none of those. The
protocol is exact, it uses only tools already in this course, and its whole
cost is one pre-shared <Term id="entanglement">entangled pair</Term> plus two classical bits over an
ordinary channel.

The derivation below is the longest in this course, and worth doing in
full. Every step is algebra you have already done; what is new is the
regrouping in the middle, where Bob's qubit turns out to have been holding
the message all along.

## Measurement gives one bit; the state has two numbers

Alice has a qubit in an unknown state and wants Bob, far away, to end up
holding a qubit in that same state. She can't just measure her qubit and
describe the result to Bob: measurement only reveals one classical bit
about it (<Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapsing</Term> the rest), nowhere near enough to reconstruct
$\alpha,\beta$ exactly. <Term id="no-cloning-theorem">Cloning</Term> is ruled out. What's left is stranger and
more powerful: entangle her unknown qubit with a shared resource, measure
*that*, and let the entanglement itself carry the necessary information
to Bob's qubit, up to a small, fully-known correction Alice tells him
how to apply.

## Four branches, and which one Alice is in

### The setup

Three qubits. Qubit 0 is Alice's **message** qubit, in the unknown state
she wants to send: $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$. Qubits 1
and 2 are a <Term id="bell-states">Bell pair</Term> prepared in advance and shared between them: Alice
holds qubit 1, Bob holds qubit 2, and they may be arbitrarily far apart
by this point.

<QuantumStateDisplay state={messageState} label="The message to teleport, |ψ⟩" />

$$
|\psi\rangle\otimes|\Phi^+\rangle = (\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)\otimes\frac{|00\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\alpha|000\rangle+\alpha|011\rangle+\beta|100\rangle+\beta|111\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

### Alice entangles her two qubits

Alice applies $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$, her message qubit as control, her half
of the Bell pair as target, to the full 3-qubit state:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterEntangle} label="After CNOT(0,1)" />

Tracking each term: $\alpha|000\rangle$ and $\alpha|011\rangle$ have
control (qubit 0) equal to 0, so they're untouched; $\beta|100\rangle$
and $\beta|111\rangle$ have control equal to 1, so qubit 1 flips:

$$
\frac{\alpha|000\rangle+\alpha|011\rangle+\beta|110\rangle+\beta|101\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

### Alice applies Hadamard to her message qubit

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterHadamard} label="After H on qubit 0" />

$H$ sends a $|0\rangle$ on qubit 0 to $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$
and a $|1\rangle$ on qubit 0 to $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$.
Applying that to all four terms above, before any regrouping:

$$
\frac12\Big[\,\alpha(|000\rangle+|100\rangle) + \alpha(|011\rangle+|111\rangle) + \beta(|010\rangle-|110\rangle) + \beta(|001\rangle-|101\rangle)\,\Big]
$$

Now collect these eight terms by Alice's two qubits (0 and 1, the first
two labels), leaving Bob's qubit (the last label) as whatever's attached
to each pair, the same bilinear regrouping from Tensor Products applied
in reverse. The $|00\rangle$ pair picks up $\alpha|0\rangle$ (from
$|000\rangle$) and $\beta|1\rangle$ (from $|001\rangle$); the $|01\rangle$
pair picks up $\beta|0\rangle$ (from $|010\rangle$) and $\alpha|1\rangle$
(from $|011\rangle$); and so on for $|10\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$. Doing
this for all eight terms produces:

$$
\frac12\Big[\,|00\rangle\otimes(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle) + |01\rangle\otimes(\beta|0\rangle+\alpha|1\rangle) + |10\rangle\otimes(\alpha|0\rangle-\beta|1\rangle) + |11\rangle\otimes(-\beta|0\rangle+\alpha|1\rangle)\,\Big]
$$

Look closely: **Bob's qubit (the last factor) is $|\psi\rangle$ itself,
or a simple, fixed transformation of it, in every single branch**, and
which branch Alice is in is exactly what her two measurement outcomes
will tell her.

### Alice measures her two qubits

<QuantumStateDisplay state={firstMeasurement.collapsed} label={`Qubit 0 measured → outcome ${firstMeasurement.outcome}`} />
<QuantumStateDisplay state={secondMeasurement.collapsed} label={`Qubit 1 measured → outcome ${secondMeasurement.outcome}`} />

Each of Alice's two measurements is exactly 50/50, **regardless of
$\alpha,\beta$**. That has to be true: if her outcome statistics depended
on the message, Bob could learn something about $|\psi\rangle$ just from
knowing *how* Alice's measurements were distributed, without even
receiving her result, which would break no-signaling. They don't depend
on it, so nothing leaks that way.

Hadamard, her two measurements, and Bob's classical-conditioned
correction. Step through it column by column below. Each step names which

### Bob applies a correction

Reading off the four-branch formula above, Bob's qubit sits in one of
four states depending on Alice's outcomes $(m_0,m_1)$:

| Alice's outcome | Bob's qubit | Correction |
|---|---|---|
| $00$ | $\alpha\vert0\rangle+\beta\vert1\rangle$ | none |
| $01$ | $\beta\vert0\rangle+\alpha\vert1\rangle = X\vert\psi\rangle$ | apply $X$ |
| $10$ | $\alpha\vert0\rangle-\beta\vert1\rangle = Z\vert\psi\rangle$ | apply $Z$ |
| $11$ | $-\beta\vert0\rangle+\alpha\vert1\rangle = XZ\vert\psi\rangle$ | apply $X$, then $Z$ |

The same four rows, drawn as the branching process they are: two
measurements, each 50/50, and a correction fixed by which of the four
branches they land in.

Alice sends her two classical bits to Bob (at ordinary, light-speed-limited
communication speed, nothing here is instantaneous). Bob applies the
matching correction:

For the branch shown here (outcome $11$), Bob applies $X$ then $Z$ and
recovers $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ exactly, the *original*
$\alpha=0.6$, $\beta=0.8i$, with no approximation. This holds for every
one of the four branches: the correction table above is exact, not
probabilistic.

## Exactly one copy exists at any moment

Nothing here violates no-cloning: Alice's message qubit is *destroyed* by
her measurements (its amplitudes are gone the moment she measures);
exactly one copy of $|\psi\rangle$ exists at any time, first on Alice's
qubit, later on Bob's, never both simultaneously. Nothing violates
no-signaling either: without Alice's two classical bits, Bob's qubit
(averaged over the four equally-likely branches he doesn't yet know
which of) carries no usable information about $\psi$ at all. The
classical message is doing indispensable work, at ordinary speed.

## Faster than light, and other things this is not

<Callout type="mistake">
**"Teleportation sends information faster than light."** It doesn't.
Bob cannot reconstruct $|\psi\rangle$ until Alice's classical bits
arrive, and those travel no faster than any other classical signal.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Teleportation clones the state, now there are two."** There is
exactly one $|\psi\rangle$ at any time. Alice's message qubit is
consumed by her measurement; it does not survive alongside Bob's copy.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify the $01$ row of the correction table: show that
   $X(\beta|0\rangle+\alpha|1\rangle)$ recovers $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $X$ swaps the two amplitudes, so it swaps $\beta$ and $\alpha$ back into
   their original slots:

   $$
   X\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle = |\psi\rangle \checkmark
   $$

   Checked on the lesson's concrete message, $\alpha = 0.6$ and
   $\beta = 0.8i$: Bob's qubit in this branch is
   $0.8i\,|0\rangle + 0.6\,|1\rangle$, and applying $X$ returns
   $0.6\,|0\rangle + 0.8i\,|1\rangle$, the original, which the engine
   confirms exactly rather than to within a tolerance.

   The reason the correction is a single fixed gate is worth saying out
   loud, because it is the whole trick. Bob's qubit in this branch is
   $X|\psi\rangle$, and $X^2 = I$, so $X$ is its own undo. The same holds
   for $Z$ in the $10$ branch. Bob does not need to know $\alpha$ or
   $\beta$ to apply $X$; nobody in the protocol ever learns them, which is
   what keeps no-cloning intact.

   The wrong turn is checking the correction only on probabilities. In this
   branch $|\psi\rangle$ has $P(1) = 0.64$ and Bob's uncorrected qubit has
   $P(1) = 0.36$, so probabilities do flag the error here. Try the same
   check on the $10$ branch and it fails silently: $Z|\psi\rangle$ has
   exactly the same probabilities as $|\psi\rangle$, and only the relative
   phase differs. Teleportation has to move the phase too, so the
   verification has to be at the level of amplitudes.

   </details>

2. Verify the $11$ row: show that applying $X$ then $Z$ to
   $-\beta|0\rangle+\alpha|1\rangle$ recovers $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Apply $X$ first, swapping the amplitudes:

   $$
   X\begin{pmatrix}-\beta\\\alpha\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\-\beta\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Then $Z$, which leaves the top entry alone and negates the bottom one:

   $$
   Z\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\-\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = |\psi\rangle \checkmark
   $$

   With the lesson's numbers, the engine collapses the full three-qubit
   state on Alice's outcome $(1,1)$ and leaves Bob holding
   $-0.8i\,|0\rangle + 0.6\,|1\rangle$; after $X$ then $Z$ on qubit 2 it
   returns $0.6\,|0\rangle + 0.8i\,|1\rangle$, the original message
   exactly.

   The order is the part that actually needs verifying, and it is the part
   students get wrong. Bob is holding $XZ|\psi\rangle$, so the undo is
   $(XZ)^{-1} = Z^{-1}X^{-1} = ZX$: apply $X$ first, then $Z$. Doing it the
   other way, $Z$ then $X$, computes $XZ(XZ|\psi\rangle)$, and since
   $XZ = -ZX$ that equals $-XXZZ|\psi\rangle = -|\psi\rangle$. In this one
   case the damage is only a global phase, so Bob would in fact still be
   fine, which is exactly what makes the habit dangerous: it works here and
   fails as soon as the same reflex is applied where the leftover phase is
   relative rather than global, for instance inside a controlled operation
   or on one branch of a larger superposition. Inverting a product reverses
   the order, and it is worth getting right where the cost of being wrong
   is invisible.

   </details>

3. Suppose Alice's classical message is lost in transit. Describe what
   Bob's qubit looks like to him (hint: he does not know which of the four
   branches he is in).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It looks like a fair coin, in every basis, and it looks that way no
   matter what $|\psi\rangle$ Alice was sending.

   Bob holds one of $|\psi\rangle$, $X|\psi\rangle$, $Z|\psi\rangle$,
   $XZ|\psi\rangle$, each with probability $\tfrac14$ and no way to tell
   which. Averaging those four possibilities gives his reduced description,
   and the engine computes it directly by tracing Alice's two qubits out of
   the full pre-measurement state:

   $$
   \rho_{\text{Bob}} = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{P \in \{I, X, Z, XZ\}} P|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|P^\dagger = \frac{1}{2}I
   $$

   The maximally mixed state. Every measurement he can make returns $50/50$,
   the Z basis and the X basis and every basis in between alike, and
   crucially the answer $\tfrac12 I$ contains no $\alpha$ or $\beta$ at
   all: the same matrix comes out for every possible message. So the loss
   is total. Bob has not received a degraded or noisy version of
   $|\psi\rangle$; he has received a qubit statistically independent of it.

   This is not a flaw in the protocol, it is the property that makes it
   consistent. If Bob's qubit carried any trace of $\psi$ before the
   classical bits arrived, Alice could modulate $\psi$ and signal to him
   instantly. The two classical bits are not paperwork appended to a
   transfer that has already happened. They are, quantitatively, the whole
   of what Bob is missing, and they travel at ordinary speed.

   The wrong turn is answering "his qubit is in one of four states, so he
   has a one-in-four chance of being right if he guesses no correction".
   That is true and it is not an information channel. Guessing gives him
   the right qubit a quarter of the time with no way to know which quarter,
   so his outcome statistics are exactly what they would be for any other
   message, and averaged over the run he learns nothing. Also worth
   avoiding: calling his qubit "a superposition of the four". It is not a
   superposition, which would be a definite pure state with definite
   relative phases. It is a mixture, which is why the correct object is a
   density matrix and not a state vector.

   </details>

4. Why does the protocol need a *pre-shared* Bell pair? Could Alice and
   Bob instead just send an unentangled pair of qubits and get the same
   result?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. The entanglement is the only thing connecting Bob's qubit to
   Alice's, and without it his qubit is untouched by anything she does.

   Run the protocol with $|00\rangle$ in place of the Bell pair and follow
   the algebra. Alice's $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ and $H$ act only on qubits 0 and
   1, so the three-qubit state stays a product with qubit 2 as a separate
   factor, and qubit 2 stays $|0\rangle$ throughout. Tracing out Alice's
   two qubits, the engine returns $\rho_{\text{Bob}} = |0\rangle\langle0|$,
   with no dependence on $\alpha$ or $\beta$ anywhere. Alice's measurement
   destroys the message, Bob's qubit is unchanged, and no correction chosen
   from any table can repair it, since a fixed single-qubit gate applied to
   $|0\rangle$ produces one fixed state and cannot reproduce an arbitrary
   $|\psi\rangle$.

   Compare with the real protocol, where the same trace gives $\tfrac12 I$.
   That difference is the entire point. $\tfrac12 I$ is what a state looks
   like when it is entangled with something you cannot see, and it is the
   raw material the two classical bits then convert into $|\psi\rangle$.
   $|0\rangle\langle0|$ is a qubit that was never in the conversation.

   The "pre-shared" part answers a natural follow-up: if a qubit can travel
   from Alice to Bob to establish the pair, why not send the message qubit
   itself? Because the two shipments happen at different times and under
   different conditions. Entanglement can be distributed in advance, in
   bulk, at a convenient moment, and stored; the message may not exist yet,
   may be produced by a computation that has not run, or may need to move
   at a moment when no quantum channel is available. Teleportation converts
   a quantum channel used earlier into a quantum channel used now, paid for
   with two classical bits. That is a scheduling win, not a bandwidth one.

   Two wrong turns are worth naming. The first is thinking any shared
   randomness would do, a pair of correlated classical bits instead of a
   Bell pair. It would not: the four-branch regrouping in the derivation
   depends on interference between amplitudes, which classical correlation
   has none of. The second is imagining the Bell pair somehow carries
   $|\psi\rangle$ on its own. It cannot, since it was created before
   $|\psi\rangle$ was involved and is completely independent of it, which is
   precisely why sending it in advance is legitimate.

   </details>

## Summary

- Teleportation moves an unknown state $|\psi\rangle$ from Alice's qubit
  to Bob's, using a shared Bell pair, one CNOT, one Hadamard, two
  measurements, and a correction chosen from a fixed 4-row table.
- Alice's two measurement outcomes are always exactly 50/50 each,
  independent of $\psi$, required for consistency with no-signaling.
- The protocol destroys the original (measurement collapses it),
  consistent with no-cloning; it requires two classical bits of
  communication, consistent with no faster-than-light signaling.
