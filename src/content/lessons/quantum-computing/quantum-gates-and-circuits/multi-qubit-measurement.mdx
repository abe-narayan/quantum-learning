## A tool built along the way

Deriving the Bell-state correlation from the measurement postulate builds a
tool along the way: measuring *part* of a multi-qubit state. That operation
is not a convenience of the mathematics. Real hardware reads out one qubit
at a time, through that qubit's own readout line, and quantum teleportation
two lessons from now is built out of nothing else.

## Collapse, but only partly

Measuring a single-qubit state <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term> it to a definite basis state,
full stop. Measuring one qubit *within* a multi-qubit state has to do
something more careful: the other qubits are still there, still possibly
in superposition, and the measurement cannot erase them. What it
does is throw away every possibility inconsistent with the outcome you
got, and rescale what's left so it's still a valid, normalized state.

## Sum, then renormalize what survives

### The rule

To measure qubit $k$ of an $n$-qubit state $|\psi\rangle$: the
probability of outcome $b\in\{0,1\}$ is the sum of $|\text{amplitude}|^2$
over every <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> term whose qubit-$k$ bit equals $b$:

$$
P(\text{qubit } k = b) = \sum_{\substack{i:\, \text{bit } k \text{ of } i \,=\, b}} |c_i|^2
$$

If outcome $b$ occurs, the post-measurement state is obtained by zeroing
out every amplitude inconsistent with qubit $k=b$, then renormalizing the
survivors by dividing by $\sqrt{P(\text{qubit }k=b)}$:

$$
|\psi'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{P(b)}}\sum_{\substack{i:\, \text{bit } k \text{ of } i \,=\, b}} c_i |i\rangle
$$

This is the general single-qubit computational-basis <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> from the
last course, applied one qubit at a time instead of to the whole
register at once. The "collapse to a definite basis state" you already
know is the special case where the state being measured has only
one qubit to begin with.

### Applied to the Bell state

$|\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|00\rangle+\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|11\rangle$
has exactly one term with qubit 0 equal to 0 (the $|00\rangle$ term) and
one with qubit 0 equal to 1 (the $|11\rangle$ term), each with
$|c_i|^2=\frac12$. So $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0)=P(\text{qubit }0{=}1)=\frac12$,
confirming the 50/50 claim. The post-measurement state is the half worth
pausing on. Apply the rule above to the outcome-0 case, and note while you
do it that nobody has touched qubit 1:

### Not every partial measurement does this

Contrast with an unentangled example: $|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$, where
qubit 1 is definitely $|0\rangle$ and qubit 0 is independently in
superposition. Measure qubit 1:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={plusZero} label="|+⟩ ⊗ |0⟩, before any measurement" />
<QuantumStateDisplay state={plusZeroAfterMeasuringQubit1} label="After measuring qubit 1 (always outcome 0)" />

Qubit 1's outcome is certain (it was already definite), and qubit 0's
superposition survives untouched. That is the whole contrast: measuring one
qubit of a product state never disturbs the other, while measuring one
qubit of an entangled state generally does. <Term id="entanglement">Entanglement</Term> is what makes the
difference possible.

## Local, and yet not separable

Partial measurement is local in a strict sense: only the measured qubit's
degree of freedom is projected. But when the qubits are entangled, "only the
measured qubit" and "the whole state" aren't separable questions:
collapsing part of an indivisible state collapses the whole description
at once, which is why the untouched qubit's outcome becomes definite even
though nothing was done to it directly.

## One partial measurement, start to finish

**A 2-qubit state is $\frac{1}{2}|00\rangle+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|01\rangle$.
Find $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0)$ and the post-measurement state if that
outcome occurs.**

Both nonzero terms ($|00\rangle$ and $|01\rangle$) have qubit 0 equal to
0, so $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0) = \left(\frac12\right)^2+\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 = \frac14+\frac34=1$,
certain, as it should be, since qubit 0 never appears as 1 anywhere in
this state to begin with. The post-measurement state is unchanged:
$\frac{1}{2}|00\rangle+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|01\rangle$. Qubit 1 keeps
whatever superposition it already had.

## Not every partial measurement disturbs the rest

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming partial measurement always collapses the other qubits too.**
Only entangled qubits get disturbed this way; the worked example and the
$|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$ case above are both counterexamples.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to renormalize after zeroing out inconsistent amplitudes.**
The surviving amplitudes must be divided by $\sqrt{P(b)}$, not left as
they were in the original state, otherwise the result isn't a valid,
normalized quantum state.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $|\Psi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)$, find
   $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0)$ and the resulting state if that outcome
   occurs. What does qubit 1 become?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Exactly one term has qubit 0 equal to 0, namely $|01\rangle$, so

   $$
   P(\text{qubit }0{=}0) = \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12 .
   $$

   Zero out the inconsistent term and renormalize. Only $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|01\rangle$
   survives, and dividing by $\sqrt{1/2}$ gives amplitude exactly 1:

   $$
   |\psi'\rangle = |01\rangle .
   $$

   Qubit 1 becomes $|1\rangle$, with certainty. The engine agrees on both
   the probability and the collapsed state.

   The instructive difference from $|\Phi^+\rangle$ is the direction of the
   correlation, not its strength. In $|\Phi^+\rangle$ the partner matches;
   here the partner is the opposite. Both are perfect, in the sense that
   one outcome fixes the other with certainty, and both come from the same
   mechanical rule: keep the terms consistent with the outcome, discard the
   rest, renormalize. There is no separate physics for anticorrelation.

   Two slips are worth naming. The first is skipping the renormalization
   and reporting $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|01\rangle$, which is not a state; the
   division by $\sqrt{P}$ is what makes the total probability 1 again once
   you have thrown half of it away. The second is saying qubit 1 is "likely
   to be 1". It is 1, full stop. After the collapse there is no amplitude
   anywhere in the state with qubit 1 equal to 0, so no future measurement
   can return 0.

   </details>

2. For the same $|\Psi^+\rangle$, what happens if you measure qubit 1
   first instead of qubit 0? Should the final joint state depend on which
   qubit you measure first?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Measuring qubit 1 first gives $P(\text{qubit }1{=}0) = \tfrac12$,
   collapsing to $|10\rangle$, and $P(\text{qubit }1{=}1) = \tfrac12$,
   collapsing to $|01\rangle$. Compare with question 1's route: measuring
   qubit 0 first gives $|01\rangle$ or $|10\rangle$, each with probability
   $\tfrac12$. Same two outcomes, same two probabilities, same two final
   states.

   No, the final joint state does not depend on the order, and this is not
   a coincidence of this example. Two computational-basis measurements on
   different qubits are described by projectors that commute, so applying
   them in either order projects onto the same subspace and produces the
   same normalized survivor. The probability factorizes the same way too:
   $P(\text{q0}{=}0)\,P(\text{q1}{=}1 \mid \text{q0}{=}0) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1$
   going one way, and $P(\text{q1}{=}1)\,P(\text{q0}{=}0 \mid \text{q1}{=}1) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1$
   going the other. Both give $\tfrac12$ for the joint result $01$.

   The order-independence matters far beyond bookkeeping. Alice and Bob may
   be light-years apart holding the two halves, and there is in general no
   observer-independent fact about whose measurement happened first.
   Relativity would be in trouble if the joint statistics depended on an
   order that different observers disagree about. They do not, and this
   commuting-projectors fact is the mechanism, the same one behind
   entanglement's inability to signal.

   The wrong turn is reading collapse as a physical influence that
   propagates from the measured qubit to its partner, which invites the
   question "which one went first?" and then predicts an order dependence
   that does not exist. The rule the lesson states involves no propagation
   at all: it discards the branches inconsistent with what was learned.
   That said, order does matter the moment the two measurements stop
   commuting, for instance measuring qubit 0 in the Z basis and then again
   in the X basis. Different qubits, same basis, is the commuting case.

   </details>

3. A 3-qubit entangled state is
   $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$. Find the post-measurement
   state after measuring qubit 0 and getting outcome 1. What are qubits 1
   and 2 now? Then repeat for the lopsided version
   $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|000\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}|111\rangle$: which parts
   of your answer change, and which do not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   For the GHZ state, only $|111\rangle$ has qubit 0 equal to 1, so
   $P(\text{qubit }0{=}1) = \tfrac12$, and renormalizing the single
   survivor gives

   $$
   |\psi'\rangle = |111\rangle .
   $$

   Qubits 1 and 2 are both $|1\rangle$, each with certainty. One
   measurement on one qubit has determined all three values at once, which
   is what makes GHZ states the standard example of correlation that is not
   merely pairwise.

   For the lopsided version the probability changes and nothing else does.
   $P(\text{qubit }0{=}1) = \left|\sqrt{2/3}\right|^2 = \tfrac23$, and
   $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0) = \tfrac13$, which the engine confirms. But the
   collapsed states are still exactly $|111\rangle$ and $|000\rangle$,
   because renormalizing a single surviving term always drives its
   amplitude to 1 regardless of how large it was to begin with. The
   original amplitudes decide *how often* each branch happens; they have no
   say in *what* the branch looks like once it happens.

   That separation is the useful takeaway, and the slip it guards against
   is carrying the old amplitude through, reporting the collapsed state as
   $\sqrt{2/3}\,|111\rangle$. That vector has norm $\sqrt{2/3}$, not 1, so
   its probabilities would sum to $\tfrac23$: two thirds of the time
   something happens, and the rest of the time, apparently, nothing exists.
   Dividing by $\sqrt{P}$ is not cosmetic tidying; it is what keeps the
   post-measurement description a description of a system that is certainly
   still there.

   </details>

4. Explain why "measuring an entangled qubit always destroys
   superposition everywhere" is an overstatement. Construct or recall a
   3-qubit example where measuring one qubit leaves some superposition
   intact among the rest.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The overstatement is in the word "everywhere". Measuring one qubit
   removes every branch inconsistent with the outcome, and how much
   superposition that destroys depends entirely on how many branches
   survive. For GHZ, one branch survives and everything becomes definite.
   Whenever two or more survive, what is left is still a superposition, and
   can even still be entangled.

   The cleanest example is the W state:

   $$
   |W\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\left(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle\right).
   $$

   Measure qubit 0. Two of the three terms, $|001\rangle$ and $|010\rangle$,
   have qubit 0 equal to 0, so $P(\text{qubit }0{=}0) = \tfrac23$, and
   renormalizing the two survivors by $\sqrt{2/3}$ gives

   $$
   \frac{|001\rangle+|010\rangle}{\sqrt2},
   $$

   in which qubits 1 and 2 are left in $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle) = |\Psi^+\rangle$,
   a maximally entangled pair. The engine confirms the probability, the
   collapsed state, and that the residual two-qubit state has entanglement
   entropy $1$ bit. So measuring one qubit of a three-qubit entangled state
   here leaves the other two not merely superposed but as entangled as two
   qubits can be.

   The other outcome makes the contrast inside a single example. With
   probability $\tfrac13$ the measurement returns 1, only $|100\rangle$
   survives, and qubits 1 and 2 collapse to $|00\rangle$ with no
   superposition left at all. Same state, same measurement, and whether
   superposition survives depends on which branch you land in. That is the
   sharpest possible refutation of "always".

   The reasoning that leads people astray is generalizing from the Bell and
   GHZ examples, where every basis term carries a distinct value of the
   measured qubit, so each outcome is consistent with exactly one term.
   Those are the special cases in which the correlation is total. The
   general rule is the mechanical one this lesson gives, and it does not
   promise anything about how much survives.

   </details>

## Summary

- Measuring qubit $k$ sums $|c_i|^2$ over basis terms consistent with each
  outcome, then collapses to the surviving, renormalized amplitudes.
- Applied to a Bell state, this shows the partner qubit becomes exactly
  determined, not just statistically likely to match.
- Applied to a product state, the untouched qubit's superposition survives
  intact.
