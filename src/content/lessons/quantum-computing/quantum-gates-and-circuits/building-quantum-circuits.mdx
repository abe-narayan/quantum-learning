## No new mathematics, one more qubit

Nine lessons have built a toolkit: tensor products, <Term id="entanglement">entanglement</Term>, CNOT,
partial measurement, no-cloning, teleportation, interference. This lesson
introduces no new mathematics. It uses all of it at once on a circuit you
have not seen yet, extending the two-qubit Bell state to three qubits.

The three-qubit case is where a genuinely new question appears. Two
entangled qubits either agree or they don't. Three can be correlated in
ways that have no two-party analogue, and the state built below is the
standard example.

## A circuit identity, revisited

Before building something new, one more circuit-equivalence check,
useful in its own right, and good practice for verifying that two
differently-drawn circuits compute the same thing. **Controlled Gates and
CNOT** showed $\text{SWAP}=\text{CNOT}(0,1)\cdot\text{CNOT}(1,0)\cdot\text{CNOT}(0,1)$
by tracking $|10\rangle$ through all three steps. That same
step-by-step-tracking technique, apply one gate, note the new state, apply
the next, is the general method for verifying *any* circuit identity, and
it is what the rest of this lesson does for a circuit you have not seen.

$$
|000\rangle \xrightarrow{H} \frac{|000\rangle+|100\rangle}{\sqrt2} \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}(0,1)} \frac{|000\rangle+|110\rangle}{\sqrt2} \xrightarrow{\text{CNOT}(0,2)} \frac{|000\rangle+|111\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

This is a **GHZ state** (named for Greenberger, Horne, and Zeilinger),
the natural three-party generalization of a Bell pair. Qubit 0's
<Term id="superposition">superposition</Term>, via two CNOTs instead of one, has been copied onto *both*
of the other qubits' correlation structure, not their amplitudes
(no-cloning still holds; nothing here clones an unknown state, since
$|0\rangle$ is a known state and this whole circuit is fixed and public),
but their entanglement with qubit 0.

### Analyzing it

**Is it entangled?** By the same style of argument as the Bell-state
proof: if $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$ factored as
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle$, every one of the six
"mixed" basis terms ($|001\rangle,|010\rangle,|011\rangle,|100\rangle,|101\rangle,|110\rangle$)
would need zero amplitude, forcing at least one of $|a\rangle,|b\rangle,|c\rangle$
to have a zero component, which then also zeroes out one of the surviving
terms ($|000\rangle$ or $|111\rangle$). Entangled by the same
contradiction, one qubit more.

**What happens on measurement?** Measure qubit 0:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={ghzMeasured.collapsed} label={`Qubit 0 measured → outcome ${ghzMeasured.outcome}`} />

All three qubits become determined and equal: measuring *any one* of the
three tells you with certainty what the other two are. Three-party
correlation, from the same H-plus-CNOTs pattern that built a two-party one,
plus a single extra CNOT.

## Order, and the cloning objection

<Callout type="warning">
**Operator order.** Still the most common error across this entire
course: a circuit's diagram reads left to right; its operator product
reads right to left. Every derivation in this course has depended on
getting this right.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing "many CNOTs from one qubit" with cloning.** The GHZ circuit
uses qubit 0 as the control for two separate CNOTs. This spreads
*correlation*, not the qubit's unknown amplitude information. Start qubit 0
in an arbitrary unknown $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$
instead of $|0\rangle$ and redo the algebra: the circuit returns
$\alpha'|000\rangle+\beta'|111\rangle$ with
$(\alpha',\beta')=H(\alpha,\beta)$. That is a different state from the GHZ
one derived above, so "works as derived" is too strong a claim, and it is
entangled for every $|\psi\rangle$ except the two that $H$ sends to a
basis state: feed in $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$ and the output is the
plain product state $|000\rangle$ or $|111\rangle$. The no-cloning point survives the change intact. What
comes out is one *entangled* 3-qubit state, a long way from three
independent copies of $|\psi\rangle$.
</Callout>

**1.** Starting from $|00\rangle$, design a circuit (a sequence of gates)
that prepares $|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$.
(Hint: start from the H-then-CNOT recipe, and figure out what
single-qubit gate(s), applied before or after, produce the extra minus
sign and the swapped basis labels.)

<details className="answer-reveal">
<summary>A defensible answer</summary>

Many circuits work, so this is marked on whether the design is justified
and verified rather than on matching one answer. Here is one worked in
full, then two others that are just as good, then what a bad answer looks
like.

A worked version, four gates in this order: $X$ on qubit 1, then $H$ on
qubit 0, then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$, then $Z$ on qubit 0.

Trace it. $X$ on qubit 1 turns $|00\rangle$ into $|01\rangle$. The
H-then-CNOT recipe run from $|01\rangle$ gives
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle) = |\Psi^+\rangle$, which is the
right pair of basis labels and the wrong sign. Finally $Z$ on qubit 0
leaves the $|01\rangle$ term alone (its qubit 0 is $0$) and negates the
$|10\rangle$ term (its qubit 0 is $1$), giving
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle) = |\Psi^-\rangle$. The engine
returns amplitudes $0.7071$ on $|01\rangle$ and $-0.7071$ on $|10\rangle$,
and reports the result entangled.

The design logic is worth separating from the trace, because it
generalizes. The recipe's *input* controls which of the four Bell states
comes out: qubit 1's starting value decides whether the output labels
agree ($\Phi$) or disagree ($\Psi$), and qubit 0's starting value decides
the sign, because $H|1\rangle = |-\rangle$ carries a minus into the second
branch. So you can steer with either the input or with corrections
afterwards.

Equally defensible. Steering purely by input: $X$ on qubit 0 and $X$ on
qubit 1, then $H$ on qubit 0, then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$, which is the recipe
run from $|11\rangle$. The engine gives the identical final state, and
this version needs no correction gate at all. Or steer purely by output:
build $|\Phi^+\rangle$ the usual way, then apply $Z$ on qubit 0 and $X$ on
qubit 1, which the engine confirms lands on $|\Psi^-\rangle$ exactly. The
$X$ swaps the labels within each branch and the $Z$ supplies the sign, and
because they act on different wires their order does not matter. Any of
these earns full credit provided the trace is shown and the final
amplitudes are checked.

What a bad answer looks like: a circuit that produces
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle)$ and a claim that the minus sign
is "just a phase". It is not a global phase. $|\Psi^+\rangle$ and
$|\Psi^-\rangle$ are orthogonal states, distinguishable with certainty by
Bob's superdense decode circuit, and they behave differently under
$H\otimes H$. Global phase means an overall factor multiplying the
*entire* state; a sign on one of two branches is a relative phase, and
relative phases are physical. Also insufficient: any answer that stops at
"apply $Z$ somewhere" without saying which qubit. $Z$ on qubit 1 negates
the $|01\rangle$ term instead, giving $-|\Psi^-\rangle$ from
$|\Psi^+\rangle$, which happens to be the same physical state here but for
a different reason, and the reasoning is what is being tested.

</details>

**2.** Prove your circuit's output is entangled, using the
coefficient-matching contradiction method from the Bell-states lesson.

<details className="answer-reveal">
<summary>Answer</summary>

Suppose $|\Psi^-\rangle = |a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$. Matching against
$0|00\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|01\rangle - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|10\rangle + 0|11\rangle$:

$$
a_0b_0=0, \qquad a_0b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_1b_0=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \qquad a_1b_1=0
$$

From $a_0b_0 = 0$, either $a_0 = 0$ or $b_0 = 0$. If $a_0 = 0$ then
$a_0b_1 = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. If $b_0 = 0$ then
$a_1b_0 = 0 \neq -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. Both branches contradict, so no
product state equals $|\Psi^-\rangle$.

The determinant shortcut confirms it in one line:
$c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10} = 0 - \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = \tfrac12$,
nonzero, so the amplitude array does not factor as a row times a column.
The platform's separability check returns determinant magnitude
$\tfrac12$ with `separable: false`, and the entanglement entropy is $1$
bit, the two-qubit maximum.

Notice that the minus sign never appears in the argument. What kills
factorability is the pattern of *zeros*: two vanishing amplitudes on one
diagonal and two nonvanishing ones on the other. All four Bell states have
that pattern, on one diagonal or the other, which is why all four are
maximally entangled and why the sign that distinguishes $|\Psi^+\rangle$
from $|\Psi^-\rangle$ has no bearing on this question.

The wrong turn is a proof that reports having tried several candidate
factorizations without success. Not finding one is not the same as there
being none. The contradiction structure is what turns a search into a
theorem: it grants the factorization exists and derives something false,
ruling out every candidate at once.

</details>

**3.** Compute the measurement statistics: if you measure qubit 0 of your
prepared state, what are $P(0)$ and $P(1)$, and what does qubit 1 become
in each case?

<details className="answer-reveal">
<summary>Answer</summary>

$P(0) = P(1) = \tfrac12$. Only the $|01\rangle$ term has qubit 0 equal to
$0$, contributing $\left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12$, and only
the $|10\rangle$ term has qubit 0 equal to $1$, contributing
$\left|-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12$. Squaring the magnitude
discards the sign, which is why the two branches are equally likely
despite the asymmetric-looking amplitudes.

Outcome $0$: zero out everything inconsistent and renormalize, leaving
$|01\rangle$, so qubit 1 is $|1\rangle$ with certainty.

Outcome $1$: the same procedure leaves $-|10\rangle$, so qubit 1 is
$|0\rangle$ with certainty. The surviving minus sign is now the only
nonzero amplitude in the state, which makes it a global phase and
therefore unobservable. $-|10\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$ are the same
physical state.

Perfect anticorrelation, then: qubit 1 always comes out the opposite of
qubit 0. That is the pattern to compare against $|\Phi^\pm\rangle$, where
the two always agree. All four Bell states give $\tfrac12$ each on the
individual measurement, and differ entirely in the joint behaviour, which
is the same lesson as the Bell-states practice: marginals are blind to
correlation.

Two slips are worth naming. The first is expecting $P(1)$ to be smaller
because its amplitude is negative. Probability is $|c|^2$, so
$\left|-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12$ exactly as for the positive
branch; a negative *probability* would be meaningless. The second is
carrying the minus sign forward as though it still mattered. It was a
relative phase while two branches existed and it became global the moment
one of them was discarded. Compare with question 2, where the same sign
was equally irrelevant to entanglement but for a different reason.

</details>

**4.** Extend your circuit by one qubit, using the same
"one more CNOT from the same control" pattern as the GHZ derivation
above, to build a 3-qubit state with the same "flavor" of correlation as
$|\Psi^-\rangle$. Predict what measuring any one qubit tells you about
the other two.

<details className="answer-reveal">
<summary>Answer</summary>

Add a third qubit in $|0\rangle$ and apply $\text{CNOT}(0,2)$, keeping
qubit 0 as the control exactly as the GHZ circuit does.

Start from $|\Psi^-\rangle\otimes|0\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|010\rangle - |100\rangle)$.
The $|010\rangle$ term has qubit 0 equal to $0$, so the new CNOT leaves it
alone; the $|100\rangle$ term has qubit 0 equal to $1$, so its target,
qubit 2, flips:

$$
\frac{|010\rangle - |101\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

The engine confirms both the input and the output amplitudes.

Measuring any single qubit determines the other two with certainty,
because the state has only two branches and the three qubits take
different values in each. Outcome $0$ on qubit 0 leaves $|010\rangle$, so
qubits 1 and 2 are $1$ and $0$. Outcome $1$ on qubit 0 leaves
$-|101\rangle$, so they are $0$ and $1$. Measuring qubit 1 or qubit 2
instead works the same way, since each of them also takes a different
value in the two branches. This is the same all-or-nothing structure as
GHZ, with a different labelling: where GHZ correlates all three to agree,
this state correlates them to a fixed pattern that flips wholesale.

The honest caveat on the phrase "same flavor" is that this is a relabelled
GHZ state rather than a genuinely new kind of correlation. Applying $X$ to
qubit 1 turns $|010\rangle - |101\rangle$ into $|000\rangle - |111\rangle$,
and one more $Z$ on qubit 0 removes the sign, so the state is
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$ dressed in local gates.
Single-qubit gates cannot change how entangled a state is, so these are
the same entanglement wearing different clothes. Genuinely inequivalent
three-qubit entanglement does exist, and the standard example is the W
state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle)$, which no
sequence of single-qubit gates can turn into a GHZ state.

The wrong turn is to use qubit 1 as the second CNOT's control, on the
reasoning that you are chaining the correlation along. That builds
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|011\rangle - |100\rangle)$, which is still a fine
entangled state but is a different one, and the pattern the question asks
for is the GHZ pattern, a single control feeding every other wire.

</details>

**5.** Explain, in your own words, why none of the circuits you just
built could be used to send a message faster than light, even though
measuring one qubit instantly tells you something about qubits that
might be far away.

<details className="answer-reveal">
<summary>A defensible answer</summary>

Marked on the argument rather than the wording. One version in full, then
the alternatives, then the failure modes.

A worked version: signalling requires that something the sender chooses
changes something the receiver can observe. Alice holds qubit 0 and Bob
holds qubit 1 of $|\Psi^-\rangle$. Whatever Alice does to her half, and
whether she measures at all, Bob's own statistics are unchanged: his
outcome is $50/50$ before and after, in the computational basis and in
every other basis, because his reduced description is the maximally mixed
$\tfrac12 I$ regardless. The correlation is real, but it only becomes
visible when the two records are brought together and compared, and
carrying a record from one place to another is ordinary classical
communication at ordinary speed. Alice learning something about Bob's
qubit is not the same as Alice telling Bob something.

Equally defensible routes. You can argue from order-independence: the
joint statistics are identical whichever of them measures first, so there
is no fact about who influenced whom, and a mechanism that cannot even be
assigned a direction cannot carry a directed message. You can argue by
analogy, carefully: a pair of gloves split between two boxes gives perfect
anticorrelation with no signalling either, and then note where the analogy
breaks, since the quantum case also correlates in bases the glove model
has no answers for, which is what Bell's theorem is about. You can argue
from the protocols: teleportation needs two classical bits and superdense
coding needs a qubit to physically travel, and both are cases where the
theory forced exactly the classical communication that a
faster-than-light shortcut would have to avoid.

What a bad answer looks like: "the collapse is instantaneous but the
information travels at light speed", offered on its own. It states the
conclusion in the vocabulary of the puzzle without saying what stops Alice
from modulating Bob's outcomes, and the missing piece is the whole answer.
Also wrong is "you cannot control what outcome you get, so you cannot
encode a message". True but incomplete, because a sender could try to
signal by *choosing whether to measure at all*, or by choosing a basis,
neither of which requires controlling the outcome. Closing that gap needs
the stronger claim: Bob's marginal statistics are identical under every
choice Alice can make. And any answer resting on "the qubits are too far
apart for a signal to arrive in time" has the physics backwards; distance
is what makes the puzzle sharp, not what resolves it.

</details>

## Summary

- Every technique from this course, tensor products, CNOT, entanglement
  proofs, partial measurement, interference, composes directly: circuits
  are built the same way regardless of how many qubits are involved.
- The GHZ state, $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$, extends a
  Bell pair to three parties using one extra CNOT from the same control
  qubit, with a matching extension to the entanglement and correlation
  arguments.
- Analyzing any circuit, whether it is entangled and what measurement does
  to it, reuses the same techniques regardless of circuit size.
