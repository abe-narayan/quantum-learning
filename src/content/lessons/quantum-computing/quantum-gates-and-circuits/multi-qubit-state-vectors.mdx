The last lesson built multi-qubit states out of single-qubit ones, via ⊗. Does every valid multi-qubit state arise that way? The answer is no, and the states that don't are the ones this track is really about.
</LessonHook>

## If every state factored, none of this would matter

Suppose every two-qubit state could be built as $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$
from two single-qubit states. Then two qubits would be two separate qubits
in bigger notation, and nothing would be possible that a pair of
independent single-qubit systems could not already do. There would be no
quantum computing worth the name.

The counting argument below settles it in half a page, and the answer is
that product states are a thin slice of the whole state space. Everything
outside that slice, which is most of it, is entangled.

## Two counts that ought to agree, and don't

Compare two ways of specifying a 2-qubit state. A **product state**
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ is fully determined by two points on two
<Term id="bloch-sphere-term">Bloch spheres</Term>: that's it, nothing more to specify. A **general** 2-qubit
state is specified by 4 independent complex amplitudes. If those two
counts don't match, there must be states of the second kind that no choice
of the first kind can reach. They don't match, and the gap between them is
what <Term id="entanglement">entanglement</Term> means.

## Counting the parameters on each side

### The general 2-qubit state

$$
|\psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle + c_{01}|01\rangle + c_{10}|10\rangle + c_{11}|11\rangle, \qquad \sum_{ij}|c_{ij}|^2=1
$$

Four independent complex numbers, one real normalization constraint. This
generalizes directly: an $n$-qubit state is a normalized linear
combination of all $2^n$ <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> states
$|b_1b_2\ldots b_n\rangle$.

### Counting real parameters

**Product states.** Each single-qubit factor contributes 2 real
parameters (its own $\theta,\varphi$, after removing normalization and
global phase just as in the single-qubit course). Two factors:
$2+2=4$ real parameters can specify *any* product state.

**General 2-qubit states.** Four complex amplitudes are 8 real numbers.
Normalization removes 1. Overall <Term id="global-relative-phase">global phase</Term> (multiplying the whole
2-qubit state by $e^{i\gamma}$, unobservable for the same reason as
before) removes 1 more: $8-1-1=6$ real parameters.

$6 > 4$. The general state space is strictly larger than the set of
product states can reach. There is a full two-real-parameter's worth
of states that no tensor product of single-qubit states can produce.
Those are the **entangled** states.

### A state that isn't a product

The parameter count says entangled states have to exist. It does not say
which states those are, so pick a candidate and test it:
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$, a state you will meet again in
two lessons under a famous name. Commit to an answer before doing the
algebra.

Here is the algebra. Suppose the state *were* a product
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle = a_0b_0|00\rangle+a_0b_1|01\rangle+a_1b_0|10\rangle+a_1b_1|11\rangle$.
Matching coefficients:

$$
a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \quad a_0b_1=0, \quad a_1b_0=0, \quad a_1b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$

From $a_0b_1=0$: either $a_0=0$ or $b_1=0$. If $a_0=0$, then
$a_0b_0=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, a contradiction. If $b_1=0$, then
$a_1b_1=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction. Either way, no choice
of $a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$ works. The state cannot be factored, and the argument
covers every possible attempt at once rather than ruling out one guess at a
time. A fully worked version of this proof, for this state, is the
centerpiece of the next lesson.

## A description you could hand to someone else

A product state means the two qubits are physically independent: you
could describe qubit A's state completely on its own, hand it to someone
else, and lose no information. An entangled state has no such
description: asking "what is qubit A's state, by itself?" has no good
answer, and only the pair together has a well-defined state. This is not a
limitation of the notation. It shows up as a difference in how the system
behaves, starting next lesson with what happens when you measure one of
the two qubits and leave the other alone.

## One candidate, factored successfully

**Does $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle + |01\rangle)$ factor as a product
state?**

Try $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ with $a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
$a_0b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $a_1b_0=0$, $a_1b_1=0$. Since
$a_0b_0\neq0$, $a_0\neq0$; then $a_1b_0=0$ forces $a_1=0$ or $b_0=0$. Try
$a_1=0$ (consistent with $a_1b_1=0$ automatically). Then
$|a\rangle=|0\rangle$, and $b_0=1,b_1=1$ up to normalization gives
$|b\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)=|+\rangle$. Check:
$|0\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|01\rangle)$, yes,
it factors. This state, unlike the Bell numerator above, is *not*
entangled: qubit 0 is definitely $|0\rangle$, qubit 1 is independently
$|+\rangle$.

## The gap only widens with more qubits

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming every multi-qubit state is "really" two separate qubits.**
The parameter-counting argument above shows this is false for a whole
$2$-real-parameter family of 2-qubit states, and the gap only grows for
more qubits.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Trying to guess factorability instead of solving for it.** Some states
factor in ways that aren't obvious by inspection (like the worked example
above). When in doubt, set up the four coefficient equations and solve,
or look for a contradiction the way the Bell-numerator proof does.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. How many real parameters does a general normalized 3-qubit state have
   (after removing normalization and global phase)? How many does a
   product of three single-qubit states have? Is the gap bigger or
   smaller than in the 2-qubit case?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Run the lesson's count with $n=3$. A general 3-qubit state has
   $2^3 = 8$ complex amplitudes, so $16$ real numbers. Normalization is one
   real constraint and global phase is one unobservable real direction, so

   $$
   16 - 1 - 1 = 14 \text{ real parameters.}
   $$

   A product $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle$ is three points on
   three Bloch spheres, $2$ angles each:

   $$
   3 \times 2 = 6 \text{ real parameters.}
   $$

   The gap is $14 - 6 = 8$. In the 2-qubit case it was $6 - 4 = 2$, so the
   gap is four times larger, and it is growing far faster than the counts
   themselves. The general formula makes the trend explicit: an $n$-qubit
   state has $2^{n+1} - 2$ parameters against a product state's $2n$, so
   the gap is $2^{n+1} - 2n - 2$. At $n = 10$ that is $2026$ against $20$.

   The lesson to draw is stronger than "entangled states exist". Product
   states are a $2n$-dimensional surface sitting inside an exponentially
   large space, so as qubit count rises they become a vanishingly thin
   sliver of it. Entanglement is not an exotic special case you have to go
   looking for. It is the generic condition, and an unentangled multi-qubit
   state is the rarity.

   The miscount to avoid is subtracting a global phase from every factor
   separately, getting $3\times 2 = 6$ for the product side but $16 - 1 - 3 = 12$
   for the general side. There is only one global phase for the whole
   system, not one per qubit. The relative phases *between* the factors are
   exactly the physically meaningful information you must not throw away,
   and discarding three phases would quietly delete two of them.

   </details>

2. Does $\frac{1}{2}(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)$ factor
   as a product state? Find $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$ or show it's impossible.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. It is $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$.

   Set up the four coefficient equations against
   $a_0b_0, a_0b_1, a_1b_0, a_1b_1$, all four equal to $\tfrac12$ here. No
   coefficient is zero, so no factor can be zero, and the equations are
   consistent: $a_0 = a_1$ (from $a_0b_0 = a_1b_0$ and $b_0 \neq 0$) and
   $b_0 = b_1$ likewise. Normalizing each factor gives
   $|a\rangle = |b\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) = |+\rangle$,
   and the check runs forward:

   $$
   |+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \tfrac12\left(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle\right) \checkmark
   $$

   There is a faster test worth carrying forward. Arrange the amplitudes as
   a $2\times2$ array with rows indexed by qubit 0 and columns by qubit 1.
   A product state's array is an outer product $a_ib_j$, whose rows are all
   proportional to each other, so its determinant $c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10}$
   must vanish. Here $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = 0$, and
   the platform's separability routine returns exactly this: separable, with
   determinant magnitude $0$. That single number decides factorability for
   any two-qubit state, and question 3 is the case where it does not vanish.

   The wrong turn is pattern-matching on "all four basis states are
   present, so the qubits must be correlated". Presence of all four terms is
   the *signature of independence* here, not of entanglement, because two
   independent coin flips also produce all four outcomes. What entanglement
   requires is that the four amplitudes fail to factor as row times column,
   and these do factor.

   </details>

3. Does $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle - |11\rangle)$ factor? (Use the same
   coefficient-matching method as the Bell-numerator proof.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. It is entangled, and the minus sign changes nothing about that.

   Matching coefficients requires

   $$
   a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \quad a_0b_1=0, \quad a_1b_0=0, \quad a_1b_1=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}.
   $$

   From $a_0b_1 = 0$, either $a_0 = 0$ or $b_1 = 0$. If $a_0 = 0$ then
   $a_0b_0 = 0 \neq \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. If $b_1 = 0$ then
   $a_1b_1 = 0 \neq -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. Both branches contradict, so no
   $|a\rangle, |b\rangle$ exists. The determinant test agrees:
   $c_{00}c_{11} - c_{01}c_{10} = \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) - 0 = -\tfrac12$,
   magnitude $\tfrac12$, and the engine's separability check reports exactly
   that value with `separable: false`. Its entanglement entropy is $1$ bit,
   the maximum a two-qubit state can carry.

   The structural point is that the *zeros* do the work, not the signs.
   Whenever a two-qubit state has two vanishing amplitudes sitting diagonally
   opposite each other and two nonvanishing ones on the other diagonal, the
   same two-branch contradiction fires. Sign, and more generally phase, never
   enters the argument.

   That is what makes the seductive answer seductive. A student who has just
   seen the proof for $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ often reads
   the minus as "a different, less symmetric state, so maybe this one does
   factor", or tries to absorb the sign into $|b\rangle$ by taking
   $b_1 \to -b_1$. Absorbing the sign is legitimate and completely useless:
   it moves the minus around but leaves the two off-diagonal zeros exactly
   where they were. A cleaner way to see it in one line is that this state is
   $(I \otimes Z)$ applied to $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$, and
   a gate acting on one qubit alone cannot create or destroy entanglement, so
   the two states must be equally entangled.

   </details>

4. Explain in one or two sentences why "entangled" is a property of the
   *state*, not of the *qubits themselves*: the same two physical qubits
   can be prepared in either an entangled or a product state.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>A defensible answer</summary>

   This one is marked on the defence rather than on a unique wording, so
   what follows is one full version, then the alternatives that earn the
   same credit and the ones that do not.

   A worked version: entanglement is a statement about the amplitude
   pattern of the joint state vector, namely whether it factors as
   $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, and the same pair of physical qubits carries
   whatever state you last prepared on them. Two qubits sitting in
   $|00\rangle$ are a product state; apply $H$ to qubit 0 and then
   $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ and the very same two qubits are in
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$, which provably does not
   factor. Nothing about the hardware changed, only the vector describing
   it, and one more $\text{CNOT}$ and $H$ would take them back.

   Equally defensible routes to the same point. You can argue from the
   parameter count: the set of product states is a measure-zero surface
   inside the full state space, and preparation moves a system around inside
   that space, so "entangled" labels a region, not a device. You can argue
   operationally: entanglement is detected by correlations across many
   repeated preparations, so it is a property of the preparation procedure,
   which is another name for the state. You can also note that entanglement
   is always relative to a chosen split of the system into parts, so it
   cannot be an intrinsic property of any one qubit.

   What a bad answer looks like: "the qubits become entangled particles",
   or anything that treats entanglement as a substance deposited on the
   hardware, a connection or channel that now exists between the two chips.
   That picture predicts things that are false, most obviously that you
   could use the connection to signal. It also has no room for the fact
   that a single $\text{CNOT}$ removes the entanglement completely, which
   is easy to check and hard to explain if entanglement is a physical
   attachment rather than a description of the state. Also insufficient:
   restating the definition without addressing the "same two qubits, either
   way" claim the question actually asks about.

   </details>

## Summary

- A general $n$-qubit state needs $2^n$ complex amplitudes, one
  normalization constraint, and has one unobservable global phase.
- Counting real parameters shows product states are a strictly smaller
  set than all valid multi-qubit states; the states outside that set are
  entangled.
- $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ provably cannot be written as
  any $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$.
- A state's own two qubits, considered separately, may have no
  well-defined individual state at all.
