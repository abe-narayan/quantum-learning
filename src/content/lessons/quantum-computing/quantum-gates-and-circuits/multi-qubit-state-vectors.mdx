## Motivation

The last lesson built multi-qubit states *out of* single-qubit ones, via
$\otimes$. That raises an obvious question: does every valid multi-qubit
state arise this way? If it did, two qubits would really just be "two
separate qubits" dressed up in bigger notation. Nothing new would be
possible that a pair of independent single-qubit systems couldn't already
do. The answer is no, and pinning down exactly why is one of the most
important arguments in this course.

## Intuition

Compare two ways of specifying a 2-qubit state. A **product state**
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ is fully determined by two points on two
Bloch spheres: that's it, nothing more to specify. A **general** 2-qubit
state is specified by 4 independent complex amplitudes. If those two
counts don't match, there must be states of the second kind that no
choice of the first kind can reach. They don't match, and the gap between
them is exactly what "entanglement" means.

## Mathematical Development

### The general 2-qubit state

$$
|\psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle + c_{01}|01\rangle + c_{10}|10\rangle + c_{11}|11\rangle, \qquad \sum_{ij}|c_{ij}|^2=1
$$

Four independent complex numbers, one real normalization constraint. This
generalizes directly: an $n$-qubit state is a normalized linear
combination of all $2^n$ computational basis states
$|b_1b_2\ldots b_n\rangle$.

### Counting real parameters

**Product states.** Each single-qubit factor contributes 2 real
parameters (its own $\theta,\varphi$, after removing normalization and
global phase exactly as in the single-qubit course). Two factors:
$2+2=4$ real parameters can specify *any* product state.

**General 2-qubit states.** Four complex amplitudes are 8 real numbers.
Normalization removes 1. Overall global phase (multiplying the whole
2-qubit state by $e^{i\gamma}$, unobservable for the same reason as
before) removes 1 more: $8-1-1=6$ real parameters.

$6 > 4$. The general state space is strictly larger than the set of
product states can reach. There is a genuine two-real-parameter's worth
of states that no tensor product of single-qubit states can produce.
Those are the **entangled** states.

### A state that isn't a product

Take the (unnormalized-looking, but check it) state
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$: you'll recognize this in two
lessons as a specific, famous state. Suppose it *were* a product state
$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle = a_0b_0|00\rangle+a_0b_1|01\rangle+a_1b_0|10\rangle+a_1b_1|11\rangle$.
Matching coefficients:

$$
a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}, \quad a_0b_1=0, \quad a_1b_0=0, \quad a_1b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$

From $a_0b_1=0$: either $a_0=0$ or $b_1=0$. If $a_0=0$, then
$a_0b_0=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, a contradiction. If $b_1=0$, then
$a_1b_1=0\neq\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, also a contradiction. Either way, no
choice of $a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$ works. This state genuinely cannot be
factored. A fully worked version of exactly this proof, for this exact
state, is the centerpiece of the next lesson.

## Physical Interpretation

A product state means the two qubits are physically independent: you
could describe qubit A's state completely on its own, hand it to someone
else, and lose no information. An entangled state has no such
description: asking "what is qubit A's state, by itself?" has no good
answer, only the pair together has a well-defined state. This isn't a
limitation of notation. It reflects something genuinely different about
how the system behaves, with consequences (starting next lesson) for what
happens when you measure just one of the two qubits.

## Worked Example

**Does $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle + |01\rangle)$ factor as a product
state?**

Try $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ with $a_0b_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
$a_0b_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $a_1b_0=0$, $a_1b_1=0$. Since
$a_0b_0\neq0$, $a_0\neq0$; then $a_1b_0=0$ forces $a_1=0$ or $b_0=0$. Try
$a_1=0$ (consistent with $a_1b_1=0$ automatically). Then
$|a\rangle=|0\rangle$, and $b_0=1,b_1=1$ up to normalization gives
$|b\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)=|+\rangle$. Check:
$|0\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|01\rangle)$, yes,
it factors. This state, unlike the Bell numerator above, is *not*
entangled: qubit 0 is definitely $|0\rangle$, qubit 1 is independently
$|+\rangle$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming every multi-qubit state is "really" two separate qubits."**
The parameter-counting argument above shows this is false for a whole
$2$-real-parameter family of 2-qubit states, and the gap only grows for
more qubits.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Trying to guess factorability instead of solving for it.** Some states
factor in ways that aren't obvious by inspection (like the worked example
above). When in doubt, set up the four coefficient equations and solve,
or look for a contradiction the way the Bell-numerator proof does.
</Callout>

4. Explain in one or two sentences why "entangled" is a property of the
   *state*, not of the *qubits themselves*: the same two physical qubits
   can be prepared in either an entangled or a product state.

## Summary

- A general $n$-qubit state needs $2^n$ complex amplitudes, one
  normalization constraint, and has one unobservable global phase.
- Counting real parameters shows product states are a strictly smaller
  set than all valid multi-qubit states; the states outside that set are
  entangled.
- $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$ provably cannot be written as
  any $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$.
- A state's own two qubits, considered separately, may have no
  well-defined individual state at all.
