## Dead on arrival, until the pair is counted

Alice needs to send Bob two classical bits, and the channel between them
will carry a single qubit, once. Measured on its own, one qubit yields at
most one bit, so the plan looks dead on arrival.

What rescues it is a resource distributed before the message existed: a
shared <Term id="bell-states">Bell pair</Term>. Teleportation used that same resource, plus one CNOT,
one Hadamard, two measurements, and two classical bits, to move an unknown
qubit state. This lesson runs nearly the same circuit backwards, for the
opposite purpose. Instead of spending <Term id="entanglement">entanglement</Term> and two classical bits
to move a quantum state, Alice spends entanglement and one qubit to move
two classical bits, and Bob recovers them exactly.

Keep an eye on the bookkeeping. The claim is not that one qubit carries two
bits of information out of nowhere. Half the pair had to be delivered to
Bob at some earlier point, and that delivery is where the second bit's
worth of transmission was paid for.

## Where the two bits are actually stored

Start from a Bell pair, held half by Alice and half by Bob. Alice picks
one of four fixed single-qubit gates, applies it to her half alone, and
physically mails Bob that one qubit. Bob, now holding both halves, runs a
single circuit and reads both bits off the pair with certainty. Nothing
about that sequence is approximate or probabilistic once Bob's qubit
arrives: the four outcomes are perfectly distinguishable, the same way the
four Bell states always have been.

The question worth carrying through the derivation is where the two bits
are stored between Alice's gate and Bob's decode.

## Four gates, four Bell states, one decode

### The setup: a shared Bell pair

Exactly as in Quantum Teleportation, Alice and Bob share a Bell pair
prepared in advance, $H$ on qubit 0 then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$:

### Alice encodes two bits into her one qubit

Alice wants to send two classical bits. She applies one of four
fixed gates to *her* qubit alone (qubit 0), chosen by the bits she wants to
send, and nothing at all to Bob's:

| Bits to send | Alice's gate | Resulting joint state |
|---|---|---|
| $00$ | $I$ (nothing) | $\vert\Phi^+\rangle$ |
| $01$ | $X$ | $\vert\Psi^+\rangle$ |
| $10$ | $Z$ | $\vert\Phi^-\rangle$ |
| $11$ | $Z$ then $X$ | $-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$ |

This is the four-row table Quantum Teleportation used for *Bob's*
correction, applied here by *Alice*, deliberately rather than as a
correction for a random measurement outcome. Computing all four with the
real engine:

of which of the four messages she's sending.

### Bob decodes: the teleportation circuit, reversed

Bob now holds both qubits. Quantum Teleportation's mid-protocol step
applied $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ then $H$ on qubit 0 to *disentangle* a message
qubit from a Bell pair before measuring. Bob runs that same circuit here,
and because $H$ and $\text{CNOT}$ are each their own inverse, this is
the adjoint of the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ circuit that built the
Bell pair in the first place:

<div className="not-prose my-6 grid grid-cols-1 gap-4 sm:grid-cols-2 lg:grid-cols-4">
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[0]} label="Decoded 00" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[1]} label="Decoded 01" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[2]} label="Decoded 10" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[3]} label="Decoded 11" />
</div>

Every one of the four decodes to the corresponding computational basis
state with probability 1 (the $11$ case lands on $-\vert11\rangle$, same
$100\%$ measurement probability, the global phase again unobservable). Bob
measures both qubits and reads off the two bits Alice sent, every
time, with no ambiguity.

Here is the whole protocol as one circuit. Step through Alice's four
possible messages below and watch only the middle of the circuit change:
zero, one, or two gates depending on which two bits she's sending. The
preparation at the start and Bob's decode at the end never move.

The first two gates are the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ preparation from the
section above. The last two gates before measurement, Bob's decode, are
$\text{CNOT}$-then-$H$: the same two gates as the preparation, in
the opposite order. That mirror-image relationship is why the
decode undoes the preparation regardless of how many gates Alice's
encoding step inserted in between.

### Why the pair has to be entangled, not merely in superposition

This only works because Alice's gate acts on a qubit that starts
*entangled* with Bob's, not merely in superposition. Replace the true Bell
pair with the unentangled product state $\vert+\rangle\otimes\vert0\rangle$
(qubit 0 measured alone is still $50/50$, exactly as it was, but qubit 1 is
no longer correlated with it), leave Alice's gate at $I$ (message $00$),
and run Bob's identical decode circuit:

Instead of landing on $\vert00\rangle$ with certainty, Bob gets a uniform
$25\%$ chance of each of the four outcomes: no information survives.
Superposition on Alice's qubit alone was never the resource; the
entanglement between the two qubits is.

## The Holevo bound, respected on both counts

No communication law is broken here. The <Term id="holevo-quantity">Holevo bound</Term> limits how much
classical information a *single* qubit can carry *on its own* to one bit;
superdense coding respects it once the accounting includes both qubits the
protocol uses. Distributing the Bell pair required sending one qubit (from
whoever prepared it to whichever of Alice or Bob did not have it), and the
message required a second, Alice's. Two qubits of quantum communication
carried two classical bits: one bit per qubit on average, which is what
Holevo demands.

What superdense coding buys is timing. The entanglement-distributing qubit
can be sent long before Alice knows what she wants to say, so at the moment
of the message only one additional qubit has to move.

<InsightBlock>
Superdense coding does not beat the one-bit-per-qubit bound. It moves half the cost earlier in time, to a moment when nobody yet knows what the message will be.
</InsightBlock>

## Verifying one row of the table by hand

**Verify the $11$ row by hand: show that applying $Z$ then $X$ to Alice's
half of $\vert\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle+\vert11\rangle)$
gives $-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$, and that Bob's decode circuit recovers
$\vert11\rangle$.**

Apply $Z$ to qubit 0 first: $Z\vert0\rangle=\vert0\rangle$ and
$Z\vert1\rangle=-\vert1\rangle$, so
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle+\vert11\rangle)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle-\vert11\rangle)=\vert\Phi^-\rangle$.
Then apply $X$ to qubit 0: $X\vert0\rangle=\vert1\rangle$ and
$X\vert1\rangle=\vert0\rangle$, so
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle-\vert11\rangle)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert10\rangle-\vert01\rangle)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert10\rangle)=-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$,
matching `encodedStates[3]` above exactly.

For the decode, apply $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ to
$-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert10\rangle)$: the $\vert01\rangle$
term has control qubit $0$, so target qubit 1 is untouched, staying
$\vert01\rangle$; the $\vert10\rangle$ term has control qubit $1$, so
target flips, $\vert10\rangle\to\vert11\rangle$. Result:
$-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert11\rangle)$. Now apply $H$ to qubit
0: on the $\vert0\rangle$-control terms, $H\vert0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert0\rangle+\vert1\rangle)$
contributes to $\vert01\rangle$; on the $\vert1\rangle$-control term,
$H\vert1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert0\rangle-\vert1\rangle)$ contributes
to $-\vert11\rangle$. Collecting terms exactly cancels every amplitude
except $-\vert11\rangle$, matching `decodedStates[3]` above: Bob measures
$11$ with certainty, up to the unobservable overall minus sign.

## She sends exactly one qubit

<Callout type="warning">
**"Alice sends two qubits."** She sends exactly one: her own half of the
pre-shared pair, after applying one of four single-qubit gates to it. Bob's
qubit never travels; it was already in his possession.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"This violates the Holevo bound, or lets Alice signal Bob
instantaneously."** Neither. Holevo's bound is respected once the
Bell-pair-distributing qubit is counted alongside Alice's message qubit,
two qubits of quantum communication for two classical bits. And the
message qubit still has to physically travel from Alice to Bob at ordinary
speed; nothing about entanglement changes that.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Bob can decode by measuring his qubit alone, without waiting for
Alice's."** Before Alice's qubit arrives, Bob holds only half of an
entangled pair; measuring it alone gives a uniformly random bit no matter
what gate Alice applied; see Bell States and Entanglement for why either
half of an entangled pair, measured on its own, is a uniformly random bit. The two bits only become readable once both qubits are back
together for the joint decode circuit.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify the $01$ row: show that applying $X$ to qubit 0 of
   $\vert\Phi^+\rangle$ gives $\vert\Psi^+\rangle$, and that Bob's decode
   circuit recovers $\vert01\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Encoding first. $X$ acts on qubit 0 only, flipping the first label of
   each term:

   $$
   (X\otimes I)\frac{\vert00\rangle+\vert11\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\vert10\rangle+\vert01\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \vert\Psi^+\rangle
   $$

   Now the decode, $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ then $H$ on qubit 0. The CNOT reads
   qubit 0 in each term: $\vert01\rangle$ has control 0 and is untouched;
   $\vert10\rangle$ has control 1, so its target flips to give
   $\vert11\rangle$. That leaves

   $$
   \frac{\vert01\rangle+\vert11\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{\vert0\rangle+\vert1\rangle}{\sqrt2}\otimes\vert1\rangle = \vert+\rangle\otimes\vert1\rangle .
   $$

   The CNOT has done the essential work: the state is a product again, and
   qubit 1 already carries the second bit. Now $H$ on qubit 0 sends
   $\vert+\rangle$ to $\vert0\rangle$, giving $\vert01\rangle$ exactly. The
   engine returns probabilities $[0,1,0,0]$.

   Watching the entanglement disappear at the CNOT is the clearest way to
   see why the decode is the preparation circuit run backwards. Preparation
   was $H$ then CNOT, taking a product state to a Bell state. Decoding is
   CNOT then $H$, taking a Bell state back to a product state. Each gate is
   its own inverse, so reversing the order inverts the whole circuit, and
   Alice's gate in the middle survives as a relabelling of which product
   state you come back to.

   The wrong turn is applying the CNOT to the whole state as if it acted on
   qubit 0. It leaves qubit 0 alone in every term; what changes is qubit 1.
   A student who flips the control instead reaches
   $\vert11\rangle$ and $\vert00\rangle$, then finds the $H$ does not
   produce a definite answer, which is the symptom rather than the cause.

   </details>

2. Alice's four encodings are $I$, $X$, $Z$ and $Z$ then $X$. Suppose she
   is allowed *any* single-qubit unitary on her half instead. What is the
   largest number of distinct messages Bob can still decode with
   certainty, and what fixes that ceiling? As a check on your reasoning,
   work out what message Alice sends if she applies $Y$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Four, so two bits, and no unitary of Alice's can raise it.

   The ceiling comes from dimension plus orthogonality, and both halves are
   needed. Bob's decode is a fixed unitary followed by a measurement, so
   two messages are perfectly distinguishable only if their joint states
   are orthogonal: any overlap means the two states share amplitude on some
   outcome, and Bob's readout is then ambiguous with nonzero probability,
   however cleverly he decodes. Two qubits span a $4$-dimensional space, so
   at most $4$ mutually orthogonal states fit in it. Alice's gate cannot
   change that, because $(U\otimes I)$ leaves her in the same
   $4$-dimensional space she started in.

   So the four Bell states are not one clever choice among many. They are a
   complete orthonormal basis of the space, which is the maximum a
   two-qubit system offers, and the protocol is optimal rather than merely
   ingenious. Scaling up says the same thing: sharing $n$ entangled pairs
   and sending $n$ qubits transmits $2n$ bits, never more.

   The $Y$ check is the useful test of whether the reasoning is really
   about orthogonality rather than about the specific gate names. Since
   $Y = iXZ$, applying $Y$ to Alice's half gives $i$ times what $Z$-then-$X$
   gives, so the joint state is $-i\vert\Psi^-\rangle$ and Bob decodes to
   $-i\vert11\rangle$: message $11$, engine confirmed, identical to the
   $ZX$ row. The overall phase is unobservable, so $Y$ is not a fifth
   message; it is the fourth message written differently. Alice's real
   freedom is a choice of four mutually orthogonal *directions*, and phases
   along those directions buy nothing.

   The wrong turn is to reason that a continuum of unitaries must give a
   continuum of messages, since Alice has three real parameters to play
   with and could surely encode more than two bits in them. She can indeed
   produce a continuum of distinct joint states. What she cannot do is make
   more than four of them mutually orthogonal, and without orthogonality
   Bob's decoding stops being certain. Near-orthogonal states can be
   distinguished most of the time, which is a real and studied setting, but
   it is no longer the exact protocol this lesson derives.

   </details>

3. Count every qubit and classical bit that has to cross between Alice and
   Bob in superdense coding and in teleportation, including the
   distribution of the Bell pair. What does each protocol achieve per unit
   of communication *at the moment the message exists*? Then say what
   happens to superdense coding's advantage if the Bell pair cannot be
   distributed in advance.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Superdense coding: one qubit crosses early, to distribute the pair, and
   one qubit crosses at message time. Total $2$ qubits, $0$ classical bits,
   delivering $2$ classical bits.

   Teleportation: one qubit crosses early, to distribute the pair, and $2$
   classical bits cross at message time. Total $1$ qubit and $2$ classical
   bits, delivering one arbitrary unknown qubit state.

   At the moment the message exists, superdense coding moves $2$ classical
   bits for the price of $1$ qubit, twice what a lone qubit could carry
   unaided. Teleportation moves a full qubit state for the price of $2$
   classical bits and no quantum channel at all, which is the more
   remarkable of the two, since a qubit state is a pair of continuous
   amplitudes and $2$ classical bits are nowhere near enough to describe
   them. Both are paid for out of a shared resource that was banked before
   anyone knew what the message would be. The two protocols are near
   mirror images: one spends entanglement to make a quantum channel out of
   a classical one, the other spends entanglement to double a quantum
   channel's classical capacity.

   If the pair cannot be distributed in advance, superdense coding's
   advantage disappears completely. Alice must now send one qubit to
   establish the pair and one qubit to carry the message, two qubits at
   message time for two classical bits, which is exactly one bit per qubit.
   She could have got that by encoding the two bits directly in two qubits
   prepared as $\vert0\rangle$ or $\vert1\rangle$, with no entanglement, no
   Bell pair and no decode circuit. The Holevo bound of one classical bit
   per qubit was never violated at any point; superdense coding only moves
   half the cost earlier in time.

   The wrong turn is describing superdense coding as "sending two bits with
   one qubit" full stop, without the earlier shipment in the ledger. Stated
   that way it does contradict the Holevo bound, and a reader who accepts
   it has been taught something false. The correct claim is a scheduling
   claim: two qubits total, one of which can be sent at a convenient
   moment long before the message exists.

   </details>

4. Replace the shared Bell pair with a partially entangled one,
   $\cos\theta\,\vert00\rangle+\sin\theta\,\vert11\rangle$, and let Alice
   send message $00$ (gate $I$). Work out Bob's four decoded outcome
   probabilities as a function of $\theta$, then evaluate them at
   $\theta=\pi/4$ and at $\theta=\pi/8$. What does the comparison say about
   which resource the protocol is actually spending?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Run the decode on the general pair. $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ sends
   $\vert00\rangle$ to itself and $\vert11\rangle$ to $\vert10\rangle$, so
   the state becomes
   $(\cos\theta\,\vert0\rangle+\sin\theta\,\vert1\rangle)\otimes\vert0\rangle$,
   a product state with qubit 1 already settled at $\vert0\rangle$. Then
   $H$ on qubit 0 gives

   $$
   \frac{(\cos\theta+\sin\theta)\,\vert00\rangle + (\cos\theta-\sin\theta)\,\vert10\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   so, using $(\cos\theta\pm\sin\theta)^2 = 1 \pm \sin 2\theta$,

   $$
   P(00) = \frac{1+\sin 2\theta}{2}, \qquad P(10) = \frac{1-\sin 2\theta}{2}, \qquad P(01) = P(11) = 0 .
   $$

   At $\theta = \pi/4$ the pair is $\vert\Phi^+\rangle$ and $\sin 2\theta = 1$,
   giving $P(00) = 1$: the protocol as derived, exact. At $\theta = \pi/8$,
   $\sin 2\theta = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, giving $P(00) \approx 0.854$ and
   $P(10) \approx 0.146$. Bob is wrong about one bit in seven, and no
   change to his decode circuit repairs it. The engine confirms both, and
   reports the pair's entanglement entropy at $\theta = \pi/8$ as $0.601$
   bits rather than the full $1$ bit.

   That is the comparison the question is after. Bob's success tracks the
   entanglement in the pair, not the superposition in Alice's qubit. Push
   to $\theta = 0$ and the pair is $\vert00\rangle$, with no entanglement
   at all, and the formula gives $P(00) = P(10) = \tfrac12$: Bob learns
   Alice's second bit for free, since qubit 1 is definitely $\vert0\rangle$
   in that case, and nothing whatsoever about her first. Entanglement is
   not a switch that is on or off. It is a quantity, and the protocol's
   fidelity degrades continuously with it.

   The wrong turn is expecting the failure to look like the lesson's
   entanglement-free frame, a flat $25\%$ on all four outcomes. That frame
   used $\vert+\rangle\otimes\vert0\rangle$, a different unentangled state,
   whose superposition on qubit 0 is what smears Bob's readout across all
   four outcomes. The $\theta = 0$ case here has no superposition either,
   so two of the four outcomes stay at zero. Two different product states,
   two different failure patterns, and neither carries any information
   about Alice's first bit. That is the sign that the missing ingredient in
   both cases is the same one, and it is not superposition.

   </details>

## Summary

- Alice applies one of four fixed gates, $I$, $X$, $Z$, or $Z$ then $X$,
  to her half of a pre-shared Bell pair, chosen by the two classical bits
  she wants to send, and transmits only that one qubit.
- The four resulting joint states are exactly the four Bell states (one up
  to an unobservable global phase), computed directly and shown to be
  mutually distinguishable.
- Bob's decode circuit, $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ then $H$ on qubit 0, is the
  exact inverse of the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ circuit that built the pair,
  and recovers both bits with certainty.
- Removing the entanglement (a product state whose qubit 0 still measures
  $50/50$ on its own) collapses Bob's result to a flat, uninformative $25\%$
  per outcome, a computed demonstration that entanglement, not mere
  superposition, is the resource superdense coding spends.
- No communication bound is violated: counting the Bell-pair-distributing
  qubit alongside the message qubit, two qubits of quantum communication
  carried two classical bits, which is Holevo's limit.

BB84 uses no entanglement to let two parties detect, rather than prevent,
anyone trying to read their qubits in transit.
