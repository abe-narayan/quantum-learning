## Motivation

Alice needs to send Bob two classical bits, and the channel between them
will carry a single qubit, once. Measured on its own, one qubit yields at
most one bit, so the plan looks dead on arrival.

What rescues it is a resource distributed before the message existed: a
shared <Term id="bell-states">Bell pair</Term>. Teleportation used that same resource, plus one CNOT,
one Hadamard, two measurements, and two classical bits, to move an unknown
qubit state. This lesson runs nearly the same circuit backwards, for the
opposite purpose. Instead of spending <Term id="entanglement">entanglement</Term> and two classical bits
to move a quantum state, Alice spends entanglement and one qubit to move
two classical bits, and Bob recovers them exactly.

Keep an eye on the bookkeeping. The claim is not that one qubit carries two
bits of information out of nowhere. Half the pair had to be delivered to
Bob at some earlier point, and that delivery is where the second bit's
worth of transmission was paid for.

## Intuition

Start from a Bell pair, held half by Alice and half by Bob. Alice's half,
on its own, looks uniformly random no matter what she does to it, a fact
this course established when computing <Term id="partial-trace">reduced states</Term> of entangled
systems. But the *joint* two-qubit state is one of only four
mutually distinguishable possibilities depending on which of four fixed
gates Alice applies to her one qubit. She picks the gate matching the two
bits she wants to send, then physically mails Bob her single qubit. Bob,
now holding both halves of the original pair, runs one circuit that reads
off both bits from the joint state with certainty. Nothing here is
approximate or probabilistic once Bob's qubit arrives: the four outcomes
are perfectly distinguishable, the same way the four Bell states always
have been.

## Mathematical Development

### The setup: a shared Bell pair

Exactly as in Quantum Teleportation, Alice and Bob share a Bell pair
prepared in advance, $H$ on qubit 0 then $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$:

### Alice encodes two bits into her one qubit

Alice wants to send two classical bits. She applies one of four
fixed gates to *her* qubit alone (qubit 0), chosen by the bits she wants to
send, and nothing at all to Bob's:

| Bits to send | Alice's gate | Resulting joint state |
|---|---|---|
| $00$ | $I$ (nothing) | $\vert\Phi^+\rangle$ |
| $01$ | $X$ | $\vert\Psi^+\rangle$ |
| $10$ | $Z$ | $\vert\Phi^-\rangle$ |
| $11$ | $Z$ then $X$ | $-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$ |

This is the four-row table Quantum Teleportation used for *Bob's*
correction, applied here by *Alice*, deliberately rather than as a
correction for a random measurement outcome. Computing all four with the
real engine:

### Bob decodes: the teleportation circuit, reversed

Bob now holds both qubits. Quantum Teleportation's mid-protocol step
applied $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ then $H$ on qubit 0 to *disentangle* a message
qubit from a Bell pair before measuring. Bob runs that same circuit here,
and because $H$ and $\text{CNOT}$ are each their own inverse, this is
the adjoint of the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ circuit that built the
Bell pair in the first place:

  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[0]} label="Decoded 00" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[1]} label="Decoded 01" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[2]} label="Decoded 10" />
  <QuantumStateDisplay state={decodedStates[3]} label="Decoded 11" />
</div>

Every one of the four decodes to the corresponding computational basis
state with probability 1 (the $11$ case lands on $-\vert11\rangle$, same
$100\%$ measurement probability, the global phase again unobservable). Bob
measures both qubits and reads off the two bits Alice sent, every
time, with no ambiguity.

Here is the whole protocol as one circuit. Step through Alice's four
possible messages below and watch only the middle of the circuit change:
zero, one, or two gates depending on which two bits she's sending. The
preparation at the start and Bob's decode at the end never move.

The first two gates are the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ preparation from the
section above. The last two gates before measurement, Bob's decode, are
$\text{CNOT}$-then-$H$: the same two gates as the preparation, in
the opposite order. That mirror-image relationship is why the
decode undoes the preparation regardless of how many gates Alice's
encoding step inserted in between.

### Why the pair has to be entangled, not merely in superposition

This only works because Alice's gate acts on a qubit that starts
*entangled* with Bob's, not merely in superposition. Replace the true Bell
pair with the unentangled product state $\vert+\rangle\otimes\vert0\rangle$
(same reduced state on qubit 0 alone, but qubit 1 no longer correlated with
it), leave Alice's gate at $I$ (message $00$), and run Bob's identical
decode circuit:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={noEntanglementDecoded} label="Bob's decode circuit applied to a product state instead of a Bell pair" />

Instead of landing on $\vert00\rangle$ with certainty, Bob gets a uniform
$25\%$ chance of each of the four outcomes: no information survives.
Superposition on Alice's qubit alone was never the resource; the
entanglement between the two qubits is.

## Physical Interpretation

No communication law is broken here. The <Term id="holevo-quantity">Holevo bound</Term> limits how much
classical information a *single* qubit can carry *on its own* to one bit;
superdense coding respects it once the accounting includes both qubits the
protocol uses. Distributing the Bell pair required sending one qubit (from
whoever prepared it to whichever of Alice or Bob did not have it), and the
message required a second, Alice's. Two qubits of quantum communication
carried two classical bits: one bit per qubit on average, which is what
Holevo demands.

What superdense coding buys is timing. The entanglement-distributing qubit
can be sent long before Alice knows what she wants to say, so at the moment
of the message only one additional qubit has to move.

<InsightBlock>
Superdense coding does not beat the one-bit-per-qubit bound. It moves half the cost earlier in time, to a moment when nobody yet knows what the message will be.
</InsightBlock>

## Worked Example

**Verify the $11$ row by hand: show that applying $Z$ then $X$ to Alice's
half of $\vert\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle+\vert11\rangle)$
gives $-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$, and that Bob's decode circuit recovers
$\vert11\rangle$.**

Apply $Z$ to qubit 0 first: $Z\vert0\rangle=\vert0\rangle$ and
$Z\vert1\rangle=-\vert1\rangle$, so
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle+\vert11\rangle)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle-\vert11\rangle)=\vert\Phi^-\rangle$.
Then apply $X$ to qubit 0: $X\vert0\rangle=\vert1\rangle$ and
$X\vert1\rangle=\vert0\rangle$, so
$\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert00\rangle-\vert11\rangle)\to\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert10\rangle-\vert01\rangle)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert10\rangle)=-\vert\Psi^-\rangle$,
matching `encodedStates[3]` above exactly.

For the decode, apply $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ to
$-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert10\rangle)$: the $\vert01\rangle$
term has control qubit $0$, so target qubit 1 is untouched, staying
$\vert01\rangle$; the $\vert10\rangle$ term has control qubit $1$, so
target flips, $\vert10\rangle\to\vert11\rangle$. Result:
$-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert01\rangle-\vert11\rangle)$. Now apply $H$ to qubit
0: on the $\vert0\rangle$-control terms, $H\vert0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert0\rangle+\vert1\rangle)$
contributes to $\vert01\rangle$; on the $\vert1\rangle$-control term,
$H\vert1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\vert0\rangle-\vert1\rangle)$ contributes
to $-\vert11\rangle$. Collecting terms exactly cancels every amplitude
except $-\vert11\rangle$, matching `decodedStates[3]` above: Bob measures
$11$ with certainty, up to the unobservable overall minus sign.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**"Alice sends two qubits."** She sends exactly one: her own half of the
pre-shared pair, after applying one of four single-qubit gates to it. Bob's
qubit never travels; it was already in his possession.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"This violates the Holevo bound, or lets Alice signal Bob
instantaneously."** Neither. Holevo's bound is respected once the
Bell-pair-distributing qubit is counted alongside Alice's message qubit,
two qubits of quantum communication for two classical bits. And the
message qubit still has to physically travel from Alice to Bob at ordinary
speed; nothing about entanglement changes that.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**"Bob can decode by measuring his qubit alone, without waiting for
Alice's."** Before Alice's qubit arrives, Bob holds only half of an
entangled pair; measuring it alone gives a uniformly random bit no matter
what gate Alice applied; see Bell States and Entanglement for why a
reduced single-qubit state of an entangled pair carries no information on
its own. The two bits only become readable once both qubits are back
together for the joint decode circuit.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Alice applies one of four fixed gates, $I$, $X$, $Z$, or $Z$ then $X$,
  to her half of a pre-shared Bell pair, chosen by the two classical bits
  she wants to send, and transmits only that one qubit.
- The four resulting joint states are exactly the four Bell states (one up
  to an unobservable global phase), computed directly and shown to be
  mutually distinguishable.
- Bob's decode circuit, $\text{CNOT}(0,1)$ then $H$ on qubit 0, is the
  exact inverse of the $H$-then-$\text{CNOT}$ circuit that built the pair,
  and recovers both bits with certainty.
- Removing the entanglement (a product state with the same single-qubit
  reduced state) collapses Bob's result to a flat, uninformative $25\%$
  per outcome, a computed demonstration that entanglement, not mere
  superposition, is the resource superdense coding spends.
- No communication bound is violated: counting the Bell-pair-distributing
  qubit alongside the message qubit, two qubits of quantum communication
  carried two classical bits, which is Holevo's limit.
