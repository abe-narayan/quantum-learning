## Motivation

Back in the first course, $H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$ was a small, striking
fact: two amplitudes that individually gave 50/50 probabilities combined,
through a second $H$, into a completely definite outcome. That was
interference on a single qubit. This lesson asks whether the same trick
scales up, and shows, with a fully worked circuit, that it does: uniform
randomness across *four* outcomes can be turned into one certain outcome,
using nothing but Hadamards and a phase gate.

## Intuition

Interference needs two things: multiple paths (amplitudes) contributing
to the same final outcome, and the freedom to arrange their phases so
they add constructively where you want a result and cancel where you
don't. A single qubit only has two paths to play with. Multiple qubits
give you exponentially more, and every quantum algorithm you'll
encounter in later courses is, at its core, an elaborate arrangement of
phases designed to make wrong answers cancel and the right answer
reinforce.

## Mathematical Development

### A uniform superposition

Apply $H$ to both qubits of $|00\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterFirstH} label="H ⊗ H applied to |00⟩" />

$$
(H\otimes H)|00\rangle = |+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)
$$

All four outcomes equally likely, and notably, this state is a plain
product state, $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, not entangled at all. Every
qubit here is doing its own independent single-qubit thing.

### Introduce a relative phase

Apply $Z$ to qubit 1 only. $Z$ leaves $|0\rangle$ alone and flips the
sign on $|1\rangle$; applied to qubit 1's $|+\rangle$ factor, this turns
it into $|-\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterPhase} label="Z on qubit 1: |+⟩ ⊗ |−⟩" />

$$
|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle-|01\rangle+|10\rangle-|11\rangle)
$$

Still a product state, still uniform *probabilities*, but the phases are
no longer all the same.

### Undo the superposition, and watch the phases collide

Apply $H\otimes H$ again:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterSecondH} label="H ⊗ H again: exactly |01⟩" />

$$
(H\otimes H)(|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle) = (H|+\rangle)\otimes(H|-\rangle) = |0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle = |01\rangle
$$

using the single-qubit facts $H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $H|-\rangle=|1\rangle$
from the last course, applied independently to each factor since this is
still a plain product state throughout. **All the probability that was
spread uniformly across four outcomes has concentrated onto exactly
one**, with certainty, not because any measurement narrowed things down,
but purely because the phases introduced by that single $Z$ gate caused
constructive interference on $|01\rangle$ and destructive interference
(exact cancellation) everywhere else.

### This didn't require entanglement, but interference can also act on entangled states

Notice every state in this derivation was a product state; interference
here is really just two independent copies of the single-qubit story
running in parallel. Interference on genuinely *entangled* states is also
possible and was already in front of you: the Bell-states lesson's
calculation $(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle=|\Phi^+\rangle$ is exactly this,
phases rearranging inside an entangled state, reproducing the same
correlated pattern rather than concentrating onto one outcome. Both are
"interference"; they differ in what structure the state has going in.

## Physical Interpretation

A circuit that ends in a superposition carries no information until it's
measured. The *only* way a circuit's design can influence what you're
likely to observe is by shaping which amplitudes reinforce and which
cancel before that final measurement. This is the single mechanism behind
every quantum algorithm's speedup: not "trying all answers at once and
picking the right one" (a common but misleading way to describe
superposition), but arranging the entire computation so that wrong
answers' amplitudes cancel and the right answer's amplitude survives.

## Worked Example

**What does $H\otimes H$, $Z$ on qubit 0 (instead of qubit 1), $H\otimes H$
produce, starting from $|00\rangle$?**

By the same reasoning with the roles of the two qubits swapped: $Z$ on
qubit 0 turns $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ into $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$,
and the second $H\otimes H$ gives $H|-\rangle\otimes H|+\rangle=|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=|10\rangle$.
Concentration still happens, on a different outcome, because the phase
was introduced on a different qubit.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming interference requires entanglement.** The main derivation in
this lesson is a product state from start to finish, full interference,
zero entanglement. They're independent concepts that can occur together
or separately.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Measuring partway through and expecting the same result.** If you
measured either qubit right after the $Z$ gate in the derivation above,
you'd destroy the superposition needed for the second $H\otimes H$ to
produce interference. You'd get a random definite outcome instead of the
guaranteed $|01\rangle$. Interference requires the amplitudes to stay
*unmeasured* until they've had a chance to combine.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $H\otimes H$, a $Z$ on one qubit, $H\otimes H$ again takes a uniform
  4-way superposition to a single definite outcome, purely through phase
  interference.
- This particular example needs no entanglement. It's two parallel
  copies of single-qubit interference.
- Interference is the general mechanism: circuits are designed so unwanted
  outcomes' amplitudes cancel and the desired outcome's amplitude survives
  until measurement.
