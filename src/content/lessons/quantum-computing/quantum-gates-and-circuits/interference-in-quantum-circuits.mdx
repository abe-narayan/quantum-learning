## The same trick, at four times the size

$H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$ was <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> on a single qubit. This lesson runs a
fully worked circuit that tries the same trick on *four* outcomes at
once, using nothing but Hadamards and one phase gate, and reads the
result off the engine rather than asserting it.

That pattern is worth recognizing now, because every algorithm in the two
courses ahead has the same three-part shape. Spread amplitude over many
possibilities, arrange phases so the wrong ones cancel, then collapse the
superposition and read off what survived.

## Multiple paths, and the freedom to set their phases

Interference needs two things: multiple paths (<Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term>) contributing
to the same final outcome, and the freedom to arrange their phases so
they add constructively where you want a result and cancel where you
don't. A single qubit only has two paths to play with. Multiple qubits
give you exponentially more, and every quantum algorithm you'll
encounter in later courses is, at its core, an elaborate arrangement of
phases designed to make wrong answers cancel and the right answer
reinforce.

## Spread, phase, then collapse back down

The circuit for the whole derivation below is: $H$ on both qubits, then
$Z$ on qubit 1 only, then $H$ on both qubits again.

### A uniform superposition

Apply $H$ to both qubits of $|00\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterFirstH} label="H ⊗ H applied to |00⟩" />

$$
(H\otimes H)|00\rangle = |+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)
$$

All four outcomes equally likely, and notably, this state is a plain
product state, $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, not <Term id="entanglement">entangled</Term> at all. Every
qubit here is doing its own independent single-qubit thing.

### Introduce a relative phase

Apply $Z$ to qubit 1 only. $Z$ leaves $|0\rangle$ alone and flips the
sign on $|1\rangle$; applied to qubit 1's $|+\rangle$ factor, this turns
it into $|-\rangle$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterPhase} label="Z on qubit 1: |+⟩ ⊗ |−⟩" />

$$
|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle-|01\rangle+|10\rangle-|11\rangle)
$$

Still a product state, still uniform *probabilities*, but the phases are
no longer all the same.

Everything so far is bookkeeping: a product state, uniform probabilities,
one misaligned phase. The next step is where that phase turns into
something a detector could report. Commit to an answer before reading it.

### Undo the superposition, and watch the phases collide

Apply $H\otimes H$ again:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={afterSecondH} label="H ⊗ H again: exactly |01⟩" />

$$
(H\otimes H)(|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle) = (H|+\rangle)\otimes(H|-\rangle) = |0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle = |01\rangle
$$

using the single-qubit facts $H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $H|-\rangle=|1\rangle$
from the last course, applied independently to each factor since this is
still a plain product state throughout. **All the probability that was
spread uniformly across four outcomes has concentrated onto one**, with
certainty. No measurement narrowed anything down. The phases introduced by
a single $Z$ gate produced constructive interference on $|01\rangle$ and
exact cancellation everywhere else.

### This didn't require entanglement, but interference can also act on entangled states

Every state in this derivation was a product state, so the interference
here is two independent copies of the single-qubit story running side by
side. Interference on *entangled* states also happens, and you have already
seen a case: the Bell-states lesson's $(H\otimes H)|\Phi^+\rangle=|\Phi^+\rangle$
is phases rearranging inside an entangled state, reproducing the same
correlated pattern rather than concentrating onto one outcome. Both are
interference. They differ in what structure the state had going in.

## Shaping which outcomes reinforce

A circuit that ends in a <Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> carries no information until it's
measured. The *only* way a circuit's design can influence what you're
likely to observe is by shaping which amplitudes reinforce and which
cancel before that final measurement. This is the single mechanism behind
every quantum algorithm's speedup: not "trying all answers at once and
picking the right one" (a common but misleading way to describe
superposition), but arranging the entire computation so that wrong
answers' amplitudes cancel and the right answer's amplitude survives.

## The same circuit with the Z moved

**What does $H\otimes H$, $Z$ on qubit 0 (instead of qubit 1), $H\otimes H$
produce, starting from $|00\rangle$?**

By the same reasoning with the roles of the two qubits swapped: $Z$ on
qubit 0 turns $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ into $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$,
and the second $H\otimes H$ gives $H|-\rangle\otimes H|+\rangle=|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=|10\rangle$.
Concentration still happens, on a different outcome, because the phase
was introduced on a different qubit.

## Interference and entanglement are independent

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming interference requires entanglement.** The main derivation in
this lesson is a product state from start to finish, full interference,
zero entanglement. They're independent concepts that can occur together
or separately.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Measuring partway through and expecting the same result.** If you
measured either qubit right after the $Z$ gate in the derivation above,
you'd destroy the superposition needed for the second $H\otimes H$ to
produce interference. You'd get a random definite outcome instead of the
guaranteed $|01\rangle$. Interference requires the amplitudes to stay
*unmeasured* until they've had a chance to combine.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Starting from $|00\rangle$, predict the result of: $H\otimes H$, then
   $Z$ on *both* qubits, then $H\otimes H$ again.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|11\rangle$, with certainty.

   The circuit stays a product state throughout, so run each qubit's story
   separately. Qubit 0 goes $|0\rangle \to |+\rangle \to |-\rangle \to |1\rangle$,
   using $Z|+\rangle = |-\rangle$ and $H|-\rangle = |1\rangle$. Qubit 1
   does exactly the same. Tensoring the two:

   $$
   |00\rangle \to |{+}{+}\rangle \to |{-}{-}\rangle \to |11\rangle
   $$

   Written out in the four-term form, the middle state is
   $\tfrac12(|00\rangle - |01\rangle - |10\rangle + |11\rangle)$, which the
   engine confirms, along with the final probabilities $[0,0,0,1]$.

   The pattern across the three variants is worth collecting, because it is
   the whole lesson in one line. A $Z$ on qubit 1 concentrates onto
   $|01\rangle$; a $Z$ on qubit 0 concentrates onto $|10\rangle$; a $Z$ on
   both concentrates onto $|11\rangle$; and no $Z$ at all returns
   $|00\rangle$, since $H^2 = I$ on each wire. The output label is a
   readout of *which qubits got a phase*, one bit per qubit. That is not a
   coincidence of small cases. It is the two-qubit instance of the pattern
   the Deutsch-Jozsa and Bernstein-Vazirani algorithms run at $n$ qubits:
   write a phase pattern into a uniform superposition, then apply
   $H^{\otimes n}$ to read the pattern back out as a bit string.

   The wrong turn is expecting cancellation to *shrink* the state, and
   answering that with a phase on both qubits everything cancels and
   nothing is left. Amplitude is conserved, always: the second $H\otimes H$
   is unitary, so the four probabilities still sum to 1. Interference moves
   probability between outcomes; it never destroys it.

   </details>

2. Explain why applying $Z$ to qubit 1 in the main derivation, rather
   than doing nothing, is what causes concentration onto $|01\rangle$
   specifically rather than back onto $|00\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $H$ is its own inverse, so with no $Z$ the two $H\otimes H$
   layers cancel exactly and the state returns to $|00\rangle$, engine
   confirmed. The $Z$ is the only thing standing between them, and its job
   is to change *which* superposition the second layer is undoing.

   Follow qubit 1 alone, since that is the only wire the $Z$ touches. After
   the first $H$ it holds $|+\rangle$, an equal sum with both amplitudes
   positive. $Z$ flips the sign of the $|1\rangle$ amplitude, producing
   $|-\rangle$: the same two probabilities, a different relative phase. Now
   the second $H$ arrives. $H$ sends $|+\rangle$ to $|0\rangle$ and
   $|-\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$, because it adds the two amplitudes to make
   the new $|0\rangle$ amplitude and subtracts them to make the new
   $|1\rangle$ amplitude. Same-sign amplitudes reinforce in the sum and
   cancel in the difference; opposite-sign amplitudes do the reverse. So
   the sign $Z$ planted decides which of the two ways the final $H$ can
   go, and qubit 1 lands on $|1\rangle$ instead of $|0\rangle$. Qubit 0,
   untouched, still lands on $|0\rangle$, giving $|01\rangle$.

   The general moral: a phase is invisible where it is introduced and
   decisive where paths recombine. Immediately after the $Z$, all four
   probabilities are still $\tfrac14$, so no measurement at that instant
   distinguishes the with-$Z$ circuit from the without-$Z$ one. The
   difference only becomes real when the second $H\otimes H$ brings the
   amplitudes back together and forces them to add or subtract.

   The tempting wrong answer is that $Z$ "made $|01\rangle$ more likely" by
   somehow favouring it directly. It did not: $Z$ is diagonal, so it never
   moves probability between basis states at all, and its output has the
   same distribution as its input. All the redistribution is done by the
   final $H\otimes H$. $Z$ only writes down the instruction that the final
   layer then carries out.

   </details>

3. Is $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, on its own, "interfering"? Why or why
   not: what has to happen before interference occurs?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>A defensible answer</summary>

   There is no single correct verdict here, so the question is marked on
   what the answer commits to and why. One position worked in full, then
   the defensible alternatives, then the answers that are simply wrong.

   The position worth defending: no, not on its own. $|{+}{+}\rangle$ is a
   state, and interference is something that happens to amplitudes when a
   later operation brings distinct paths back to the same final basis
   label. Nothing in the four amplitudes $\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12,\tfrac12$
   is adding to or cancelling anything; each basis state carries exactly one
   amplitude and there is nothing for it to combine with. What has to happen
   is a subsequent operation that maps two or more basis states onto a shared
   outcome, at which point their amplitudes add as complex numbers and their
   relative phases start to matter. In this lesson that operation is the
   second $H\otimes H$, and the proof that it is the operative step is the
   comparison in question 2: the same $|{+}{+}\rangle$ can end at
   $|00\rangle$ or, after a $Z$, at $|01\rangle$. If the state alone were
   already interfering, its future could not depend on what comes next.

   Equally defensible. You can say yes, on the grounds that
   $|+\rangle$ *is* what came out of an earlier interference, $H$ acting on
   $|0\rangle$, and that "a state in superposition" is exactly the standing
   condition for interference in the double-slit sense: the electron is
   already going both ways, and the screen only reveals it. That is a
   coherent use of the word, and physicists do speak this way. You can also
   take the operational line: interference is defined by observable
   consequences, so the honest answer is that the question is not settled
   until a measurement basis is chosen, since $|{+}{+}\rangle$ shows no
   interference in the X basis and full interference in the Z basis after
   an $H$. Any of these earns credit provided it names what would have to be
   true for the verdict to change.

   What a bad answer looks like. "Yes, because it is entangled" is wrong
   twice: $|{+}{+}\rangle$ is a plain product state, and entanglement is not
   what interference requires, which is this lesson's headline. "No, because
   there is no measurement yet" inverts the physics: measurement is what
   *ends* the opportunity for interference, not what creates it, which is why
   the lesson's warning callout says measuring after the $Z$ destroys the
   effect. And any answer that leans on "the qubit is secretly 0 and 1 at the
   same time and the circuit tries both" is the parallel-universe story the
   lesson explicitly rejects: a circuit that merely tried all four
   possibilities would leave them all at $\tfrac14$ forever, which is exactly
   the state you start from and exactly not the state you end on.

   </details>

## Summary

- $H\otimes H$, a $Z$ on one qubit, $H\otimes H$ again takes a uniform
  4-way superposition to a single definite outcome, purely through phase
  interference.
- This example needs no entanglement. It is two parallel copies of
  single-qubit interference.
- Interference is the general mechanism: circuits are designed so unwanted
  outcomes' amplitudes cancel and the desired outcome's amplitude survives
  until measurement.
