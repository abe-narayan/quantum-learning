## Motivation

Every multi-qubit operation so far has been described in prose and
equations. Real circuits — the ones you'll see in papers, textbooks, and
every quantum SDK — are drawn as diagrams. Learning to read one fluently
is a small, mechanical skill; getting the *ordering convention* backwards
is the single most common way people misread a circuit, so this lesson
spends real time on that specifically.

## Intuition

A circuit diagram is a small piece of sheet music: one horizontal line
per qubit ("wire"), time flowing left to right, and symbols placed on the
wires telling you what happens to that qubit at that moment. Reading a
circuit is reading a story of the state evolving, one column at a time.

## Mathematical Development

### The conventions

- **Wires**, read top to bottom, are qubits 0, 1, 2, ... — matching this
  platform's ordering convention exactly, so the topmost wire is always
  qubit 0.
- **Boxes** on a wire (labeled $H$, $X$, $S$, etc.) are single-qubit
  gates, identical to the ones from the last course.
- **A filled dot** connected by a vertical line to a $\oplus$ symbol (or
  to another gate box) is a **controlled operation**: the dot marks the
  control qubit, the connected symbol marks the target and what happens
  to it. The next lesson covers this in full.
- **A meter symbol** (⏱-like gauge icon, conventionally) marks a
  measurement of that wire at that point in time.
- **A blank wire** at a given time step means: nothing happens to that
  qubit there — which is not "no operation," it's *implicitly the
  identity gate*, tensored in with whatever the other wires are doing.

### From diagram to operator expression

A circuit is read left to right, but the corresponding operator product —
exactly as established in the last course's circuit lesson — is written
and multiplied **right to left**: the leftmost box in the diagram is the
rightmost (first-applied) operator in the product. If qubit 0 gets an $H$
while qubit 1 does nothing, that single column is the operator
$H\otimes I$ (not $I\otimes H$ — order in the tensor product matches
wire order, qubit 0 first). A second column applying $\text{CNOT}$ across
both wires, written as its own two-qubit operator, composes on the left:

$$
\text{full circuit} = \text{CNOT} \cdot (H\otimes I)
$$

This is exactly the same right-to-left composition rule from the last
course's capstone lesson — nothing new about *how* operators combine, only
new about *reading a picture* instead of a written expression.

## Physical Interpretation

A circuit diagram is a complete recipe: given it and an input state,
there's exactly one predicted output state, obtained by working through
the columns left to right and multiplying operators right to left. Two
circuits that look different on paper but reduce to the same operator
product are physically identical — this is the basis for "circuit
identities," used later in this course to simplify or rewrite circuits
without changing what they compute.

## Worked Example

**A circuit has $X$ on qubit 1 in the first column, then $\text{CNOT}$
with qubit 1 as control and qubit 0 as target in the second column.
Predict the output starting from $|00\rangle$.**

Column 1: $X$ on qubit 1 turns $|00\rangle$ into $|01\rangle$ (qubit 0
untouched, qubit 1 flips). Column 2: $\text{CNOT}$ with control = qubit 1
(which is now 1) flips the target, qubit 0: $|01\rangle \to |11\rangle$.
Final state: $|11\rangle$, with certainty (no superposition was ever
introduced, so this circuit is really just doing classical bit-flip logic
here).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Reading the operator product in diagram order.** The diagram reads left
to right; the operator product, when you write it out, reads right to
left. Confusing these flips which gate you think acted first.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the implicit identity on untouched wires.** A blank space on
a wire during a column that has gates elsewhere is not "nothing" in the
operator expression — it's an explicit $I$ factor in that column's tensor
product, needed to keep the dimensions consistent.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A circuit has $H$ on qubit 0 and $H$ on qubit 1 in the same column.
   Write the operator for that column as a tensor product.
2. Write the full operator expression (as a product of tensor products)
   for a 3-column circuit: column 1 is $H$ on qubit 0 only; column 2 is
   $X$ on qubit 1 only; column 3 is $\text{CNOT}$(0,1).
3. Two circuits are proposed for the same task; one has 2 columns, the
   other has 3, but a friend claims they're equivalent. What would you
   need to check to confirm or refute this?
4. Starting from $|10\rangle$, predict the output of: $H$ on qubit 0, then
   $\text{CNOT}$(0, 1). Work it out by hand, then compare to the "Bell
   state" derivation in the next lesson once you get there.

## Summary

- Wires are qubits (qubit 0 on top); time flows left to right; boxes are
  gates; a dot-to-$\oplus$ connection is a controlled operation; blank
  space means an implicit identity.
- A circuit's diagram order (left to right) and its operator-product order
  (right to left) are inverses of each other — the same rule from the
  last course, just applied to pictures.
- Each column of simultaneous single-qubit gates is a tensor product of
  those gates (with identity filling in untouched wires).
