Everything in the last course lived on one qubit's Bloch sphere. Real computation needs several qubits working together, and "several qubits" is not "the single-qubit thing, twice."
</LessonHook>

## Four possibilities, held all at once

A classical 2-bit register needs 2 bits to describe: which one of
$\{00,01,10,11\}$ it is in. A quantum 2-qubit register has to describe a
*superposition* over all four of those possibilities at once, which takes 4
complex <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term>, not 2. This lesson derives where that "4" comes from,
and it does not stop at 4. By the end you will see why $n$ qubits need
$2^n$ amplitudes, the one fact that makes quantum computers hard to
simulate classically and (in later courses) part of why they can be
powerful.

## Two spheres, and what combining them should cost

Think of two independent qubits as two independent Bloch spheres. Fully
describing "where qubit A is" takes its own $(\theta,\varphi)$, and
"where qubit B is" takes another, independent, pair. Combining them
shouldn't lose any of that information: whatever mathematical operation
combines two qubit states needs to preserve both spheres' worth of detail
*and* leave room for something classical bits could never do, correlation
between them that isn't just "A's state" plus "B's state" separately. The
**<Term id="tensor-product">tensor product</Term>**, written $\otimes$, is that operation.

## Every amplitude times every amplitude

### Defining the tensor product

For two single-qubit kets $|a\rangle = a_0|0\rangle + a_1|1\rangle$ and
$|b\rangle = b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle$, their tensor product is the
4-component vector:

$$
|a\rangle \otimes |b\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}a_0\\a_1\end{pmatrix} \otimes \begin{pmatrix}b_0\\b_1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a_0 b_0\\a_0 b_1\\a_1 b_0\\a_1 b_1\end{pmatrix}
$$

Every entry of the first vector multiplies every entry of the second.
That is what makes the result 4-dimensional from two
2-dimensional inputs ($2\times2=4$), rather than 4-dimensional from
"stacking" them ($2+2=4$ would give the wrong structure entirely; stacking
loses the ability to represent correlations).

### Basis states tensor by concatenating labels

Apply the definition to $|0\rangle=(1,0)$ and $|1\rangle=(0,1)$:

$$
|0\rangle \otimes |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\cdot0\\1\cdot1\\0\cdot0\\0\cdot1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} = |01\rangle
$$

The result is the <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term> state whose label is the
two input labels written side by side. This is a useful shorthand you'll
lean on constantly: $|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle = |ij\rangle$, and you
rarely need to write out the column-vector arithmetic by hand once you
trust this rule.

These are different states, confirming the prediction above: $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$
and $|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$ are as distinct as the classical strings

### Combining superpositions: cross terms

The tensor product is *bilinear*, it distributes over addition in each
argument, so combining two states that are each already superpositions
produces every pairwise cross term:

$$
(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)\otimes(\gamma|0\rangle+\delta|1\rangle) = \alpha\gamma|00\rangle + \alpha\delta|01\rangle + \beta\gamma|10\rangle + \beta\delta|11\rangle
$$

All four terms are generally nonzero even if you started with two
"simple" single-qubit superpositions. This is the mechanism, not yet the
full story, behind why multi-qubit states can look complicated fast.

### Normalization survives tensoring

If $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ are each <Term id="normalization">normalized</Term>
($\langle a|a\rangle=\langle b|b\rangle=1$), is $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$
automatically normalized too? Compute its norm using the inner product
from Dirac Notation:

$$
\langle a\otimes b|a\otimes b\rangle = \sum_{i,j}|a_ib_j|^2 = \left(\sum_i|a_i|^2\right)\left(\sum_j|b_j|^2\right) = \langle a|a\rangle\langle b|b\rangle = 1\cdot1 = 1
$$

Yes. Tensoring two normalized states always gives a normalized state, so
you never need a separate renormalization step just because you combined
two qubits.

### Dimension growth and the qubit-ordering convention

Tensoring $n$ single qubits together gives a $2^n$-dimensional state
vector: each additional qubit *doubles* the dimension rather than adding a
fixed amount to it. Three qubits need 8 amplitudes, ten need 1024.

Doubling is easy to say and hard to feel, so here is what it costs in
memory. The bars plot $\log_2$ of the amplitude count at evenly spaced
qubit counts (5, 10, 15, ..., 50), so a straight line means exponential
growth, and the caption on each bar is the storage a double-precision
state vector would need, computed by the same `stateVectorMemoryBytes`
the platform's simulator-cost problems use.

Each bar is exactly five units taller than the one before it, so the tops
form a straight line: that is the point, since the cost is exponential in
the qubit count, with no bend anywhere to grow out of. Thirty qubits fit
in a large workstation's memory. Forty need a serious cluster. Fifty need
about 18 petabytes, and nobody has found a general way around that. This is
why "just simulate it classically" stops being an answer at a few dozen
qubits. The same $2^n$ is the raw resource multi-qubit algorithms will
later try to exploit.

One convention needs to be fixed and used consistently everywhere from
here on: **qubit 0 is the leftmost (most significant) label in a
multi-qubit ket.** For three qubits, $|q_0q_1q_2\rangle$, so
$|100\rangle$ has qubit 0 equal to 1 and qubits 1, 2 equal to 0. This
matches how the simulator engine indexes qubits, and it's the convention
every lesson, test, and future circuit diagram on this platform will use.

## Another postulate, not a consequence

That composite systems are described by the tensor product of their
parts' state spaces is itself a postulate of quantum mechanics, not
something derivable from the single-qubit postulates you already know.
It's an experimentally confirmed fact about how nature composes systems,
and everything about multi-qubit computing follows from taking it
seriously, including a consequence the next lesson is built entirely
around: **not every vector in this combined space can be written as a
tensor product of two single-qubit states.**

## Tensoring plus with minus

**Compute $|+\rangle \otimes |-\rangle$**, where
$|\pm\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)$.

Using the bilinear expansion with $\alpha=\beta=\gamma=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
$\delta=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$:

$$
|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle = \frac12|00\rangle - \frac12|01\rangle + \frac12|10\rangle - \frac12|11\rangle
$$

Each coefficient has magnitude $\frac12$, so every one of the four
outcomes is equally likely if measured. That makes sense, since $|+\rangle$
and $|-\rangle$ are each individually 50/50 in the computational basis,
and the qubits here are independent: nothing in this construction has
correlated them.

## Multiply amplitudes, not probabilities

<Callout type="warning">
**Multiplying probabilities instead of amplitudes.** It's tempting to
combine "50% this, 50% that" intuitively and skip the amplitude algebra,
but relative *phases* between terms only show up correctly if you
multiply amplitudes first and take $|\cdot|^2$ afterward, not the other
way around. This matters enormously once gates introduce phases.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Getting the factor order backwards.** $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle=|01\rangle$
is not the same state as $|1\rangle\otimes|0\rangle=|10\rangle$. Always
track which ket is qubit 0.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $|1\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ explicitly as a 4-component vector.
   Then compute $|+\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$ the same way. Which components
   do the two column vectors share, which differ, and what does each state
   predict for a measurement of qubit 0 on its own?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Feed each pair into the definition, every entry of the first vector
   times every entry of the second:

   $$
   |1\rangle\otimes|+\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\cdot1\\0\cdot1\\1\cdot1\\1\cdot1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{|10\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   $$
   |+\rangle\otimes|1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot0\\1\cdot1\\1\cdot0\\1\cdot1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{|01\rangle+|11\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   The two vectors agree in slot 1 (the $|00\rangle$ amplitude, zero in
   both) and in slot 4 (the $|11\rangle$ amplitude, $1/\sqrt2$ in both).
   They disagree in slots 2 and 3: the first state puts its second
   $1/\sqrt2$ on $|10\rangle$, the second puts it on $|01\rangle$. Two of
   four components differ, which is enough to make them orthogonal states,
   not merely differently written ones.

   The measurement question is where the difference stops being
   bookkeeping. In $|1\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ every surviving term starts
   with the label 1, so measuring qubit 0 returns 1 with probability
   $\tfrac12+\tfrac12 = 1$, a certainty. In $|+\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$ the
   surviving labels are $01$ and $11$, so qubit 0 comes out 0 half the time
   and 1 half the time. The certain qubit and the coin-flip qubit have
   traded places, exactly as the written order says they should.

   The wrong turn is treating $\otimes$ like ordinary multiplication and
   assuming it commutes, which it does not. What makes this seductive is
   that both states really do contain "one definite qubit and one
   $|+\rangle$ qubit", so they feel like the same physical situation
   described in either order. They are not: the labels record *which
   physical wire* is which, and a laboratory that measures wire 0 gets a
   guaranteed 1 in one case and a fair coin in the other. If you ever need
   to convert between them, that takes a real operation, the SWAP gate, and
   SWAP costs three CNOTs.

   </details>

2. Compute $|{+i}\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$, where
   $|{+i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   |{+i}\rangle\otimes|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1\\1\cdot0\\i\cdot1\\i\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\i\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{|00\rangle + i|10\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   Normalization survives, as the lesson proved it must:
   $\left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 + \left|\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \tfrac12+\tfrac12 = 1$.
   The four measurement probabilities are $\tfrac12, 0, \tfrac12, 0$.

   The slot the $i$ lands in is the whole exercise. It comes from qubit
   0's $|1\rangle$ amplitude, and qubit 1 is definitely $|0\rangle$, so the
   basis label is qubit 0's digit followed by qubit 1's digit: $10$, not
   $01$. Writing $i|01\rangle$ is the standard slip, and it is seductive
   because $|{+i}\rangle$ is written first while its interesting amplitude
   sits second, so the eye wants to pair "second thing" with "second
   digit". Concatenate labels instead of counting positions and the
   ambiguity disappears.

   One more thing worth noticing: that $i$ is not a global phase you may
   discard. It is a *relative* phase between the $|00\rangle$ and
   $|10\rangle$ branches, and it is what distinguishes this state from
   $|+\rangle\otimes|0\rangle$, which has the same four probabilities. A
   computational-basis measurement cannot tell the two apart; putting an
   $H$ on qubit 0 first can, since $H$ turns $|+\rangle$ into $|0\rangle$
   but sends $|{+i}\rangle$ to a state that is still an even split.

   </details>

3. How many amplitudes does a 5-qubit state vector have? A 20-qubit one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $2^5 = 32$ amplitudes for 5 qubits, and $2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$ for 20.

   The trap is $2n$: 10 and 40. That answer counts the amplitudes needed to
   describe each qubit *separately*, two per qubit, and it is the right
   count for a product state. It is the wrong count for a general state,
   because the general state also has to carry every correlation between
   the qubits, and there are exponentially many patterns of correlation to
   record. The dimension multiplies as qubits are added rather than adding,
   which is what the tensor product's "every entry times every entry" rule
   does.

   Attaching a unit to the numbers makes the difference concrete. Stored in
   double-precision complex numbers, 16 bytes each, the 5-qubit vector is
   $32 \times 16 = 512$ bytes, small enough to sit in a CPU's registers and
   caches. The 20-qubit vector is $1{,}048{,}576 \times 16 = 16{,}777{,}216$
   bytes, about 17 MB, still ordinary but now a real array in memory. Add
   another 20 qubits and the same formula gives about 18 TB. The bar chart
   above plots exactly this, and the straight line of bar tops is the point:
   the cost has no bend anywhere to grow out of.

   </details>

4. Verify by direct calculation that
   $\langle 01|(|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle) = 1$ and
   $\langle 00|(|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle) = 0$, using the inner product
   from Dirac Notation.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   First write the state as a column vector. From the lesson's worked
   expansion, $|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle = (0,1,0,0)^\mathsf{T} = |01\rangle$.
   The two bras are the conjugate transposes of the corresponding basis
   columns, $\langle 01| = (0,1,0,0)$ and $\langle 00| = (1,0,0,0)$, with
   conjugation doing nothing here because every entry is real.

   $$
   \langle 01|(|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle) = (0,1,0,0)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} = 0\cdot0 + 1\cdot1 + 0\cdot0 + 0\cdot0 = 1
   $$

   $$
   \langle 00|(|0\rangle\otimes|1\rangle) = (1,0,0,0)\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} = 1\cdot0 + 0\cdot1 + 0\cdot0 + 0\cdot0 = 0
   $$

   Only one product term is ever nonzero, because a basis bra picks out one
   slot of the column and discards the rest. That is the general fact these
   two calculations are instances of: the four two-qubit computational
   basis states are orthonormal, $\langle ij|kl\rangle = \delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}$,
   which is precisely why $|\langle ij|\psi\rangle|^2$ is a probability and
   why the four probabilities sum to 1.

   Two habits go wrong here. The first is reading $\langle 01|$ as somehow
   "the state $|01\rangle$ again" and expecting the answer to be a vector.
   A bra times a ket is a *number*, an overlap; a ket times a bra would be a
   $4\times4$ matrix. The second is forgetting the complex conjugate in the
   bra. It costs nothing on this example and everything on the previous
   question's state: $\langle {+i}0|({+i}\otimes 0)$ comes out to
   $\overline{(1/\sqrt2)}\cdot(1/\sqrt2) + \overline{(i/\sqrt2)}\cdot(i/\sqrt2) = \tfrac12+\tfrac12 = 1$
   only because the conjugate turns $i \cdot i$ into $(-i)\cdot i = +1$.
   Without it you would get $\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$ and conclude that a
   perfectly good normalized state has zero length.

   </details>

## Summary

- $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ multiplies every amplitude of $|a\rangle$ by
  every amplitude of $|b\rangle$; basis states tensor by concatenating
  labels, $|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle=|ij\rangle$.
- Tensoring two normalized states always yields a normalized state.
- $n$ qubits need $2^n$ amplitudes: exponential growth is structural, not
  incidental.
- Qubit 0 is the leftmost label in a multi-qubit ket, everywhere on this
  platform.
