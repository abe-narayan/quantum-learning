<LessonHook eyebrow="One word is load-bearing">
Two pieces are on the table. For a 2-qubit pure state, a reduced state's mixedness is equivalent to entanglement, and von Neumann entropy quantifies mixedness. Put them together and you have a number that measures entanglement. The word "pure" in the first piece is load-bearing, and this lesson shows what breaks without it.
</LessonHook>

## Composing two earlier results

Lesson 5 proved that a reduced state's mixedness is equivalent to
entanglement for 2-qubit pure states, and Lesson 6 built von Neumann
entropy to quantify mixedness. Composing the two gives **entanglement
entropy**: a single number, in bits, saying how entangled a pure
bipartite state is.

Both halves of that sentence deserve scrutiny, and one of them is a trap.
"How entangled" implies a graded quantity rather than a yes/no flag, which
turns out to be right. "Pure bipartite state" looks like a technical
precondition that could probably be relaxed later, which turns out to be
wrong. Drop it and the formula reports entanglement for a state that has
none, and the size of the error is worth predicting before you see it.

## The definition, then the case that breaks it

### The definition

For a pure 2-qubit state $|\psi\rangle_{AB}$, its **entanglement
entropy** is defined as the <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">von Neumann entropy</Term> of either reduced state:

$$
E(|\psi\rangle) = S(\rho_A) = S(\rho_B)
$$

The two are always equal for a pure bipartite state, and for the 2-qubit
case this course works in that is already settled rather than borrowed.
Lesson 5's identity gave
$1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=1-\text{Tr}(\rho_B^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$, so the two
reduced states have the same purity; and a $2\times2$ density matrix's
eigenvalue pair is $\left(1\pm\sqrt{2\,\text{Tr}(\rho^2)-1}\right)/2$,
fixed by the purity alone. Same purity, same eigenvalues, same entropy.
(In any dimension it follows instead from $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ sharing
their nonzero eigenvalues, a standard consequence of the <Term id="schmidt-decomposition">Schmidt
decomposition</Term>; `entanglementEntropy`'s tests check the equality
directly for several states either way.) `entanglementEntropy` in `entanglement.ts`
computes $S(\rho_A)$ specifically: `pureStateDensityMatrix`, then
`reducedDensityMatrixQubit0`, then `vonNeumannEntropy`, composing three
pieces this course has already built and tested individually.

### Computed for the states this course has used

$$
E(|00\rangle) = 0 \qquad E(|{+}0\rangle) = 0 \qquad E(|\Phi^+\rangle) = 1 \text{ bit} \qquad E(|\psi(\theta)\rangle) = -\cos^2\theta\log_2\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta\log_2\sin^2\theta
$$

Product states give 0 (Lesson 5's theorem: pure reduced state implies zero
entropy). Bell states give 1 bit (maximally mixed reduced state). The
partially entangled family
$|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$
interpolates continuously between the two, since Lesson 4 already found
$\rho_A(\theta)=\text{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)$. So this is a
graded measure rather than a binary flag, and it increases monotonically
as $\theta$ moves from $0$ toward $\pi/4$ (checked in
`entanglement.test.ts`). Plotted across the family, the climb toward
$\pi/4$ and the mirrored descent back to $0$ by $\pi/2$ are visible rather
than asserted:

### Dropping "pure": the I/4 test case

Apply the same formula, $S(\rho_A)$, to a *mixed* 2-qubit global state and
see what it reports. Take $\rho_{AB}=I/4$, the maximally mixed state on two
qubits, which factors as $(I/2)\otimes(I/2)$: a product of two independent
qubits, with zero entanglement by construction.

That is why the definition above required the global state to be pure. (2 bits is out of reach for a different reason: S(ρ_A) is a single qubit's entropy, capped at 1 bit. Its reduced state, computed by the same <Term id="partial-trace">partial trace</Term> as always, is
$\rho_A=\text{Tr}_B(I/4)=I/2$ (verified against the engine), giving
$S(\rho_A)=1$ bit: the same value as a maximally entangled Bell state.
The formula has just reported maximal entanglement for a state with none.

### Diagnosing the failure

$S(\rho_A)$, computed from a mixed global state, conflates two different
sources of uncertainty:

1. Uncertainty about qubit A *because it is entangled with qubit B*. This
   is the entanglement you wanted to measure.
2. Uncertainty about qubit A *because the preparation was already a
   classical mixture*. This is what happened with $I/4$, where the
   mixedness came from preparing each qubit independently as a mixed
   state, with no entanglement anywhere.

For a pure global state, source 2 cannot occur. There is no leftover
global uncertainty to confuse with entanglement, which is what made
Lesson 5's proof work. For a mixed global state, the two sources add
together in $S(\rho_A)$, and $\rho_A$ alone does not contain enough
information to separate them again.

The limitation is structural, deep in how the quantity is defined, which is why a real
measure of mixed-state entanglement (the mixed-state Wootters concurrence,
mentioned but not implemented in the next lesson) needs substantially more
machinery than "take the reduced entropy."

## When no other source of mixedness can confuse it

Entanglement entropy is the right tool when there is no independent source
of mixedness to confuse it with, and that is guaranteed only when the full
system starts in a definite, known pure state. The moment the global state
carries classical uncertainty of its own, reduced entropy stops tracking
entanglement specifically, as the $I/4$ counterexample shows.

## Entropy of a ninety-ten split

**Compute the entanglement entropy of $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.9}|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.1}|11\rangle$,
using Lesson 5's identity as a shortcut.**

Lesson 5 gives $\rho_A=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ directly from
$|a|^2=0.9,|d|^2=0.1$ (no off-diagonal terms here since $b=c=0$). This is
already diagonal, so (Lesson 6) its entropy is just the Shannon entropy
of $(0.9,0.1)$: $E=-0.9\log_2(0.9)-0.1\log_2(0.1)\approx0.469$ bits,
well above 0 (it's entangled) but well below 1 bit (less than maximally
entangled), consistent with its lopsided $90\%/10\%$ amplitude split.

## The mixed global state that reports a large number

<Callout type="warning">
**Computing $S(\rho_A)$ for a mixed global state and calling it
"entanglement entropy."** As the $I/4$ counterexample shows, this can
report large values for states with zero entanglement. Entanglement
entropy, as defined here, requires the global state to be pure:
`entanglementEntropy`'s implementation works only with a `StateVector`
for exactly this reason, and refuses a general density matrix.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "entangled" as binary once entropy is nonzero.** Entanglement
entropy is graded: $0.469$ bits (the worked example above) reflects
partial entanglement, meaningfully less than a Bell state's full 1 bit,
not merely "entangled, like any other entangled state."
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $E(|\Psi^-\rangle)$ for the Bell state
   $|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $E(|\Psi^-\rangle) = 1$ bit, the maximum.

   Take the shortest route. Lesson 5's identity gives
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=1-2|ad-bc|^2$, and for $|\Psi^-\rangle$ the
   determinant is $\tfrac12$, so the purity is $0.5$. A $2\times2$ density
   matrix with purity $0.5$ has eigenvalues
   $\left(1\pm\sqrt{2(0.5)-1}\right)/2 = \left(1\pm0\right)/2$, both equal
   to $\tfrac12$, and

   $$
   S = -2\left(\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12\right) = 1 \text{ bit.}
   $$

   The partial trace confirms it directly: $\rho_A = I/2$, and
   `entanglementEntropy` returns $0.9999999999999999$, one bit to within
   floating-point noise.

   All four Bell states give the same answer, for the same reason: all four
   have $|ad-bc|=\tfrac12$, and the identity depends on nothing else. So
   $|\Phi^+\rangle$, $|\Phi^-\rangle$, $|\Psi^+\rangle$ and $|\Psi^-\rangle$
   are equally and maximally entangled, which is why any of them serves
   equally well as the resource in teleportation or superdense coding, and
   why the four are interconvertible by single-qubit gates.

   One bit is not an arbitrary scale marker. It is the largest entropy a
   single qubit's density matrix can have, from the last lesson, so
   "maximally entangled pair" and "one ebit of entanglement" are the same
   statement. The wrong turn is expecting a two-qubit maximally entangled
   state to carry $2$ bits, one per qubit. $E$ is defined as the entropy of
   *one* reduced state, not the sum over both, precisely so that the two
   qubits do not get counted twice for a single shared resource.

   </details>

2. Using Lesson 5's identity as a shortcut, find the entanglement entropy
   of $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.99}|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.01}|11\rangle$, and
   confirm it's much closer to 0 than to 1 bit. Then describe how $E$
   behaves as the smaller weight shrinks further, and say whether it ever
   reaches exactly $0$ before the state becomes an outright product state.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|ad-bc| = \sqrt{0.99}\sqrt{0.01} = \sqrt{0.0099} \approx 0.0995$, so
   the identity gives

   $$
   \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1 - 2(0.0099) = 0.9802 .
   $$

   Inverting the purity-to-eigenvalue relation,
   $\lambda_\pm = \left(1\pm\sqrt{2(0.9802)-1}\right)/2 = (1\pm0.98)/2$,
   which is $(0.99, 0.01)$: exactly the two weights, as it must be for a
   state of the form $a|00\rangle+d|11\rangle$, whose reduced state is
   $\mathrm{diag}(|a|^2,|d|^2)$. Then

   $$
   E = -0.99\log_2 0.99 - 0.01\log_2 0.01 = 0.0144 + 0.0664 = 0.0808 \text{ bits,}
   $$

   and `entanglementEntropy` returns $0.08079313589591124$. About one
   twelfth of a bit, far nearer $0$ than $1$.

   As the smaller weight $\varepsilon$ shrinks, $E \approx \varepsilon\log_2(1/\varepsilon) + \varepsilon\log_2 e$
   and goes to zero, but not linearly. The $\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ factor
   makes the fall slower than $\varepsilon$ itself, so the derivative
   $dE/d\varepsilon$ blows up at the origin: at $\varepsilon=0.01$ the
   entropy is $0.0808$, at $\varepsilon=0.001$ it is $0.0114$, a tenfold
   cut in weight buying only a sevenfold cut in entropy. It reaches exactly
   $0$ only at $\varepsilon=0$, where the state is $|00\rangle$ and there is
   genuinely no entanglement. There is no threshold below which a state is
   "effectively unentangled" as far as the measure is concerned.

   The wrong turn is reading $0.0808$ as "barely entangled, so essentially
   a product state" and treating the two as interchangeable. They are not:
   this state fails the separability test outright, and a small amount of
   entanglement is qualitatively different from none, in the way a small
   nonzero probability is different from an impossible event. What is true
   is that using it as a teleportation resource would give a poor fidelity,
   and that quantitative statement is what $0.0808$ bits is for.

   </details>

3. Construct your own mixed global 2-qubit state (different from $I/4$)
   that is manifestly a product state (i.e., $\rho_{AB}=\rho_A\otimes\rho_B$
   for some pair of single-qubit density matrices), and show its reduced
   entropy $S(\rho_A)$ is nonetheless nonzero whenever $\rho_A$ itself is
   mixed, reinforcing why this construction can't be trusted as an
   entanglement measure once $\rho_{AB}$ is mixed.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Take $\rho_A = \mathrm{diag}(0.7, 0.3)$ and $\rho_B = \mathrm{diag}(0.9, 0.1)$,
   two independently prepared, independently biased coin-flip qubits, and
   set $\rho_{AB} = \rho_A \otimes \rho_B = \mathrm{diag}(0.63, 0.07, 0.27, 0.03)$.
   This is a product by construction, so the two qubits are uncorrelated in
   every respect and the entanglement is zero, with nothing to prove.

   Now apply the formula anyway. The engine's partial trace over qubit 1
   returns $\mathrm{diag}(0.7,0.3)$, exactly $\rho_A$ back, as question 3
   of the partial-trace lesson guarantees for any product. Its von Neumann
   entropy is

   $$
   S(\rho_A) = -0.7\log_2 0.7 - 0.3\log_2 0.3 = 0.8813 \text{ bits,}
   $$

   which `vonNeumannEntropy` confirms. A reading of $0.88$ bits for a state
   with zero entanglement, and it is not a rounding artefact but almost the
   full scale. Choosing $\rho_A$ closer to $I/2$ pushes the reading to $1$
   bit, which is the $I/4$ case in the lesson.

   The construction generalizes to any $\rho_A$ you like, and that is the
   point. $S(\rho_A)$ measures how mixed qubit A is, full stop. When
   $\rho_{AB}$ is pure, there is only one way for A to be mixed, namely by
   being entangled with B, so the measure is unambiguous. Once $\rho_{AB}$
   is itself mixed, A can be mixed because it is entangled, or because the
   preparation of A was already a classical coin flip, and $S(\rho_A)$
   sums the two without labelling them. Here it is $100\%$ the second
   source, and reduced entropy has no way to say so.

   The wrong turn is trying to repair the measure by subtracting the global
   entropy, $S(\rho_A) - S(\rho_{AB})$. Try it. Entropy is additive over a
   tensor product, so $S(\rho_{AB}) = S(\rho_A)+S(\rho_B) = 0.8813 + 0.4690 = 1.3503$
   bits, and the difference is $-0.469$: a negative "entanglement" for a
   state with none, which is a new failure rather than a fix. The quantity
   worth knowing about is the conditional entropy
   $S(A|B) = S(\rho_{AB}) - S(\rho_B)$, which is $+0.8813$ here, as any
   sensible conditional uncertainty should be for independent systems, and
   $-1$ for a Bell state, where $S(\rho_{AB})=0$ and $S(\rho_B)=1$. Its
   negativity really is a signature of entanglement, and it is still not a
   measure. Honest entanglement measures for mixed states exist and cost
   considerably more work, which is why this course stops at the pure case
   and says so.

   </details>

4. Why does the diagnosis in "Diagnosing the failure" specifically fail
   to apply when $\rho_{AB}$ is pure? Point to the exact step in Lesson
   5's proof that breaks if $\rho_{AB}$ is allowed to be mixed.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Lesson 5's proof begins by writing
   $|\psi\rangle = a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ and
   derives the identity $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$ from those four
   amplitudes. That very first line is the step that breaks. A mixed
   $\rho_{AB}$ has no state vector, so there are no $a,b,c,d$ to write
   down, no determinant $ad-bc$ to form, and nothing for the right-hand
   side of the identity to mean. Everything after it is downstream of a
   quantity that does not exist.

   When $\rho_{AB}$ *is* pure the line is available, and it does all the
   work. The identity ties the reduced state's mixedness to $|ad-bc|$,
   which is exactly the separability determinant, so "$\rho_A$ is mixed"
   and "$|\psi\rangle$ does not factor" become two readings of a single
   equation. There is only one possible source of mixedness in $\rho_A$,
   because the global state has no uncertainty of its own to contribute:
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_{AB}^2)=1$ says the pair is completely specified. If
   part of a completely specified whole is uncertain, the uncertainty can
   only be about the relationship, which is what entanglement is.

   Drop purity and that last sentence fails, which is the honest statement
   of why the $I/4$ case is not a defect in the formula. $I/4$ has
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_{AB}^2)=0.25$, so the pair is not completely specified,
   and $\rho_A=I/2$'s uncertainty is inherited from the global mixture
   rather than created by correlation. The formula still computes the
   entropy of $\rho_A$ correctly; what fails is the *interpretation* that
   reduced mixedness can only have come from entanglement.

   The wrong turn is to describe the requirement as a technicality that a
   more careful implementation could relax. There is no version of
   $S(\rho_A)$ that distinguishes the two sources, since $\rho_A$ is
   literally the same matrix in both cases: $I/2$ from a Bell state and
   $I/2$ from $I/4$ are indistinguishable, by the very result the
   convex-combinations lesson proved. Any measure that tells them apart has
   to look at $\rho_{AB}$, not only at $\rho_A$, which is why mixed-state
   entanglement measures are a harder subject and not a footnote.

   </details>

## Summary

- Entanglement entropy $E(|\psi\rangle)=S(\rho_A)$ is well-defined and
  meaningful only for a globally *pure* bipartite state.
- It equals 0 for product states, 1 bit for Bell states, and varies
  continuously in between, which makes it a graded measure and never a flag.
- $\rho_{AB}=I/4$ is a concrete, verified counterexample: a product
  (zero-entanglement) mixed global state whose reduced entropy is a
  full 1 bit, proving reduced entropy is not a valid entanglement
  measure once the global state is mixed.
- The failure has a precise cause: a mixed global state lets reduced
  entropy conflate real entanglement with ordinary classical mixedness,
  something a pure global state cannot do.
