## Motivation

Two pieces are now on the table: Lesson 5 proved that, for a 2-qubit
*pure* state, the reduced state's mixedness is exactly equivalent to
entanglement. Lesson 6 built the standard way to quantify mixedness, von
Neumann entropy. Combining them gives **entanglement entropy**, but the
word "pure" in the first sentence is not decoration, and this lesson
proves exactly why it can't be dropped.

## Mathematical Development

### The definition

For a pure 2-qubit state $|\psi\rangle_{AB}$, its **entanglement
entropy** is defined as the von Neumann entropy of either reduced state:

$$
E(|\psi\rangle) = S(\rho_A) = S(\rho_B)
$$

The two are always equal for a pure bipartite state (not asserted:
`entanglementEntropy`'s tests check it directly for several states, and
it follows from the fact that $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ share the same
nonzero eigenvalues for any pure bipartite state, a standard consequence
of the Schmidt decomposition). `entanglementEntropy` in `entanglement.ts`
computes $S(\rho_A)$ specifically: `pureStateDensityMatrix`, then
`reducedDensityMatrixQubit0`, then `vonNeumannEntropy`, composing three
pieces this course has already built and tested individually.

### Computed for the states this course has used

$$
E(|00\rangle) = 0 \qquad E(|{+}0\rangle) = 0 \qquad E(|\Phi^+\rangle) = 1 \text{ bit} \qquad E(|\psi(\theta)\rangle) = -\cos^2\theta\log_2\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta\log_2\sin^2\theta
$$

Product states give exactly 0 (Lesson 5's theorem: pure reduced state
$\Rightarrow$ zero entropy). Bell states give exactly 1 bit (maximally
mixed reduced state). The partially entangled family
$|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$
interpolates continuously between the two, since Lesson 4 already found
$\rho_A(\theta)=\text{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)$ directly. This
is a genuine graded measure, not a binary "entangled or not" flag, and
it increases monotonically as $\theta$ moves from $0$ toward $\pi/4$
(verified directly in `entanglement.test.ts`).

### Why "pure" cannot be dropped: a counterexample

Try applying the same formula, $S(\rho_A)$, to a *mixed* 2-qubit global
state and see what goes wrong. Take
$\rho_{AB}=I/4$, the maximally mixed state on two qubits. This is
manifestly a **product state with zero entanglement**: $I/4=(I/2)\otimes(I/2)$
exactly, the tensor product of two independent, unentangled, maximally
mixed qubits. There is no correlation between them whatsoever, let
alone entanglement.

Its reduced state, computed by the same partial trace as always, is
$\rho_A=\text{Tr}_B(I/4)=I/2$ (verified directly against the engine),
giving $S(\rho_A)=1$ bit, the *same* value as a maximally entangled
Bell state. Reduced entropy just reported "maximal entanglement" for a
state that has provably none at all.

### Diagnosing the failure

The problem is that $S(\rho_A)$, computed from a mixed global state,
conflates two genuinely different sources of uncertainty that a pure
global state doesn't have to distinguish between:

1. Uncertainty about qubit A *because it's entangled with qubit B*
   (genuine entanglement).
2. Uncertainty about qubit A *because the whole preparation was already
   a classical mixture, with no entanglement involved at all*, exactly
   what happened with $I/4$, where the mixedness came entirely from
   preparing each qubit independently as a mixed state.

For a pure global state, source 2 is impossible by definition: there's
no leftover global uncertainty to confuse with entanglement, which is
exactly what made Lesson 5's proof work. For a mixed global state, the
two sources add together in $S(\rho_A)$ with no way to separate them
back out from $\rho_A$ alone. This is a real, structural limitation, not
a minor technicality: it's the reason a genuine measure of mixed-state
entanglement (like the mixed-state Wootters concurrence, mentioned but
not implemented in the next lesson) needs fundamentally more machinery
than "just take the reduced entropy."

## Physical Interpretation

Entanglement entropy is the right tool exactly when there is no
independent, non-entanglement source of mixedness to confuse it with,
guaranteed only when the full system starts in a definite, known pure
state. The moment the global state itself carries classical uncertainty,
reduced entropy stops being a reliable signal of entanglement
specifically, as the $I/4$ counterexample proves outright.

## Worked Example

**Compute the entanglement entropy of $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.9}|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.1}|11\rangle$,
using Lesson 5's identity as a shortcut.**

Lesson 5 gives $\rho_A=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ directly from
$|a|^2=0.9,|d|^2=0.1$ (no off-diagonal terms here since $b=c=0$). This is
already diagonal, so (Lesson 6) its entropy is just the Shannon entropy
of $(0.9,0.1)$: $E=-0.9\log_2(0.9)-0.1\log_2(0.1)\approx0.469$ bits,
well above 0 (it's entangled) but well below 1 bit (less than maximally
entangled), consistent with its lopsided $90\%/10\%$ amplitude split.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Computing $S(\rho_A)$ for a mixed global state and calling it
"entanglement entropy."** As the $I/4$ counterexample shows, this can
report large values for states with zero entanglement. Entanglement
entropy, as defined here, requires the global state to be pure:
`entanglementEntropy`'s implementation works only with a `StateVector`
for exactly this reason, not a general density matrix.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "entangled" as binary once entropy is nonzero.** Entanglement
entropy is graded: $0.469$ bits (the worked example above) reflects
partial entanglement, meaningfully less than a Bell state's full 1 bit,
not simply "entangled, like any other entangled state."
</Callout>

## Summary

- Entanglement entropy $E(|\psi\rangle)=S(\rho_A)$ is well-defined and
  meaningful only for a globally *pure* bipartite state.
- It equals 0 for product states, 1 bit for Bell states, and varies
  continuously in between: a genuine graded measure, not a flag.
- $\rho_{AB}=I/4$ is a concrete, verified counterexample: a product
  (zero-entanglement) mixed global state whose reduced entropy is a
  full 1 bit, proving reduced entropy is not a valid entanglement
  measure once the global state is mixed.
- The failure has a precise cause: a mixed global state lets reduced
  entropy conflate real entanglement with ordinary classical mixedness,
  something a pure global state cannot do.
