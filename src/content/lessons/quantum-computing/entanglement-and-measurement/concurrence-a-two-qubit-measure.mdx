## Motivation

Somewhere in this platform's existing code is a function that already
computes a genuine entanglement measure, written long before this
lesson, for what looks like an entirely unrelated purpose. Finding it,
and proving why it works, is this lesson's actual subject.

Entanglement entropy needed an eigenvalue calculation, small for a
$2\times2$ reduced state, but a genuinely different kind of computation
than anything amplitude-based. **Concurrence** answers the same
question (how entangled is this pure 2-qubit state?) with a formula
built directly from the four amplitudes, no diagonalization required.

## Mathematical Development

### The formula, and where it already lives

For $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$
(normalized), **concurrence** is defined as

$$
C(|\psi\rangle) = 2|ad-bc|
$$

Quantum Gates & Circuits' `testSeparability` already computes
`determinantMagnitude`$=|ad-bc|$ to test whether $|\psi\rangle$ factors
into a product state: the *same* quantity, used there as a yes/no
separability test. Concurrence is nothing more than twice that same
number, kept as a graded quantity instead of thresholded into a boolean.
`concurrenceOfPureState` in `entanglement.ts` is a two-line function that
calls `testSeparability` and doubles its determinant: not a
coincidence of implementation convenience, but a direct reuse of an
identity that was already proven true.

### Exact relationship to purity

Lesson 5 proved $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$ for any normalized
2-qubit pure state. Substituting $C=2|ad-bc|$, i.e.
$|ad-bc|=C/2$:

$$
1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 2\left(\frac{C}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{C^2}{2}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
C = \sqrt{2\big(1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\big)}
$$

Concurrence and reduced purity are not just qualitatively related:
they determine each other exactly, by this formula. Both are 0 together
(product states) and both reach their respective maxima together (Bell
states: $C=1$, $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=0.5$).

### Range and the partially-entangled family

$C$ ranges from $0$ (product state) to $1$ (Bell state, the maximum a
normalized state allows, matching Lesson 5's proof that $|ad-bc|\leq0.5$).
For $|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$
($b=c=0$): $C(\theta)=2|\cos\theta\sin\theta|=|\sin2\theta|$, computed
directly and checked against the engine for several values of $\theta$,
including the general formula, not just the endpoints.

### Concurrence vs. entanglement entropy: related, not identical

Both $C(\theta)=|\sin2\theta|$ and $E(\theta)$ (Lesson 7's entanglement
entropy for the same family) are 0 at $\theta=0$ and reach their maximum
at $\theta=\pi/4$, and both are verified to be zero or nonzero together
for every state tested, but they are genuinely different functions of
$\theta$, not two names for the same number. $C$ is built from
amplitudes with no logarithm anywhere; $E$ is a Shannon entropy, built
from $\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta$ through $-p\log_2p$ terms. They agree on
*which* states are entangled, and on the direction of "more" vs. "less,"
without being numerically equal at intermediate $\theta$: two
independent, cross-checking measures rather than duplicates of each
other. Plotted together, the gap between them is visible directly, not
just asserted:

Both curves start at 0, both reach their maximum at $\theta=\pi/4$, and
both are 0 only at $\theta=0$, exactly the agreement claimed above, but
$E(\theta)$ (built from a logarithm) rises faster near the endpoints than
$C(\theta)$ (built from a sine) does: two genuinely different functions
that happen to agree on the two things that matter for this course, zero
vs. nonzero, and which of two states is more entangled.

### Scope: pure two-qubit states only

$C=2|ad-bc|$ is the pure-state concurrence formula for exactly two
qubits; it is not the general definition. The Wootters concurrence for
a general *mixed* two-qubit state requires the eigenvalues of the
non-Hermitian matrix product $\rho(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)\rho^*(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)$,
a genuinely harder numerical problem (eigenvalues of a non-Hermitian
matrix, which don't even have to be real) that this platform's
$2\times2$-Hermitian-only eigensolver cannot handle and does not attempt.
`concurrenceOfPureState` is named and scoped accordingly; it is not a
drop-in replacement for the general formula, and this course does not
claim it is.

## Physical Interpretation

Two structurally different roads, diagonalizing a reduced density
matrix or evaluating a determinant of amplitudes, arrive at consistent
conclusions about how entangled a state is. The curves plotted earlier
make that agreement concrete rather than asserted: both start at zero,
both peak at the same $\theta$, and neither ever contradicts the other
about which of two states is more entangled, even while tracing visibly
different shapes along the way.

That agreement is itself informative: it means "how entangled is this
state" is a well-posed physical question with an answer robust to which
valid measure you use to ask it, at least for the pure 2-qubit states
this course covers.

## Worked Example

**Compute the concurrence of $|\psi(0.3)\rangle=\cos(0.3)|00\rangle+\sin(0.3)|11\rangle$
two ways: directly from the determinant, and from the $|\sin2\theta|$
shortcut.**

Directly: $a=\cos(0.3)\approx0.9553$, $d=\sin(0.3)\approx0.2955$,
$b=c=0$, so $|ad-bc|=|ad|\approx0.2823$, giving $C\approx0.5646$.

Via the shortcut: $|\sin(2\times0.3)|=|\sin(0.6)|\approx0.5646$, the same
value, as the derivation guarantees for every $\theta$, not just this
one. $\theta=0.3$ sits well left of the peak on the curve plotted
earlier, consistent with both derivations landing on a value clearly
below concurrence's maximum of 1.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Using $C=2|ad-bc|$ on a mixed 2-qubit density matrix.** This formula
requires a pure state's amplitudes $a,b,c,d$ directly; it has no
well-defined input for a mixed $\rho_{AB}$. The general mixed-state
Wootters concurrence is a different, more involved formula this platform
does not implement (see the Scope note above).
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting $C(\theta)$ and $E(\theta)$ to be numerically equal.** They
agree on the two endpoints ($0$ and maximal) and on ordering, but are
different functions in between: check the worked example's
$\theta=0.3$: $C\approx0.565$, while $E(0.3)$ (Lesson 7's entropy
formula) comes out to a different number.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the concurrence of $|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$

2. Using the exact relationship derived above, find $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$
   for a state with concurrence $C=0.6$, without computing the reduced
   density matrix directly.
3. Compute $E(0.3)$ (entanglement entropy, from Lesson 7's formula, using
   $\cos^2(0.3)\approx0.9126,\sin^2(0.3)\approx0.0874$) and compare it
   numerically to $C(0.3)\approx0.5646$ from the worked example, confirming
   they're close but not equal.
4. Explain, using this lesson's exact identity, why $C=1$ forces
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=0.5$ exactly, i.e., why maximal concurrence
   necessarily means a maximally mixed reduced state, not just a "very
   mixed" one.

## Summary

- $C(|\psi\rangle)=2|ad-bc|$ is exactly twice the determinant magnitude
  `testSeparability` already computes, a direct reuse, not a
  reimplementation.
- $C$ and reduced purity satisfy the exact identity
  $C=\sqrt{2(1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2))}$, tying the two measures together
  precisely.
- $C$ ranges from 0 (product states) to 1 (Bell states), agreeing with
  entanglement entropy on which states are entangled without being
  numerically identical to it.
- This is the pure two-qubit formula specifically; the general
  mixed-state Wootters concurrence needs non-Hermitian eigenvalues this
  platform does not compute.
