## An eigenvalue calculation, and an alternative to it

Entanglement entropy needed an eigenvalue calculation. That is a small
computation for a $2\times2$ reduced state, but it is a different kind of
computation from anything amplitude-based, and it does not generalize
cheaply.

**Concurrence** answers the same question (how entangled is this pure
2-qubit state?) with a formula built from the four amplitudes and no
diagonalization at all. Having two independent routes to one physical
quantity is worth more than it might sound: when they agree, as they will
below, the agreement is evidence that "how entangled is this state" is a
well-posed question rather than an artifact of one definition.

## A determinant of amplitudes, and what it equals

### The formula, and where it already lives

For $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$
(normalized), **concurrence** is defined as

$$
C(|\psi\rangle) = 2|ad-bc|
$$

`testSeparability` already computes
`determinantMagnitude`$=|ad-bc|$ to test whether $|\psi\rangle$ factors
into a product state, which is the criterion Lesson 5 derived from
coefficient matching: the *same* quantity, used there as a yes/no
separability test. Concurrence is nothing more than twice that same
number, kept as a graded quantity instead of thresholded into a boolean.
`concurrenceOfPureState` in `entanglement.ts` is a two-line function that
calls `testSeparability` and doubles its determinant: not a
coincidence of implementation convenience, but a direct reuse of an
identity that was already proven true.

### Exact relationship to purity

Lesson 5 proved $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$ for any normalized
2-qubit pure state. Substituting $C=2|ad-bc|$, i.e.
$|ad-bc|=C/2$:

$$
1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 2\left(\frac{C}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{C^2}{2}
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
C = \sqrt{2\big(1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\big)}
$$

Concurrence and reduced <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> are not just qualitatively related:
they determine each other exactly, by this formula. Both are 0 together
(product states) and both reach their respective maxima together (Bell
states: $C=1$, $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=0.5$).

The identity above was derived algebraically, but it's worth watching it
hold numerically, for a family where $C$ and $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ are
computed two independent ways. For
$|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$, the
left-most bar below comes straight from $C=2|ad-bc|$, the middle bar
from the reduced density matrix's trace, and the right bar from plugging
that same trace into the identity's right-hand side, with no reference to
$C$ anywhere. Watch the right bar track the left one as $\theta$ sweeps.

### Range and the partially-entangled family

$C$ ranges from $0$ (product state) to $1$ (Bell state, the maximum a
normalized state allows, matching Lesson 5's proof that $|ad-bc|\leq0.5$).
For $|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$
($b=c=0$): $C(\theta)=2|\cos\theta\sin\theta|=|\sin2\theta|$, computed
directly and checked against the engine for several values of $\theta$,
including the general formula that covers everything between the endpoints.

### Concurrence set against entanglement entropy

Both $C(\theta)=|\sin2\theta|$ and $E(\theta)$ (Lesson 7's entanglement
entropy for the same family) are 0 at $\theta=0$ and reach their maximum
at $\theta=\pi/4$, and both are verified to be zero or nonzero together
for every state tested. $C$ is built from amplitudes with no logarithm
anywhere; $E$ is a Shannon entropy, built from $\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta$
through $-p\log_2p$ terms. Two different functions, then, sharing two
fixed points. The interesting question is how they leave those points.

Both curves start at 0, both reach their maximum at $\theta=\pi/4$, and
both come back to 0 together at $\theta=\pi/2$, where the state is the
product state $|11\rangle$ again. They vanish at exactly the same two
values of $\theta$ and nowhere else, which is the agreement predicted
above. Between those points they separate. $C(\theta)$, built from a sine, rises
faster near $\theta=0$ than $E(\theta)$, built from a logarithm, because
$E$'s slope vanishes there and it stays flat before catching up. Two
different functions, agreeing on the two things this course needs: zero
versus nonzero, and which of two states is more entangled.

### Scope: pure two-qubit states only

$C=2|ad-bc|$ is the pure-state concurrence formula for exactly two
qubits; it is not the general definition. The <Term id="mixed-state-concurrence">Wootters concurrence</Term> for
a general *mixed* two-qubit state requires the eigenvalues of the
non-Hermitian matrix product $\rho(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)\rho^*(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)$,
a substantially harder numerical problem (eigenvalues of a non-Hermitian
matrix, which don't even have to be real) that this platform's
$2\times2$-Hermitian-only eigensolver cannot handle and does not attempt.
`concurrenceOfPureState` is named and scoped accordingly; it is not a
drop-in replacement for the general formula, and this course does not
claim it is.

## Two roads to a consistent verdict

Two structurally different roads, diagonalizing a <Term id="partial-trace">reduced density
matrix</Term> or evaluating a determinant of amplitudes, arrive at consistent
conclusions about how entangled a state is. The curves plotted earlier
make that agreement concrete rather than asserted: both start at zero,
both peak at the same $\theta$, and neither ever contradicts the other
about which of two states is more entangled, even while tracing visibly
different shapes along the way.

That agreement is itself informative: it means "how entangled is this
state" is a well-posed physical question with an answer robust to which
valid measure you use to ask it, at least for the pure 2-qubit states
this course covers.

## The same concurrence, computed two ways

**Compute the concurrence of $|\psi(0.3)\rangle=\cos(0.3)|00\rangle+\sin(0.3)|11\rangle$
two ways: directly from the determinant, and from the $|\sin2\theta|$
shortcut.**

Directly: $a=\cos(0.3)\approx0.9553$, $d=\sin(0.3)\approx0.2955$,
$b=c=0$, so $|ad-bc|=|ad|\approx0.2823$, giving $C\approx0.5646$.

Via the shortcut: $|\sin(2\times0.3)|=|\sin(0.6)|\approx0.5646$, the same
value, as the derivation guarantees for every $\theta$, not just this
one. $\theta=0.3$ sits well left of the peak on the curve plotted
earlier, consistent with both derivations landing on a value clearly
below concurrence's maximum of 1.

## This formula has no mixed-state input

<Callout type="warning">
**Using $C=2|ad-bc|$ on a mixed 2-qubit density matrix.** This formula
requires a pure state's amplitudes $a,b,c,d$ directly; it has no
well-defined input for a mixed $\rho_{AB}$. The general mixed-state
Wootters concurrence is a different, more involved formula this platform
does not implement (see the Scope note above).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting $C(\theta)$ and $E(\theta)$ to be numerically equal.** They
agree on the two endpoints ($0$ and maximal) and on ordering, but are
different functions in between: check the worked example's
$\theta=0.3$: $C\approx0.565$, while $E(0.3)$ (Lesson 7's entropy
formula) comes out to a different number.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the concurrence of $|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$
   directly from $a,b,c,d$. Then say what $C$ would be if that minus sign
   were a plus, and what the comparison tells you about which features of
   the amplitudes concurrence responds to.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Read off $a=0$, $b=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $c=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $d=0$:

   $$
   C = 2|ad-bc| = 2\left|0 - \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\right| = 2\left(\tfrac12\right) = 1
   $$

   Maximal, and `concurrenceOfPureState` returns $1.0000000000000002$.

   With a plus sign, $|\Psi^+\rangle$ has $b=c=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and
   $ad-bc = -\tfrac12$, whose magnitude is again $\tfrac12$, so $C=1$
   unchanged. Both are maximally entangled, and so are $|\Phi^\pm\rangle$
   by the same arithmetic with the roles of the diagonals exchanged.

   The comparison isolates what $C$ can and cannot see. It responds only to
   the *magnitude* of a single determinant built from the four amplitudes,
   so any change that leaves $|ad-bc|$ alone is invisible to it: the sign
   here, and more generally any relative phase, since multiplying $b$ by
   $e^{i\varphi}$ multiplies $ad-bc$'s components without changing the
   magnitude in the two-term case. That is the right behaviour, because the
   four Bell states are interconvertible by single-qubit gates and
   single-qubit gates cannot change how entangled a pair is. A measure that
   scored $|\Psi^+\rangle$ and $|\Psi^-\rangle$ differently would be
   measuring the wrong thing.

   What $C$ does see is the balance between the two diagonals of the
   amplitude array. Change $b$ to $0.9$ and $c$ to $-\sqrt{0.19}$ and the
   determinant magnitude falls to $0.392$, giving $C=0.785$.

   The wrong turn is placing $|\Psi^-\rangle$'s amplitudes in the $a,d$
   slots because the state looks symmetric. The slot order is
   $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$, and this state occupies
   the middle two. Here the mistake happens to give the same magnitude,
   which is exactly why it survives to bite on a state like
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|01\rangle)$, where the correct answer is
   $C=0$ and the swapped one is not.

   </details>

2. Using the exact relationship derived above, find $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$
   for a state with concurrence $C=0.6$, without computing the reduced
   density matrix directly. Then run the relation the other way: what
   concurrence corresponds to a reduced purity of $0.9$, and is that state
   more or less entangled than the $C=0.6$ one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The identity is $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=C^2/2$, so

   $$
   \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1 - \frac{0.6^2}{2} = 1 - 0.18 = 0.82 .
   $$

   Comfortably between the pure-state value $1$ and the Bell-state floor
   $0.5$: partially entangled, and correspondingly partially mixed.

   Inverting, $C = \sqrt{2\left(1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\right)}$, so purity
   $0.9$ gives $C = \sqrt{0.2} \approx 0.447$. Less entangled than the
   $C=0.6$ state, and consistently so: higher purity means a reduced state
   closer to pure, which means less has been lost to the correlation.

   The pairing is worth reading as a single dial. As entanglement runs from
   none to maximal, $C$ climbs $0 \to 1$ while purity falls $1 \to 0.5$,
   and the identity pins them to each other exactly, with no state
   anywhere in between escaping the relation. So for a two-qubit pure
   state, "how entangled" and "how mixed is either half" are one quantity
   with two names, and which one to compute is purely a matter of
   convenience: $C$ from four amplitudes with no diagonalization, purity
   from a partial trace and a matrix square.

   The wrong turn is dropping the factor of $2$ and writing
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1-C^2 = 0.64$. That value is below the $0.5$ floor
   as soon as $C$ passes $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, which is the fastest way to
   notice the error: any formula that lets a single qubit's purity fall
   under $0.5$ has a coefficient wrong. Checking the endpoint $C=1$ against
   the known Bell answer $0.5$ pins the factor without re-deriving
   anything.

   </details>

3. Compute $E(0.3)$ (entanglement entropy, from Lesson 7's formula, using
   $\cos^2(0.3)\approx0.9127,\sin^2(0.3)\approx0.0873$) and compare it
   numerically to $C(0.3)\approx0.5646$ from the worked example, confirming
   they're close but not equal.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The reduced state is $\mathrm{diag}(0.9127, 0.0873)$, already diagonal,
   so its entropy is the Shannon entropy of that pair:

   $$
   E(0.3) = -0.9127\log_2 0.9127 - 0.0873\log_2 0.0873 = 0.1203 + 0.3072 = 0.4275 \text{ bits}
   $$

   The engine returns $0.4275017710560216$ for $E$ and
   $0.5646424733950353$ for $C$, the latter matching $\sin(0.6)$ to the
   last digit, as $C(\theta)=|\sin 2\theta|$ predicts.

   Close, and not equal, and the gap is the interesting part. Both are $0$
   at $\theta=0$ and both reach their maximum of $1$ at $\theta=\pi/4$, so
   they agree at the endpoints and are forced to agree on the *ordering* of
   states in this family: whenever one says a state is more entangled, so
   does the other. They disagree on the scale in between, because $C$ is
   built from amplitudes and $E$ from a logarithm of probabilities, and no
   change of units turns one into the other.

   Near $\theta=0$ they separate fastest. $C \approx 2\theta$ climbs with
   slope $2$, while $E$ leaves the origin with zero slope, since its
   leading behaviour is $\varepsilon\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ in the small
   weight $\varepsilon=\sin^2\theta \approx \theta^2$. At $\theta=0.3$ that
   already shows: $C$ has covered $56\%$ of its range and $E$ only $43\%$.

   Neither is wrong, and that is the honest conclusion. "How entangled is
   this state" has a well-posed *ordering* but no unique numerical scale,
   the way temperature has a well-posed ordering readable in Celsius or
   Fahrenheit. $E$ is the one to quote when the answer must be in bits and
   comparable to a channel capacity; $C$ is the one to compute when you
   have amplitudes and no eigensolver. The wrong turn is treating a
   disagreement between them as evidence that one is miscalibrated.

   </details>

4. Explain, using this lesson's exact identity, why $C=1$ forces
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=0.5$ exactly, i.e., why maximal concurrence
   necessarily means an exactly maximally mixed reduced state, rather
   than merely a "very mixed" one. Then state the matching claim for
   entanglement entropy.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The identity is an equation, not an inequality, so substituting
   $C=1$ leaves nothing to estimate:

   $$
   1 - \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = \frac{C^2}{2} = \frac12 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 0.5
   $$

   Exactly $0.5$, for every state with $C=1$, with no family of nearly
   maximal states sitting just above it. And $0.5$ is the minimum a single
   qubit's purity can reach, so the reduced state is not merely very mixed
   but at the floor, which for a $2\times2$ density matrix means
   eigenvalues $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ and therefore $\rho_A = I/2$ exactly:
   the maximally mixed state, with no freedom left in it.

   The matching claim for entropy follows immediately, since the
   eigenvalues are now pinned: $E = -2\left(\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12\right) = 1$
   bit, again exactly, and again the maximum a qubit's entropy can take.
   So the three statements "$C=1$", "$\rho_A=I/2$" and "$E=1$ bit" are
   equivalent for a two-qubit pure state, each forcing the other two.

   Why the exactness is worth insisting on: it is what makes "maximally
   entangled" a sharp class rather than a vague region. Any state achieving
   $C=1$ has both halves in precisely $I/2$, which is what teleportation
   and superdense coding need, and it is why those protocols are exact
   rather than approximate. Drop $C$ to $0.99$ and the purity is $0.51$,
   close but no longer the floor, and the protocols degrade measurably.

   The wrong turn is arguing from an inequality: $C\leq1$ implies
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\geq0.5$, so $C=1$ makes the purity "as low as
   possible", and inferring that it is near $0.5$ rather than equal to it.
   The identity does the work an inequality cannot. It is also worth
   noticing what the identity does *not* claim, namely anything at all
   about a mixed global state, where $\rho_A=I/2$ is perfectly compatible
   with zero entanglement, as the previous lesson's $I/4$ case shows.

   </details>

## Summary

- $C(|\psi\rangle)=2|ad-bc|$ is exactly twice the determinant magnitude
  `testSeparability` already computes: a direct reuse, with no
  reimplementation.
- $C$ and reduced purity satisfy the exact identity
  $C=\sqrt{2(1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2))}$, tying the two measures together
  precisely.
- $C$ ranges from 0 (product states) to 1 (Bell states), agreeing with
  entanglement entropy on which states are entangled without being
  numerically identical to it.
- This is the pure two-qubit formula specifically; the general
  mixed-state Wootters concurrence needs non-Hermitian eigenvalues this
  platform does not compute.
