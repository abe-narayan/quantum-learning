## A sentence worth reading slowly

The Bell state's <Term id="partial-trace">reduced density matrix</Term> works out to $\rho_A=I/2$. The whole
is perfectly well-defined; either half alone is maximally undefined. Read
slowly, that should sound like a contradiction: how can you know
everything about a pair and nothing about either member?

It is not a quirk of $|\Phi^+\rangle$'s 50/50 amplitudes. This lesson
proves the effect is forced for any 2-qubit pure state, and produces an
exact formula for how mixed a subsystem becomes as a function of how
entangled the whole state is. The formula turns out to be one line, and it
connects to the separability determinant this platform already computes.

## One identity, proven by direct expansion

### Setting up the general 2-qubit case

Let $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ be any
normalized 2-qubit state. The whole lesson turns on one criterion:
$|\psi\rangle$ is a product state exactly when $ad-bc=0$. Multi-Qubit
State Vectors tested factorability by matching coefficients one at a
time; the determinant packages that same test into a single number, and
the two-line argument for it is worth having in front of you.

If $|\psi\rangle=(a_0|0\rangle+a_1|1\rangle)\otimes(b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle)$,
then matching coefficients gives $a=a_0b_0$, $b=a_0b_1$, $c=a_1b_0$ and
$d=a_1b_1$, so $ad=a_0b_0a_1b_1=bc$ and the determinant vanishes.
Conversely, suppose $ad=bc$. If $a\neq0$, set
$|\alpha\rangle\propto a|0\rangle+c|1\rangle$ and
$|\beta\rangle\propto a|0\rangle+b|1\rangle$; their tensor product has
amplitudes proportional to $(a^2,\,ab,\,ac,\,cb)$, and $cb=ad$ turns the
last one into $ad$, so after dividing through by $a$ the four amplitudes
are $(a,b,c,d)$ as required. If $a=0$ then $bc=0$, and whichever of $b,c$
vanishes leaves a two-term state that factors by inspection. So
$ad-bc=0$ is exactly the condition, and it is what `testSeparability`
computes, returning $|ad-bc|$ as `determinantMagnitude`.

The partial trace (last lesson) gives qubit 0's reduced state:

$$
\rho_A = \begin{pmatrix} |a|^2+|b|^2 & ac^*+bd^* \\ (ac^*+bd^*)^* & |c|^2+|d|^2 \end{pmatrix}
$$

Write $p=|a|^2+|b|^2$ (so $q=|c|^2+|d|^2=1-p$ by normalization) and
$z=ac^*+bd^*$ for the off-diagonal entry.

### The purity identity, derived

For any $2\times2$ density matrix $\begin{pmatrix}p&z\\z^*&1-p\end{pmatrix}$,
direct matrix multiplication gives
$\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=p^2+(1-p)^2+2|z|^2$. So:

$$
1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1-p^2-(1-p)^2-2|z|^2 = 2p(1-p)-2|z|^2 = 2\big(pq-|z|^2\big)
$$

Now expand $pq-|z|^2$ directly in terms of $a,b,c,d$:

$$
pq = |a|^2|c|^2+|a|^2|d|^2+|b|^2|c|^2+|b|^2|d|^2
$$

$$
|z|^2 = |ac^*+bd^*|^2 = |a|^2|c|^2+|b|^2|d|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}(ab^*c^*d)
$$

$$
pq-|z|^2 = |a|^2|d|^2+|b|^2|c|^2-2\,\text{Re}(ab^*c^*d)
$$

Compare this to $|ad-bc|^2=(ad-bc)(a^*d^*-b^*c^*)=|a|^2|d|^2+|b|^2|c|^2-2\,\text{Re}(ad\,b^*c^*)$.
Since complex multiplication commutes, $ab^*c^*d=ad\,b^*c^*$ (the same
product, just reordered), so $pq-|z|^2=|ad-bc|^2$ exactly. Substituting
back:

$$
\boxed{1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 2|ad-bc|^2}
$$

An exact algebraic identity, holding for *every* normalized 2-qubit pure
state, with no approximation anywhere in the derivation. It is verified
numerically against the engine (`purity` composed with
`reducedDensityMatrixQubit0`, compared to `testSeparability`'s
determinant) for a Bell state, a product state, a partially entangled
state, and an unstructured "random-looking" state; all four agree to
floating-point precision.

The mechanism behind the identity is visible in the partial trace itself.
The $|00\rangle\langle11|$ cross term of $\rho_{AB}$, of size $ad$, has
mismatched bra and ket indices on *both* qubits, so it never survives into
$\rho_A$ (or $\rho_B$). That excluded cross term, which grows with
$ad-bc$, is what $\rho_A$ loses track of.
For each state below, all three presets have the same amplitude pattern
($a|00\rangle+d|11\rangle$), so $\rho_A$ stays exactly diagonal
throughout; what changes is how far that diagonal sits from $1{:}0$:

### The theorem

Since $|ad-bc|^2\geq0$ always, the identity immediately gives
$\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\leq1$, with equality **exactly when** $ad-bc=0$,
exactly `testSeparability`'s condition for a product state. This proves,
for 2-qubit pure states:

$$
\rho_A \text{ is pure} \iff |\psi\rangle_{AB} \text{ is a product state} \iff \rho_B \text{ is pure}
$$

The $\rho_B$ end of that chain comes free: repeating the expansion with
the roles of $b$ and $c$ swapped gives $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_B^2)=2|ad-cb|^2$,
the same number. The two qubits of a pure pair are always exactly as
mixed as each other.

The identity also bounds how far this can go. Normalization caps the
determinant at $\tfrac12$:

$$
|ad-bc| \leq |a||d| + |b||c| \leq \frac{|a|^2+|d|^2}{2} + \frac{|b|^2+|c|^2}{2} = \frac12
$$

using the triangle inequality, then $xy\leq(x^2+y^2)/2$ on each pair, then
$|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2=1$. So $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ never exceeds
$2(\tfrac12)^2=\tfrac12$, and $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ never falls below
$0.5$: the reduced state of one qubit cannot be more mixed than $I/2$,
which is the floor the Bell states already sit on.

Equivalently: **a 2-qubit pure state's reduced state is mixed if and only
if the state is entangled.** Not "mixed states are associated with
entanglement" as a slogan, but an if-and-only-if, with the amount of
mixedness ($1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$) pinned to how far the separability
determinant sits from zero.

### Scope: this is a 2-qubit statement

This proof used the 2-qubit reduced-density-matrix formula explicitly.
It is not a general $n$-qubit argument. The underlying idea (a pure
global state has a mixed reduced state exactly when there's entanglement
across the cut) is true far more generally, provable with the <Term id="schmidt-decomposition">Schmidt
decomposition</Term> for any bipartite split of any dimension, but that
general proof needs a general eigendecomposition, which this platform does
not implement (see `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`). The boxed identity is airtight
for the case this course uses: two qubits, one cut.

## Mixedness that is not uncertainty leaking in

A reduced state's mixedness is not uncertainty leaking in, the way it was
for the classical mixtures two lessons ago. The global state
$|\psi\rangle_{AB}$ is fully known. The mixedness of $\rho_A$ is instead a
signature of *correlation with something outside the subsystem being
described*.

This is the sharpest sense in which entanglement is not classical
correlation. Complete knowledge of the whole gives incomplete knowledge of
each part, whenever the parts are entangled. Nothing in classical physics
works that way, and it is worth being precise about why, because the
obvious objection nearly works. Two coins glued to always match have the
joint distribution $\tfrac12$ for 00 and $\tfrac12$ for 11, whose two
marginals are both uniform: a known joint, maximally uncertain parts. But
that distribution is *itself* a statement of ignorance, the classical
counterpart of a mixed state, not of a pure one. A classical state of
complete knowledge is a definite assignment of values, and a definite
assignment for the pair is automatically a definite assignment for each
member. There is no classical state of maximal knowledge whose parts are
uncertain. $|\Phi^+\rangle$ is exactly that, with no ignorance anywhere
left to appeal to.

That correlation is easiest to see as a physical thing, rather than as an
algebraic byproduct of the <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term> identity, in the maximally entangled case:
$|\Phi^+\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$. Alice and Bob
each hold one qubit, and alone each holds the maximally mixed
$\rho_A=\rho_B=I/2$ derived above. The interactive below samples one real
joint measurement at a time, built with the platform's engine (`HADAMARD`
then `applyCNOT`, the construction the CHSH lesson uses) rather than a
hand-written coin flip. Watch both glyphs, held far apart, land on the
same outcome every time:

## Purity without computing a partial trace

**Use the boxed identity to find $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ for
$|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.9}\,|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.1}\,|11\rangle$ without
computing the partial trace directly.**

Here $a=\sqrt{0.9}$, $b=c=0$, $d=\sqrt{0.1}$, so
$ad-bc=\sqrt{0.9}\sqrt{0.1}=\sqrt{0.09}=0.3$. By the identity,
$1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=2(0.3)^2=0.18$, so $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=0.82$,
closer to 1 (less mixed) than the Bell state's 0.5, matching the
intuition that this state is less entangled than a maximally entangled
Bell pair (its amplitudes are $90\%/10\%$, not $50\%/50\%$).

$\rho_A=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ is itself just a Z-basis mixture of
$|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ weighted 90/10, which is the kind of object
the density matrix explorer builds directly:

## Nonzero loss is not maximal entanglement

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking any nonzero purity loss means "maximal" entanglement.**
$1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ ranges continuously from 0 (product state) to
0.5 (maximally entangled, at $|ad-bc|=0.5$, the largest a normalized state
allows). It is a graded quantity, not a yes/no flag.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming this proof generalizes automatically to 3+ qubits, or to
mixed global states.** It does not, without further work this platform
does not undertake (see the Scope note above). The 2-qubit proof uses the
2-qubit reduced-density-matrix formula by name, and that formula changes
the moment there is a third qubit.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the boxed identity, find $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ for
   $|\Psi^-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$ without
   computing the partial trace directly. Then state what the same identity
   gives for the *other* qubit, $\text{Tr}(\rho_B^2)$, and say why that
   needs no second calculation.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Read the amplitudes off: $a=0$, $b=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
   $c=-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $d=0$. So

   $$
   ad-bc = 0 - \left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)\left(-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) = \tfrac12,
   $$

   and the identity gives
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1 - 2\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 = 1 - \tfrac12 = 0.5$.
   Maximally mixed. Computing the partial trace directly confirms it:
   the engine returns $\rho_A = \mathrm{diag}(0.5,0.5) = I/2$ with purity
   $0.5$, and `testSeparability` reports determinant magnitude $0.5$.

   $\text{Tr}(\rho_B^2)$ is $0.5$ as well, and no second calculation is
   needed because the identity is symmetric in the two qubits: the lesson
   states it as $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=1-\text{Tr}(\rho_B^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$,
   one determinant governing both reduced states at once. That symmetry is
   not decoration. It is what makes "the entanglement of a pure bipartite
   state" a property of the state rather than a property of which half you
   happen to hold, and it is what lets the next lesson define entanglement
   entropy as $S(\rho_A)$ without having to say which qubit.

   The value $\tfrac12$ for $|ad-bc|$ is the maximum a normalized state
   allows, which question 3 proves, so $|\Psi^-\rangle$ is maximally
   entangled and its halves are maximally mixed. The minus sign played no
   part: it cancelled against $c$'s own sign in the product $bc$, and even
   if it had not, the identity uses $|ad-bc|$, a magnitude.

   The slip to watch for is putting the amplitudes in the wrong slots. The
   ordering is $a,b,c,d$ for $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$,
   so for $|\Psi^-\rangle$ the two nonzero amplitudes are $b$ and $c$, not
   $a$ and $d$. Getting that backwards gives $ad-bc = \tfrac12 - 0$, the
   same magnitude here by luck of the symmetric arrangement, and a wrong
   answer on any state where the two diagonals differ.

   </details>

2. For $|\psi(\theta)\rangle=\cos\theta|00\rangle+\sin\theta|11\rangle$,
   express $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)$ as a function of $\theta$ using the
   identity, and confirm it matches $\cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta$ (which
   you can get directly from $\rho_A(\theta)=\text{diag}(\cos^2\theta,\sin^2\theta)$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Here $a=\cos\theta$, $b=c=0$, $d=\sin\theta$, so
   $ad-bc=\cos\theta\sin\theta$ and

   $$
   \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1 - 2\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta .
   $$

   The direct route squares the diagonal matrix:
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=\cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta$. The two agree because

   $$
   \cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta = (\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta)^2 - 2\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta = 1 - 2\cos^2\theta\sin^2\theta ,
   $$

   which is completing the square on $x^2+y^2$ with $x+y=1$. The engine
   evaluates both at $\theta=0.2$, $0.6$ and $\pi/4$ and they agree to
   eight decimal places, coming out as $0.92417668$, $0.56565157$ and
   $0.50000000$.

   A third form is worth having, since $2\cos\theta\sin\theta=\sin 2\theta$:

   $$
   \text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 1 - \tfrac12\sin^2 2\theta .
   $$

   That version makes the behaviour obvious at a glance. Purity is $1$
   whenever $\sin 2\theta = 0$, at $\theta=0$ and $\theta=\pi/2$, where the
   state is $|00\rangle$ or $|11\rangle$ and there is no entanglement. It
   bottoms out at $0.5$ where $|\sin2\theta|=1$, at $\theta=\pi/4$, the
   Bell state. And $\sin^2 2\theta$ never exceeds $1$, so purity never
   drops below $0.5$: a single qubit cannot be more mixed than maximally
   mixed, and the algebra enforces it without being told.

   The wrong turn is expecting $\cos^4\theta+\sin^4\theta$ to equal $1$ by
   analogy with $\cos^2+\sin^2$. It does not; the cross term is exactly
   what is missing, and that missing cross term *is* the mixedness. A
   student who writes $1$ here concludes every state in the family is pure,
   including the Bell state.

   </details>

3. Confirm algebraically that $|ad-bc|$ reaches its maximum possible
   value, $0.5$, for a Bell state, and explain why normalization
   $|a|^2+|b|^2+|c|^2+|d|^2=1$ prevents it from ever exceeding $0.5$ for
   a state of the form $a|00\rangle+d|11\rangle$ ($b=c=0$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $b=c=0$ the determinant is just $|ad| = |a||d|$, and normalization
   reads $|a|^2+|d|^2=1$. The bound is the arithmetic-geometric mean
   inequality in the form that follows from $(|a|-|d|)^2\geq0$:

   $$
   |a|^2 + |d|^2 \geq 2|a||d| \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |a||d| \leq \tfrac{|a|^2+|d|^2}{2} = \tfrac12
   $$

   Equality holds exactly when $|a|=|d|$, which with normalization forces
   $|a|=|d|=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$: the Bell states. Substituting,
   $|ad-bc| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = \tfrac12$,
   confirmed by the engine on $|\Phi^+\rangle$ and $|\Psi^-\rangle$ alike.

   Chaining it through the identity closes the loop with the previous
   lesson. $|ad-bc|\leq\tfrac12$ gives
   $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2) = 2|ad-bc|^2 \leq \tfrac12$, so
   $\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)\geq0.5$. The floor on purity and the ceiling on the
   determinant are the same fact seen from two sides, and both are
   consequences of normalization rather than separate assumptions.

   The bound survives the general case, with $b$ and $c$ nonzero, though
   the argument needs one more step:
   $|ad-bc| \leq |a||d|+|b||c| \leq \tfrac{|a|^2+|d|^2}{2}+\tfrac{|b|^2+|c|^2}{2} = \tfrac12$
   by the triangle inequality followed by the same mean inequality on each
   pair. So $\tfrac12$ is the ceiling for every normalized two-qubit state,
   which is what makes $C=2|ad-bc|$ a sensible measure running from $0$ to
   $1$ two lessons from now.

   The wrong turn is trying to push $|a||d|$ higher by making one amplitude
   large. It backfires: $|a|=0.9$ forces $|d|=\sqrt{1-0.81}\approx0.436$
   and the product falls to $0.392$. The product of two numbers with a
   fixed sum of squares is maximized by balance, not by extremes, and
   maximal entanglement is exactly the balanced case.

   </details>

4. Why does this lesson's proof break down immediately for a 3-qubit
   pure state, even before reaching the question of a general
   eigensolver? (Hint: what does $\rho_A$ even look like, and what is its
   dimension, once "the rest of the system" is two qubits instead of one?)
   Name one thing from this lesson that *does* carry over to three qubits,
   and one that does not.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The proof is built on four amplitudes $a,b,c,d$ arranged as a $2\times2$
   array whose determinant $ad-bc$ is a single number. A 3-qubit pure state
   has eight amplitudes. There is no $2\times2$ array to take a determinant
   of, so the quantity on the right-hand side of the identity does not
   exist, and the derivation has nothing to start from. That happens
   before any question of diagonalizing anything.

   The dimensions make the same point from the other side. Split the three
   qubits as A (qubit 0) against B (qubits 1 and 2). Then $\rho_A$ is still
   $2\times2$, but $\rho_B$ is $4\times4$, so the lesson's symmetric claim
   that the two reduced states share a purity is now a statement relating
   matrices of different sizes, and the closed-form $2\times2$ eigensolver
   this course uses cannot touch $\rho_B$ at all. (The purities do still
   agree, by the Schmidt decomposition, but that is a theorem this course
   does not prove and it is certainly not what the four-amplitude algebra
   establishes.)

   What carries over: the qualitative theorem. For a pure global state and
   any bipartite cut, $\rho_A$ is mixed exactly when the two sides are
   entangled across that cut, and $S(\rho_A)$ still quantifies how much.
   Applied to the cut "qubit 0 against the other two", every step of that
   reasoning survives, and it is what makes entanglement entropy usable on
   large systems.

   What does not carry over: the idea that one number measures the
   entanglement of the state. With three qubits there are three different
   cuts and they need not agree, and worse, states can be inequivalent even
   when every cut matches. The GHZ state
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$ and the W state
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(|001\rangle+|010\rangle+|100\rangle)$ are both
   entangled across every cut, and neither can be converted into the other
   by any local operations, so "how entangled is it" stops having a single
   answer.

   The wrong turn is answering that the proof fails because $\rho_A$
   becomes too big to diagonalize by hand. $\rho_A$ for one qubit is
   $2\times2$ no matter how many qubits it is entangled with, and the
   engine handles it fine. The obstruction is on the amplitude side, in the
   determinant, not on the reduced-state side.

   </details>

## Summary

- For any normalized 2-qubit pure state $a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$,
  $1-\text{Tr}(\rho_A^2)=2|ad-bc|^2$: an exact algebraic identity, proven
  by direct expansion.
- Consequently, $\rho_A$ is pure if and only if the global state is a
  product state: entanglement and reduced-state mixedness are
  mathematically equivalent for 2-qubit pure states.
- The mixedness of an entangled subsystem's reduced state reflects
  correlation with the rest of the system, not any uncertainty about the
  (perfectly well-defined) global state.
- This specific proof is a 2-qubit result; the broader idea generalizes
  via the Schmidt decomposition, which this platform does not implement
  in general.
