## A scale with no interpretation attached

Purity's tie to entanglement holds for 2-qubit pure states, and its scale
is arbitrary: 1 down to 1/d, with no interpretation attached to the
number itself. The measure physicists reach for instead is
**<Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">von Neumann entropy</Term>**, which is measured in bits and means what
bits usually mean.

This lesson builds it as the quantum generalization of a classical idea
you may already know, the Shannon entropy of a probability distribution,
rather than as a new formula to accept. There is one trap along the way,
and it catches people who assume the diagonal of $\rho$ is the
distribution to plug in.

## Eigenvalues in closed form, then entropy from them

### Eigenvalues of a density matrix

A <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermitian</Term> matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis.
Hermitian Operators, in Mathematical Foundations, proves that in general;
this course has not needed it before, and does not need to take it on
trust now either, because the $2\times2$ closed form below shows the
reality of both eigenvalues on its face.
For a $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix
$\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a&z\\z^*&d\end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic
polynomial $\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-|z|^2)=0$ has the closed-form
solution

$$
\lambda_{\pm} = \frac{a+d}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{a-d}{2}\right)^2+|z|^2}
$$

That is the ordinary quadratic formula and nothing more exotic, and it is
what `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` computes. Scope matters here: this is a
closed-form solver for the $2\times2$ case, which is all this needs.
Eigenvalues of an arbitrary $N\times N$ Hermitian matrix need iterative
numerical methods this engine does not implement. Every density matrix
this course needs (single-qubit reduced states) is $2\times2$, so the
closed form covers it.

For a valid density matrix, both eigenvalues are guaranteed
nonnegative (positivity) and sum to 1 (trace), so $\lambda_+,\lambda_-$
form a true probability distribution over two outcomes.

### Von Neumann entropy

$$
S(\rho) = -\text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho) = -\sum_i \lambda_i\log_2\lambda_i
$$

The middle expression is the definition; the right-hand side is what it
equals once $\rho$ is diagonalized (the trace of a function of $\rho$
applied via its eigenvalues, a standard fact for Hermitian matrices,
usable here without re-deriving general functional calculus since the
$2\times2$ case reduces to explicit eigenvalues directly). By convention
$0\log_20\equiv0$ (the limit of $x\log_2x$ as $x\to0^+$), so a zero
eigenvalue contributes nothing rather than producing an undefined term.
`vonNeumannEntropy`'s implementation handles this directly rather than
letting it silently become `NaN`.

### This is Shannon entropy, generalized

If $\rho$ happens to already be diagonal, $\rho=\text{diag}(p_1,\dots,p_d)$
for a classical probability distribution $p_i$, then the
<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenvalues</Term> *are* the $p_i$, and

$$
S(\rho) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i = H(p)
$$

which is Shannon's classical entropy of the distribution $p$. Von Neumann
entropy is not a quantum analogue built by resemblance. For a diagonal
$\rho$ it is the same formula, term for term.

What makes it quantum is that $\rho$ need not be diagonal, and that the
eigenvalues rather than the diagonal entries are what the formula needs.
That distinction is invisible for classical distributions and is where the
next section's trap lives.

### Two limits, verified

For any pure state, one eigenvalue is 1 and the other is 0. Trace 1
alone (Lesson 1) doesn't force this by itself, but $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$
is also idempotent, $\rho^2=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|=\rho$
since $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$, and an idempotent matrix's eigenvalues
must each satisfy $\lambda^2=\lambda$, so $\lambda\in\{0,1\}$; trace 1
then forces exactly one of the two eigenvalues to be $1$ and the other
$0$. This is made concrete by `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` returning
exactly $(1,0)$ for $\rho_{|0\rangle}$, so
$S=-1\log_21-0\log_20=0$: **zero entropy**, the
minimum, for every pure state without exception. For the maximally mixed
qubit $I/2$, both eigenvalues are $0.5$, giving
$S=-2(0.5\log_20.5)=-2(0.5)(-1)=1$: **exactly 1 bit**, the maximum a
single qubit's density matrix can reach. Both values are computed
directly by `vonNeumannEntropy` and checked as regression tests.

Those two values are just the endpoints of a continuous trade-off.
Sweep the diagonal family $\rho(p)=\text{diag}(p,1-p)$ across every
$p\in[0,1]$, not merely $p=1$ and $p=0.5$, and plot both
$\text{Tr}(\rho(p)^2)$ and $S(\rho(p))$, computed directly by `purity`
and `vonNeumannEntropy` (the same engine functions used everywhere
else in this lesson, with nothing re-derived by hand) at each point:

Purity traces a U shape, $1$ at both pure endpoints ($p=0$ and $p=1$)
down to its minimum $0.5$ exactly at $p=0.5$; entropy traces the
mirrored inverted peak, $0$ at both endpoints up to its maximum $1$ bit
at the same $p=0.5$. The two limits from the previous paragraph are
just this curve's endpoints and its midpoint, not a special case: purity
and entropy move in exact lockstep, one falling wherever the other
rises, across the entire family.

### The diagonal-entries trap

Here is the subtlety the definition's phrasing was built to avoid.
Consider $\rho_1=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ (a classical 90/10 mixture) versus
$\rho_2=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix}$: same diagonal
entries, different off-diagonal term.

  explanation="Feed a=0.9, d=0.1, z=0.3 into the quadratic formula above: the discriminant is √(0.4² + 0.3²) = 0.5, and the mean is 0.5, so the eigenvalues are exactly 1 and 0. Adding coherence did not add uncertainty; it removed all of it. Identical diagonals, opposite entropies, and no warning sign in the diagonal that anything is different."

$\rho_1$'s eigenvalues are just its
diagonal entries, $0.9$ and $0.1$, giving $S(\rho_1)\approx0.469$ bits.
But $\rho_2$'s eigenvalues (via the quadratic formula, with
$a=0.9,d=0.1,z=0.3$) come out to $1$ and $0$. So $\rho_2$ is a *pure*
state wearing $\rho_1$'s diagonal, and $S(\rho_2)=0$. Both values come
from the engine rather than from assertion.

Reading entropy off the diagonal would have given the wrong answer for
$\rho_2$, and given it silently, with no sign anything had gone wrong.
Entropy is a property of the whole matrix through its eigenvalues.

## Uncertainty, measured in bits

Entropy quantifies, in bits, how much uncertainty remains about which
definite state a system is in. Zero for a pure state, where the system is
unambiguously in one specific quantum state, rising toward its maximum as
the mixture spreads evenly across more distinguishable possibilities.

Because it is built from eigenvalues, it does not change when you
re-describe $\rho$ in another basis. Purity is basis-independent for the
same reason, so that is not what separates them. What entropy adds is a
unit. Purity $0.82$ says a state is somewhat mixed without saying how
much of what; entropy $0.469$ bits says how many bits of information you
are missing, on the same scale a classical channel is measured in.

## Entropy of a three-quarters mixture

**A qubit is prepared as $|0\rangle$ with probability $0.75$ and
$|1\rangle$ with probability $0.25$. Find its von Neumann entropy.**

This mixture's density matrix is already diagonal:
$\rho=\text{diag}(0.75,0.25)$, so its eigenvalues are just $0.75,0.25$
themselves. $S=-0.75\log_2(0.75)-0.25\log_2(0.25)$. Using
$\log_2(0.75)\approx-0.415$ and $\log_2(0.25)=-2$:
$S\approx-0.75(-0.415)-0.25(-2)=0.311+0.5=0.811$ bits, noticeably less
than the maximally mixed qubit's 1 bit (since $0.75/0.25$ is less
"balanced" than $0.5/0.5$), but well above the pure-state minimum of 0.

## The diagonal is not the spectrum

<Callout type="warning">
**Reading entropy off ρ's diagonal entries directly.** As the
$\rho_1$-vs-$\rho_2$ example shows, this is only valid when $\rho$ is
already diagonal. In general, entropy needs $\rho$'s eigenvalues, which
can differ substantially from its diagonal entries.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing $S(\rho)=0$ with "nothing is known."** It is the opposite.
$S(\rho)=0$ means the state is known in full: one specific pure state.
Entropy measures residual uncertainty, so zero entropy is maximal
knowledge.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Find the von Neumann entropy of $\rho=\text{diag}(0.6,0.4)$. Then
   compute the same state's purity, and say which of the two numbers you
   would quote to a colleague as "this qubit carries so much uncertainty",
   and why.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The matrix is already diagonal, so its eigenvalues are its diagonal
   entries and the von Neumann entropy is the Shannon entropy of
   $(0.6, 0.4)$:

   $$
   S = -0.6\log_2 0.6 - 0.4\log_2 0.4 = 0.6(0.7370) + 0.4(1.3219) = 0.9710 \text{ bits}
   $$

   The engine returns $0.9709505944546686$. Purity is
   $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=0.36+0.16=0.52$.

   Quote the entropy. Its units are bits and they mean what bits usually
   mean: $0.971$ bits is the average number of yes-or-no answers needed to
   pin down which eigenstate the qubit is in, and it is directly comparable
   to a classical channel's capacity or to a compression rate. Purity's
   $0.52$ is a real number on a scale running from $1$ down to $\tfrac12$
   for a qubit, with no interpretation attached to any value in between.
   It is an excellent detector, since $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=1$ exactly
   characterizes purity, and a poor measure.

   The two are not independent, which is worth seeing. For a qubit both
   are functions of the eigenvalue pair, so each determines the other, and
   the engine's own `vonNeumannEntropy` computes the eigenvalues first for
   exactly that reason. The information is the same; the scale is not, and
   choosing a scale with an operational meaning is most of what makes a
   quantity useful.

   A close call worth noticing: $0.971$ bits is nearly the maximum of $1$,
   even though the state is a $60/40$ split rather than $50/50$. Entropy is
   flat near its peak, so a fairly lopsided distribution still carries
   almost maximal uncertainty. The slip is reading $0.971$ as "almost
   completely uncertain, so almost $I/2$": the purity of $0.52$, against
   $I/2$'s $0.5$, says the same thing more bluntly, but a state can be a
   long way from $I/2$ in the Bloch ball while its entropy is barely
   distinguishable.

   </details>

2. A qubit has density matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0.6&0.48\\0.48&0.4\end{pmatrix}$
   (Hermitian, trace 1, and positive semidefinite since
   $0.6\times0.4-0.48^2=0.0096\geq0$). Use `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2`'s
   formula to find its eigenvalues, and explain why its entropy is close
   to, but not exactly, 0. (Compare $0.48$ to the largest off-diagonal
   magnitude a *pure* state with this diagonal could have,
   $\sqrt{0.6\times0.4}\approx0.490$.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Apply the closed form with $a=0.6$, $d=0.4$, $|z|=0.48$:

   $$
   \lambda_\pm = \frac{0.6+0.4}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{0.6-0.4}{2}\right)^2 + 0.48^2} = 0.5 \pm \sqrt{0.01 + 0.2304} = 0.5 \pm 0.49031
   $$

   so $\lambda_+ = 0.99031$ and $\lambda_- = 0.00969$, matching
   `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` exactly. The entropy is

   $$
   S = -0.99031\log_2 0.99031 - 0.00969\log_2 0.00969 = 0.0139 + 0.0648 = 0.0788 \text{ bits},
   $$

   and the engine returns $0.07875748$.

   Small but nonzero, and the comparison in the hint says why. A pure state
   with diagonal $(0.6,0.4)$ would be
   $\sqrt{0.6}|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.4}|1\rangle$, whose coherence has magnitude
   $\sqrt{0.6\times0.4}=0.48990$. This state's coherence is $0.48$, about
   $2\%$ short. Coherence is what a pure state has the maximum of, so
   falling a little short of the maximum makes the state a little mixed,
   and $0.0788$ bits is that little. Its purity is $0.9808$, likewise just
   under $1$.

   The determinant is the cleanest way to see it. $\det\rho = \lambda_+\lambda_-$,
   and a pure state has $\lambda_-=0$, hence determinant zero. Here
   $\det\rho = 0.24-0.2304 = 0.0096$, small and positive, which is exactly
   what forces $\lambda_-$ to be small and positive rather than zero. The
   positivity condition the question quotes and the near-purity are the
   same inequality, read at different distances from its boundary.

   The wrong turn is to plug the diagonal $(0.6,0.4)$ into the entropy
   formula and report $0.971$ bits. That is question 1's answer, and it is
   more than twelve times too large. Von Neumann entropy needs the
   *eigenvalues*, and for a non-diagonal $\rho$ those are not the diagonal
   entries. The trap exists because the two coincide whenever $\rho$ is
   diagonal, which is most of the examples a course can show quickly.

   </details>

3. Explain in one sentence why $S(\rho)=1$ bit is the *maximum* possible
   entropy for a single qubit, using the constraint that both
   eigenvalues are nonnegative and sum to 1. Then say what the maximum is
   for a two-qubit density matrix, and which state reaches it.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The two eigenvalues form a probability distribution over two outcomes,
   and the Shannon entropy of a two-outcome distribution is maximized at
   the uniform one, $\lambda_1=\lambda_2=\tfrac12$, giving
   $S=-2\left(\tfrac12\log_2\tfrac12\right)=1$ bit; every other admissible
   pair is less uniform and gives less.

   The engine agrees: $S(I/2)=1$ exactly, and pushing the eigenvalues apart
   in either direction lowers it, to $0.971$ at $(0.6,0.4)$, to $0.722$ at
   $(0.8,0.2)$, and to $0$ at $(1,0)$.

   For $n$ qubits the same argument gives $\log_2 d$ with $d=2^n$, so a
   two-qubit density matrix maxes out at $2$ bits, reached uniquely by
   $I/4$: four eigenvalues, all equal to $\tfrac14$, and
   $-4\left(\tfrac14\log_2\tfrac14\right)=2$. In general an $n$-qubit state
   caps at $n$ bits, which is the reassuring statement that $n$ qubits
   cannot hide more than $n$ bits of this kind of uncertainty.

   That cap is what makes the entanglement-entropy convention sensible.
   $E(|\Phi^+\rangle)=1$ bit is a full unit because $S(\rho_A)$ is a single
   qubit's entropy and $1$ is its ceiling, so "one ebit" and "maximally
   entangled pair" are the same statement. And it is why a two-qubit
   reduced entropy of $2$ bits, which $I/4$ has, cannot be read as
   entanglement at all: the next lesson's counterexample turns on exactly
   this.

   The slip is arguing that $1$ bit is the maximum "because a qubit stores
   one bit". The two facts are related but the reasoning is backwards, and
   it gives the wrong answer as soon as the eigenvalues are not the object
   being counted. What the argument needs is that $S$ is the entropy of the
   eigenvalue distribution, that there are exactly $d$ eigenvalues, and
   that a distribution over $d$ outcomes cannot beat $\log_2 d$.

   </details>

4. Two different qubit density matrices could, in principle, have the
   same von Neumann entropy without being the same matrix. Give an
   example of two different $\rho$'s with the same entropy (Hint: entropy
   only depends on the *eigenvalues*, not on which basis they're
   eigenvalues in).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Take

   $$
   \rho_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0.6&0\\0&0.4\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.1\\0.1&0.5\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   These are visibly different matrices, disagreeing in every entry, and
   the engine confirms they are not equal to any tolerance. Yet
   $\rho_2$'s eigenvalues, from the closed form with $a=d=0.5$ and
   $|z|=0.1$, are $0.5\pm0.1$, that is $0.6$ and $0.4$. The same pair as
   $\rho_1$'s. So both have entropy $0.9709505944546686$, identical to the
   last digit the engine prints, and both have purity $0.52$.

   The recipe generalizes, and it is worth having. $\rho_2$ is the mixture
   "$|+\rangle$ with probability $0.6$, $|-\rangle$ with probability
   $0.4$", which is $\rho_1$'s recipe rewritten in the $X$ basis instead of
   the $Z$ basis. More generally $\rho_2 = U\rho_1U^\dagger$ for a unitary
   $U$, here the Hadamard, and conjugating by a unitary permutes nothing
   about the eigenvalues, only the eigenvectors they belong to. Every
   density matrix therefore sits in a whole family of states sharing its
   entropy: one for every choice of orthonormal eigenbasis.

   Physically, entropy measures *how much* uncertainty there is and is
   deliberately silent about *what* the uncertainty is about. $\rho_1$ is
   uncertain between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$; $\rho_2$ is uncertain
   between $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$; the amounts match and the
   directions do not. On the Bloch ball the two points sit at the same
   radius, $|\vec r|=0.2$, on different axes, and entropy is a function of
   the radius alone.

   This is also why unitary evolution cannot change entropy, a fact the
   next-but-one lesson proves directly: a gate rotates the Bloch ball and
   leaves radii alone. If you want to raise a state's entropy you must do
   something that is not a unitary on that system, such as entangling it
   with something else and looking away.

   </details>

## Summary

- A $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix's eigenvalues have an exact closed form
  (the quadratic formula), a solver specific to $2\times2$, not a
  general eigensolver.
- Von Neumann entropy $S(\rho)=-\sum_i\lambda_i\log_2\lambda_i$ reduces
  exactly to classical Shannon entropy when $\rho$ is diagonal.
- $S=0$ for every pure state; $S=1$ bit for the maximally mixed qubit:
  the two extremes, both derived and verified against the engine.
- Entropy must be computed from eigenvalues, not diagonal entries. Two
  matrices with identical diagonals can have opposite entropies.
