## Motivation

Purity, $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$, has done real work in the last two lessons
— detecting mixedness, and (for 2-qubit pure states) tying directly to
entanglement. But it isn't the measure physicists actually reach for
first. The standard one is **von Neumann entropy**, and this lesson
builds it properly: not as a new formula to accept, but as the direct
quantum generalization of an idea this platform already uses — Shannon
entropy of a classical probability distribution.

## Mathematical Development

### Eigenvalues of a density matrix

A Hermitian matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthonormal eigenbasis —
a fact this platform has used since Operators, Observables & Measurement.
For a $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix
$\rho=\begin{pmatrix}a&z\\z^*&d\end{pmatrix}$, the characteristic
polynomial $\lambda^2-(a+d)\lambda+(ad-|z|^2)=0$ has the closed-form
solution

$$
\lambda_{\pm} = \frac{a+d}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{a-d}{2}\right)^2+|z|^2}
$$

— the ordinary quadratic formula, nothing more exotic. This is exactly
what `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` computes. It is worth being explicit about
scope here: this is a closed-form solver for the $2\times2$ case
specifically, not a general eigensolver — finding eigenvalues of an
arbitrary $N\times N$ Hermitian matrix requires genuinely different
(generally iterative) numerical methods, which this platform does not
implement. Every density matrix this course actually needs (single-qubit
reduced states) is exactly $2\times2$, so the closed form is complete
for this course's scope.

For a valid density matrix, both eigenvalues are guaranteed
nonnegative (positivity) and sum to 1 (trace), so $\lambda_+,\lambda_-$
form a genuine probability distribution over exactly two outcomes.

### Von Neumann entropy

$$
S(\rho) = -\text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho) = -\sum_i \lambda_i\log_2\lambda_i
$$

The middle expression is the definition; the right-hand side is what it
equals once $\rho$ is diagonalized (the trace of a function of $\rho$
applied via its eigenvalues — a standard fact for Hermitian matrices,
usable here without re-deriving general functional calculus since the
$2\times2$ case reduces to explicit eigenvalues directly). By convention
$0\log_20\equiv0$ (the limit of $x\log_2x$ as $x\to0^+$), so a zero
eigenvalue contributes nothing rather than producing an undefined term
— `vonNeumannEntropy`'s implementation handles this directly rather than
letting it silently become `NaN`.

### This is exactly Shannon entropy, generalized

If $\rho$ happens to already be diagonal — $\rho=\text{diag}(p_1,\dots,p_d)$
for a genuine classical probability distribution $p_i$ — then the
eigenvalues *are* the $p_i$, and

$$
S(\rho) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i = H(p)
$$

exactly Shannon's classical entropy of the distribution $p$. Von Neumann
entropy is not a quantum analogue built by loose resemblance — for a
diagonal $\rho$, it is literally, term for term, the same formula. What
makes it quantum is that $\rho$ need not be diagonal at all, and the
eigenvalues (not the diagonal entries) are what the formula actually
needs — which is exactly where the subtlety in the next section comes
from.

### Two limits, verified

For any pure state, one eigenvalue is 1 and the other is 0 (proven in
Lesson 1's Hermitian-trace-1 discussion, made concrete by
`eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` returning exactly $(1,0)$ for
$\rho_{|0\rangle}$), so $S=-1\log_21-0\log_20=0$ — **zero entropy**, the
minimum, for every pure state without exception. For the maximally mixed
qubit $I/2$, both eigenvalues are $0.5$, giving
$S=-2(0.5\log_20.5)=-2(0.5)(-1)=1$ — **exactly 1 bit**, the maximum a
single qubit's density matrix can reach. Both values are computed
directly by `vonNeumannEntropy` and checked as regression tests.

### The diagonal-entries trap

Here is the subtlety the definition's phrasing was built to avoid.
Consider $\rho_1=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ (a classical 90/10 mixture) versus
$\rho_2=\begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.3\\0.3&0.1\end{pmatrix}$ — same diagonal
entries, different off-diagonal term. $\rho_1$'s eigenvalues are just its
diagonal entries, $0.9$ and $0.1$, giving $S(\rho_1)\approx0.469$ bits.
But $\rho_2$'s eigenvalues (via the quadratic formula, with
$a=0.9,d=0.1,z=0.3$) come out to exactly $1$ and $0$ — $\rho_2$ is
secretly a *pure* state, despite its diagonal looking identical to
$\rho_1$'s, and $S(\rho_2)=0$. Both values were computed directly from
the engine, not asserted. Reading entropy off the diagonal alone would
have silently given the wrong answer for $\rho_2$ — entropy is a
property of the full matrix (via its eigenvalues), never a shortcut
through the diagonal.

## Physical Interpretation

Entropy quantifies, in bits, how much genuine uncertainty remains about
which "definite state" a system is really in — zero for a pure state
(the system is definitely, unambiguously, in one specific quantum state)
and rising toward its maximum as the mixture spreads evenly across more
distinguishable possibilities. Because it's built from eigenvalues, it
is unchanged by which basis you happen to describe $\rho$ in — a
basis-independent, physically meaningful number, exactly the property a
good measure of "how mixed is this, really" should have.

## Worked Example

**A qubit is prepared as $|0\rangle$ with probability $0.75$ and
$|1\rangle$ with probability $0.25$. Find its von Neumann entropy.**

This mixture's density matrix is already diagonal:
$\rho=\text{diag}(0.75,0.25)$, so its eigenvalues are just $0.75,0.25$
themselves. $S=-0.75\log_2(0.75)-0.25\log_2(0.25)$. Using
$\log_2(0.75)\approx-0.415$ and $\log_2(0.25)=-2$:
$S\approx-0.75(-0.415)-0.25(-2)=0.311+0.5=0.811$ bits — noticeably less
than the maximally mixed qubit's 1 bit (since $0.75/0.25$ is less
"balanced" than $0.5/0.5$), but well above the pure-state minimum of 0.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Reading entropy off ρ's diagonal entries directly.** As the
$\rho_1$-vs-$\rho_2$ example shows, this is only valid when $\rho$ is
already diagonal. In general, entropy needs $\rho$'s eigenvalues, which
can differ substantially from its diagonal entries.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing $S(\rho)=0$ with "nothing is known."** It's the opposite:
$S(\rho)=0$ means the state is known *completely* (a specific pure
state) — entropy measures residual uncertainty, so zero entropy is
maximal knowledge, not minimal.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A $2\times2$ Hermitian matrix's eigenvalues have an exact closed form
  (the quadratic formula) — a solver specific to $2\times2$, not a
  general eigensolver.
- Von Neumann entropy $S(\rho)=-\sum_i\lambda_i\log_2\lambda_i$ reduces
  exactly to classical Shannon entropy when $\rho$ is diagonal.
- $S=0$ for every pure state; $S=1$ bit for the maximally mixed qubit —
  the two extremes, both derived and verified against the engine.
- Entropy must be computed from eigenvalues, not diagonal entries — two
  matrices with identical diagonals can have entirely different entropy.
