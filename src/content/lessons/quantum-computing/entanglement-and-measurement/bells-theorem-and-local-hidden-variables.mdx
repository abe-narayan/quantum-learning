## Thirty years with no way to decide

Einstein's belief, that measurement outcomes are fixed in advance by some
deeper layer of reality, was a reasonable position held by one of the
greatest physicists who ever lived. For thirty years nobody could think of
an experiment that would test it.

Here is the question Bell asked instead. Suppose Alice and Bob agree on
any strategy they like before separating: coin flips, a shared secret
code, an entire library of instructions distributed in advance. However
clever the strategy, is there a limit to how correlated their measurement
outcomes can be once they are too far apart to communicate?

There is, and it is a provable ceiling. No scheme of this kind, however
elaborate, can push a particular combination of four correlations past the
number 2. Quantum mechanics, checked against that bound in the next
lesson, reaches $2\sqrt2$.

Bell States and Entanglement, back in Quantum Gates & Circuits, already
showed something suggestive: measuring both qubits of $|\Phi^+\rangle$ in
the $X$ basis still gives perfect correlation. It was careful to say that
this on its own convicts nobody, since a pair of slips of paper carrying a
predetermined answer for $Z$ and for $X$ reproduces both results exactly.
What it promised instead was a bound on correlations measured across
several angles at once, which every predetermined description has to obey
and an entangled pair does not. This lesson supplies that bound, and its
proof. It is classical probability and
combinatorics, with no quantum mechanics in it anywhere, and that is the
point. The bound has to be established without reference to the theory it
will be used to test.

Bell was not the first to worry about this. Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen
raised the same concern three decades earlier, in their 1935 paper arguing
that quantum mechanics must be an incomplete description of reality. What
bothered Einstein was the instantaneous, correlated update of a distant
particle's state, which he dismissed in a 1947 letter to Max Born as
*spukhafte Fernwirkung*, spooky action at a distance.

Bell's paper appeared in November 1964 and then waited. It drew fewer than
a dozen citations in six years, and sat largely unnoticed until Alain
Aspect's experiments in the early 1980s put it to a laboratory test:
decades after Einstein's death, and decades after he had concluded quantum
mechanics had to be missing something.

## A cheat sheet handed out before they separate

Picture Alice and Bob's shared strategy as a cheat sheet, $\lambda$,
handed to both of them by the source before they separate. Each of them
looks only at their own measurement setting and their own copy of the
cheat sheet, never at what the other person chose or got. You might
guess that a rich enough cheat sheet could correlate their answers as
strongly as you like. It cannot. The combination of correlations defined
below, called $S$, is boxed in at magnitude 2 however the cheat sheet is
designed or distributed. The bound turns out to be even more rigid than
that, in a way worth predicting before you see it, and you can check the
rigid version exhaustively further down by stepping through all 16
deterministic cheat sheets one at a time.

## The model, the combination, and the bound

### The local hidden-variable model, precisely

Two experimenters, Alice and Bob, each hold one particle from a pair
produced by a common source. Alice chooses a <Term id="measurement">measurement</Term> setting
$a\in\{A,A'\}$ and gets an outcome $\pm1$; Bob independently chooses
$b\in\{B,B'\}$ and gets an outcome $\pm1$. A **local hidden-variable
(LHV) model** proposes: there is some variable $\lambda$ (possibly
unmeasurable, of any nature at all, "hidden") produced by the source
and shared by both particles, and each outcome is a definite,
predetermined function of $\lambda$ and *only that party's own setting*:

$$
\text{Alice's outcome} = A(a,\lambda) \in\{+1,-1\}, \qquad \text{Bob's outcome} = B(b,\lambda)\in\{+1,-1\}
$$

The word **local** refers specifically to $A(a,\lambda)$ not depending
on $b$, and $B(b,\lambda)$ not depending on $a$: whatever Bob chooses
to measure cannot affect Alice's predetermined outcome function, and
vice versa. This is the precise assumption <Term id="bells-theorem">Bell's theorem</Term> tests, not a
vague notion of "classical physics" in general.

### The CHSH combination, for a fixed λ

Define, for one fixed value of $\lambda$:

$$
S(\lambda) = A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda) + A(a,\lambda)B(b',\lambda) + A(a',\lambda)B(b,\lambda) - A(a',\lambda)B(b',\lambda)
$$

Before factoring this expression, predict how constrained it is. Four
terms, each a product of two numbers from $\{+1,-1\}$, so the naive
range is $-4$ to $+4$.

Factor by grouping the two terms that share $A(a,\lambda)$ and the two
that share $A(a',\lambda)$:

$$
S(\lambda) = A(a,\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)+B(b',\lambda)\big] + A(a',\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)-B(b',\lambda)\big]
$$

Since $B(b,\lambda),B(b',\lambda)\in\{+1,-1\}$, exactly one of two cases
holds: either they're equal (making $B(b,\lambda)+B(b',\lambda)=\pm2$
and $B(b,\lambda)-B(b',\lambda)=0$), or they're opposite (making the sum
$0$ and the difference $\pm2$). In the first case,
$S(\lambda)=\pm2A(a,\lambda)\in\{+2,-2\}$; in the second,
$S(\lambda)=\pm2A(a',\lambda)\in\{+2,-2\}$. Either way:

$$
|S(\lambda)| = 2 \quad\text{for every single } \lambda
$$

Note that this says $|S(\lambda)| = 2$, not $\le 2$. For any one fixed
$\lambda$, the combination hits $\pm2$ on the nose, because every outcome
is determined once $\lambda$ is fixed.

There are $2^4=16$ ways to assign $\pm1$ to
$A(a,\lambda),A(a',\lambda),B(b,\lambda),B(b',\lambda)$, which is to say
16 possible deterministic hidden variables. Step through all of them below
and watch the four correlation terms and their combination $S(\lambda)$.
Every one lands on $+2$ or $-2$.

### Averaging over λ gives the CHSH inequality

Real experiments don't control $\lambda$; it varies from trial to
trial according to some probability distribution $p(\lambda)$ set by the
source. The measurable correlation is
$E(a,b)=\int A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda)\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda$, and the
CHSH combination of *these* is:

$$
S = E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b') = \int S(\lambda)\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda
$$

Using $|S(\lambda)|=2$ for every $\lambda$, and that $p(\lambda)$ is a
genuine probability density ($p(\lambda)\geq0$, integrating to 1):

$$
|S| = \left|\int S(\lambda)p(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right| \leq \int|S(\lambda)|\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = \int 2\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = 2
$$

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "S", symbol: "S", gloss: "The CHSH combination of four measured correlations." },
  ]}
>
$$
\boxed{|S| \leq 2}
$$
</EquationReveal>

This is the **CHSH inequality**. Look at what went into it: (1) outcomes
are $\pm1$, (2) each party's outcome depends only on its own setting and
$\lambda$, and (3) $\lambda$ has *some* probability distribution. Nothing
about what $\lambda$ physically is, how complicated it may be, or how it
is distributed was ever needed.

That is what gives the bound its reach. It holds for every LHV model at
once, including ones nobody has invented, which is why measuring a
violation settles a question rather than refuting one model.

## What structure, exactly, is being ruled out

Bell's theorem does not merely show that "classical physics predicts
different numbers than quantum mechanics for this experiment." It
shows that *no* theory fitting the LHV structure above, regardless of
how exotic its hidden mechanism, can ever produce $|S|>2$. If an
experiment measures $|S|>2$, every local hidden-variable explanation is
simultaneously ruled out at once, not just whichever specific classical
model someone happened to write down.

**What this does not rule out:** hidden-variable theories that are
*nonlocal*, where $A(a,\lambda)$ is allowed to depend on Bob's distant
setting $b$ too. Bohmian mechanics is a real, actively-studied example
of just such a theory, and it is not excluded by Bell's theorem,
because it does not satisfy the locality assumption the proof above
depends on. Bell's theorem is a precise statement about *local*
determinism specifically, not determinism in general.

## One trivial deterministic model, scored

**Suppose a source always sends $\lambda$ such that
$A(A,\lambda)=A(A',\lambda)=+1$ and $B(B,\lambda)=B(B',\lambda)=+1$ for
every trial (a trivial, fully deterministic LHV model with no randomness
at all). Compute $S$.**

$E(a,b)=(+1)(+1)=1$ for every pair of settings, since the outcomes are
always $+1$ regardless of setting, with no $\lambda$-averaging needed (a
single deterministic case). So
$S=1+1+1-1=2$: right at the boundary, not exceeding it, just as the
inequality requires ($|S|\leq2$, and this trivial model saturates it
without violating it).

## Local is the word doing the work

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking Bell's theorem proves "there are no hidden variables."** It
proves something more specific and more powerful: no *local*
hidden-variable model can reach $|S|>2$. Nonlocal hidden-variable
theories are a separate category, untouched by this proof.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Believing $|S(\lambda)|=2$ for a fixed $\lambda$ contradicts
$|S|\le2$ for the averaged, measured $S$.** They're consistent: the
proof shows each individual $\lambda$ gives exactly $\pm2$, and
averaging values that are each exactly $\pm2$ can only ever produce
something with magnitude *at most* 2 (it can't average to something
outside the range of the values being averaged), never more.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Suppose $A(a,\lambda)=A(a',\lambda)=+1$ always, but
   $B(b,\lambda)=+1,B(b',\lambda)=-1$ always. Compute $S$ for this
   deterministic model and confirm it satisfies $|S|\leq2$. Then work out
   the complete set of values $S$ can take across all 16 deterministic
   strategies, and say what that set reveals about the bound.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Every correlation is a product of two fixed numbers here, so
   $E(a,b)=+1$, $E(a,b')=-1$, $E(a',b)=+1$, $E(a',b')=-1$, and

   $$
   S = 1 + (-1) + 1 - (-1) = 2 .
   $$

   $|S|=2$, satisfying the bound and in fact saturating it. The factored
   form makes it clearer:
   $S = A(B+B') + A'(B-B') = (1)(1-1) + (1)(1+1) = 0 + 2$.

   Enumerating all $16$ assignments of $\pm1$ to $A, A', B, B'$ gives a
   surprisingly thin set of outcomes: $S$ is $+2$ for eight of them and
   $-2$ for the other eight, and never anything else. There is no
   deterministic strategy scoring $0$, or $1.7$, or $1.999$.

   The reason is the factoring itself. $B+B'$ and $B-B'$ can never both be
   nonzero, since $B$ and $B'$ are each $\pm1$: if they agree, the
   difference vanishes and the sum is $\pm2$; if they disagree, the sum
   vanishes and the difference is $\pm2$. So exactly one of the two terms
   survives, multiplied by $A$ or $A'$, both of which are $\pm1$. The
   result is $\pm2$ with no other possibility.

   What this says about the bound is worth stating plainly. $|S|\leq2$ is
   not a loose estimate that a cleverer deterministic cheat sheet might
   creep up on. Every deterministic strategy is already at the ceiling.
   Values strictly inside $(-2,2)$ arise only by averaging over a
   distribution $p(\lambda)$ of strategies, and averaging over numbers all
   equal to $\pm2$ cannot escape $[-2,2]$: that convexity is the entire
   content of the theorem. Quantum mechanics reaching $2\sqrt2$ is
   therefore not "doing slightly better than the best cheat sheet". It is
   outside the range any average of cheat sheets can produce.

   The wrong turn is treating a specific model like this one as evidence
   for the bound. It is a single point in a set of $16$, and even
   enumerating all $16$ covers only the deterministic case; the proof has to
   handle every probability distribution over them at once, which is what
   the factoring argument does.

   </details>

2. In the factoring step, explain why exactly one of
   "$B(b,\lambda)=B(b',\lambda)$" or "$B(b,\lambda)\neq B(b',\lambda)$"
   must hold for any fixed $\lambda$, given that both outcomes are in
   $\{+1,-1\}$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because for a fixed $\lambda$ both $B(b,\lambda)$ and $B(b',\lambda)$
   are single, definite numbers drawn from a two-element set, and two
   definite numbers are either equal or unequal. The dichotomy is
   exhaustive, so at least one case holds, and exclusive, so not both. That
   is all the step needs.

   The load-bearing word is *definite*. An LHV model asserts that
   $B(b,\lambda)$ has a value for every setting $b$, whether or not Bob
   chose that setting, and whether or not anyone will ever measure it. Once
   both values exist as numbers, the case split is available, and it is
   what lets the proof write $B+B'=0$ or $B-B'=0$ and factor $S$ into a
   single surviving term.

   This is exactly where quantum mechanics declines to play. In the quantum
   description there is no joint value for two non-commuting observables:
   the outcome of measuring $B$ is not a function that also has an entry
   for what $B'$ would have given on the same run. So the dichotomy has
   nothing to apply to, the factoring step never happens, and $2\sqrt2$ is
   not blocked. Counterfactual definiteness, the assumption that unmeasured
   settings still have answers, is smuggled in right here, and it is one of
   the two assumptions a violation refutes.

   The wrong turn is answering "because $B$ has to be $+1$ or $-1$", which
   is true and is not the point. The two-element range matters for the
   later step where $B+B'$ or $B-B'$ equals $\pm2$; the dichotomy itself
   would work for any set of values at all. What the step actually needs is
   that both quantities exist simultaneously for the same $\lambda$.

   </details>

3. Why does the proof of $|S|\leq2$ never need to know the actual
   probability distribution $p(\lambda)$, beyond it being a valid
   probability distribution (nonnegative, integrates to 1)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the bound is established for each $\lambda$ separately, before
   any averaging happens. The factoring argument shows that the quantity

   $$
   A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda) + A(a,\lambda)B(b',\lambda) + A(a',\lambda)B(b,\lambda) - A(a',\lambda)B(b',\lambda)
   $$

   equals $\pm2$ for every single $\lambda$, whatever the four outcome
   functions are. $S$ is the $p$-weighted average of that quantity, and an
   average of numbers all lying in $[-2,2]$ lies in $[-2,2]$, for any
   nonnegative weights summing to one.

   That is the whole role of $p(\lambda)$: it has to be a genuine average
   rather than an arbitrary linear combination. Nonnegativity forbids
   cancelling a $+2$ against another $+2$ with a negative weight, and the
   normalization forbids scaling the answer up by using total weight
   greater than one. Given those two, nothing else about $p$ can matter.

   The generality this buys is the reason the theorem is a theorem and not a
   model comparison. $\lambda$ may be a single bit, a real number, a
   continuum of fields, or an entire library of pre-agreed instructions; it
   may be distributed in any way at all, including in ways nobody could
   ever measure. None of that changes an average of $\pm2$s. This is why a
   violation cannot be explained away by proposing a more elaborate hidden
   variable, and why experimentalists test the number rather than arguing
   about mechanisms.

   The wrong turn is imagining the proof needs $\lambda$ to be
   uniformly distributed, or finite, or independent of the settings. It
   needs none of those. Independence of $p(\lambda)$ from the settings *is*
   a real assumption of the setup, but it enters earlier, in writing the
   same $p$ for all four correlation terms, and it is the freedom-of-choice
   assumption a real experiment has to close by other means.

   </details>

4. A friend proposes a hidden-variable model where Bob's outcome
   function is allowed to depend on Alice's setting $a$ as well as
   Bob's own setting $b$. Explain, citing the specific step in the proof
   above that would break, why this model is not covered by the CHSH
   inequality proven here. Then say how large $S$ could get in such a
   model, and what actually rules it out.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The step that breaks is the factoring. The proof writes
   $S(\lambda) = A(B+B') + A'(B-B')$, which requires the *same* two numbers
   $B$ and $B'$ to appear in all four correlation terms. If Bob's outcomes
   are $B(b,a,\lambda)$, then the $B$ appearing in $E(a,b)$ and the $B$
   appearing in $E(a',b)$ are different quantities, there is nothing common
   to factor out, and the argument stops on its first line. Locality is
   not an extra assumption bolted onto the proof; it is the thing that
   makes $B$ writable without an $a$ in it.

   With that freedom, $S$ can reach $4$, the algebraic maximum. Let Bob's
   device return the value that makes each term favourable: outcomes
   agreeing with Alice's for the three terms that enter with a plus sign
   and disagreeing for the one that enters with a minus. Then
   $E(a,b)=E(a,b')=E(a',b)=+1$ and $E(a',b')=-1$, so $S=1+1+1+1=4$. Since
   each correlation is bounded by $1$ in magnitude, no model of any kind
   can do better, and quantum mechanics reaching only $2\sqrt2\approx2.83$
   is itself a fact in need of explanation, known as Tsirelson's bound.

   What rules the model out is not a Bell test. It is relativity, plus the
   experimental fact that no signal has ever been observed on this channel.
   A device whose output depends on a distant setting can be used to send a
   message instantly, simply by having Alice choose $a$ or $a'$ and letting
   Bob read the shift in his own marginal statistics. Quantum mechanics
   never permits that: whatever Alice does, Bob's local distribution is
   fixed, which is the no-signaling property this pillar has appealed to
   since teleportation.

   So the honest summary is that a CHSH violation refutes the conjunction
   of locality and definite pre-existing values, and does not by itself say
   which one to give up. Non-local hidden variables remain logically
   available, and Bohmian mechanics is a worked example of one, and they
   pay for it by needing an explanation of why the non-locality is
   permanently hidden from view.

   The wrong turn is calling the friend's model "already excluded by the
   experiments". The experiments exclude local models. This one is
   excluded by a different argument, and knowing which argument does which
   job is most of what the theorem is for.

   </details>

## Summary

- A local hidden-variable (LHV) model has each party's outcome
  determined by a shared variable $\lambda$ and *only that party's own*
  setting: this locality assumption is precise and essential to the
  proof.
- For any fixed $\lambda$, the CHSH combination $S(\lambda)$ is exactly
  $\pm2$, a direct consequence of both parties' outcomes being $\pm1$.
- Averaging over $\lambda$'s distribution can only ever produce
  $|S|\leq2$: the CHSH inequality, proven for every LHV model without
  exception.
- Bell's theorem rules out *local* hidden-variable theories specifically;
  it says nothing about nonlocal hidden-variable theories like Bohmian
  mechanics.

## Try It Yourself

one question the proof above says nothing about, because it is a question
