Einstein believed quantum mechanics had to be incomplete: that a measurement outcome was fixed in advance by some deeper layer of reality, not left genuinely open until someone looked. In 1964 John Bell found a way to settle the question — not with an argument, but with a number.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

It was a
reasonable position, held by one of the greatest physicists who ever
lived.

Suppose Alice and Bob agree on any strategy they like before separating:
coin flips, a shared secret code, an entire library of instructions
distributed in advance. However clever that strategy, is there a limit
to how correlated their measurement outcomes can be, once they're too
far apart to communicate? There is, and it's a hard, provable ceiling.
No scheme of this kind, however elaborate, can push a specific
combination of four correlations past the number 2. Quantum mechanics,
checked against this exact bound in the next lesson, blows straight
through it, all the way to $2\sqrt2$: a number Einstein's picture of
reality has no way to produce.

Bell States and Entanglement, back in Quantum Gates & Circuits, already
showed something suggestive: measuring both qubits of $|\Phi^+\rangle$
in the $X$-basis still gives perfect correlation, and promised that no
*predetermined* classical description of the two qubits could reproduce
that in full generality. This lesson delivers the proof. It's entirely
classical probability and combinatorics; no quantum mechanics appears
anywhere in it, which is the point. It establishes a hard limit that
*any* local, predetermined theory must obey, before quantum mechanics is
checked against that limit next.

Bell was not the first to worry about this. Einstein, Podolsky, and
Rosen raised the same concern three decades earlier, in their 1935 paper
arguing that quantum mechanics must be an incomplete description of
reality; what specifically bothered Einstein was the instantaneous,
correlated update of a distant particle's state, which he dismissed in a
1947 letter to Max Born as *spukhafte Fernwirkung* ("spooky action at a
distance"). Bell's own proof below, submitted to the short-lived journal
*Physics Physique Fizika* in November 1964, drew fewer than a dozen
citations in its first six years and sat largely unnoticed until Alain
Aspect's experiments in the early 1980s put it to an actual experimental
test — decades after Einstein's death, and decades after he had
concluded quantum mechanics simply had to be missing something. Full
citation: J. S. Bell, "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox," *Physics
Physique Fizika* **1**(3), 195–200 (1964).

## Intuition

Picture Alice and Bob's shared strategy as a cheat sheet, $\lambda$,
handed to both of them by the source before they separate. Each of them
looks only at their own measurement setting and their own copy of the
cheat sheet, never at what the other person chose or got. You might
guess a rich enough cheat sheet could correlate their answers as
strongly as you like. It can't: the specific combination of
correlations defined below, called $S$, turns out to be boxed in at
magnitude 2 no matter how the cheat sheet is designed or distributed.
You can check this exhaustively yourself further down, stepping through
all 16 possible deterministic cheat sheets and watching $S$ land on
exactly $+2$ or $-2$, every single time.

## Mathematical Development

### The local hidden-variable model, precisely

Two experimenters, Alice and Bob, each hold one particle from a pair
produced by a common source. Alice chooses a <Term id="measurement">measurement</Term> setting
$a\in\{A,A'\}$ and gets an outcome $\pm1$; Bob independently chooses
$b\in\{B,B'\}$ and gets an outcome $\pm1$. A **local hidden-variable
(LHV) model** proposes: there is some variable $\lambda$ (possibly
unmeasurable, of any nature at all, "hidden") produced by the source
and shared by both particles, and each outcome is a definite,
predetermined function of $\lambda$ and *only that party's own setting*:

$$
\text{Alice's outcome} = A(a,\lambda) \in\{+1,-1\}, \qquad \text{Bob's outcome} = B(b,\lambda)\in\{+1,-1\}
$$

The word **local** refers specifically to $A(a,\lambda)$ not depending
on $b$, and $B(b,\lambda)$ not depending on $a$: whatever Bob chooses
to measure cannot affect Alice's predetermined outcome function, and
vice versa. This is the precise assumption <Term id="bells-theorem">Bell's theorem</Term> tests, not a
vague notion of "classical physics" in general.

### The CHSH combination, for a fixed λ

Define, for one fixed value of $\lambda$:

$$
S(\lambda) = A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda) + A(a,\lambda)B(b',\lambda) + A(a',\lambda)B(b,\lambda) - A(a',\lambda)B(b',\lambda)
$$

Before factoring this expression, predict how constrained it actually is.

Factor by grouping the two terms that share $A(a,\lambda)$ and the two
that share $A(a',\lambda)$:

$$
S(\lambda) = A(a,\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)+B(b',\lambda)\big] + A(a',\lambda)\big[B(b,\lambda)-B(b',\lambda)\big]
$$

Since $B(b,\lambda),B(b',\lambda)\in\{+1,-1\}$, exactly one of two cases
holds: either they're equal (making $B(b,\lambda)+B(b',\lambda)=\pm2$
and $B(b,\lambda)-B(b',\lambda)=0$), or they're opposite (making the sum
$0$ and the difference $\pm2$). In the first case,
$S(\lambda)=\pm2A(a,\lambda)\in\{+2,-2\}$; in the second,
$S(\lambda)=\pm2A(a',\lambda)\in\{+2,-2\}$. Either way:

$$
|S(\lambda)| = 2 \quad\text{for every single } \lambda
$$

not merely "at most 2": for any *one* fixed $\lambda$, this
combination is always exactly $\pm2$, since every outcome is
deterministic once $\lambda$ is fixed.

There are exactly $2^4=16$ ways to assign $\pm1$ to
$A(a,\lambda),A(a',\lambda),B(b,\lambda),B(b',\lambda)$ (one full
"deterministic hidden variable"). Step through all 16 below and watch
the four correlation terms and their combination $S(\lambda)$: every
single one lands at exactly $+2$ or $-2$, never anything in between and
never anything outside that pair of values.

### Averaging over λ gives the CHSH inequality

Real experiments don't control $\lambda$; it varies from trial to
trial according to some probability distribution $p(\lambda)$ set by the
source. The measurable correlation is
$E(a,b)=\int A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda)\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda$, and the
CHSH combination of *these* is:

$$
S = E(a,b)+E(a,b')+E(a',b)-E(a',b') = \int S(\lambda)\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda
$$

Using $|S(\lambda)|=2$ for every $\lambda$, and that $p(\lambda)$ is a
genuine probability density ($p(\lambda)\geq0$, integrating to 1):

$$
|S| = \left|\int S(\lambda)p(\lambda)\,d\lambda\right| \leq \int|S(\lambda)|\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = \int 2\,p(\lambda)\,d\lambda = 2
$$

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "S", symbol: "S", gloss: "The CHSH combination of four measured correlations." },
  ]}
>
$$
\boxed{|S| \leq 2}
$$
</EquationReveal>

This is the **CHSH inequality**. It was derived using nothing but: (1)
outcomes are $\pm1$, (2) each party's outcome depends only on its own
setting and $\lambda$, and (3) $\lambda$ has *some* probability
distribution. No assumption about what $\lambda$ physically is, how
complicated it can be, or how it's distributed was ever needed. The
bound holds for every LHV model without exception, which is exactly why
a violation of it (checked with the actual engine in the next lesson) is
so significant.

## Physical Interpretation

Bell's theorem does not merely show that "classical physics predicts
different numbers than quantum mechanics for this experiment." It
shows that *no* theory fitting the LHV structure above, regardless of
how exotic its hidden mechanism, can ever produce $|S|>2$. If an
experiment measures $|S|>2$, every local hidden-variable explanation is
simultaneously ruled out at once, not just whichever specific classical
model someone happened to write down.

**What this does not rule out:** hidden-variable theories that are
*nonlocal*, where $A(a,\lambda)$ is allowed to depend on Bob's distant
setting $b$ too. Bohmian mechanics is a real, actively-studied example
of exactly such a theory, and it is not excluded by Bell's theorem,
because it does not satisfy the locality assumption the proof above
depends on. Bell's theorem is a precise statement about *local*
determinism specifically, not determinism in general.

## Worked Example

**Suppose a source always sends $\lambda$ such that
$A(A,\lambda)=A(A',\lambda)=+1$ and $B(B,\lambda)=B(B',\lambda)=+1$ for
every trial (a trivial, fully deterministic LHV model with no randomness
at all). Compute $S$.**

$E(a,b)=(+1)(+1)=1$ for every pair of settings, since the outcomes are
always $+1$ regardless of setting, with no $\lambda$-averaging needed (a
single deterministic case). So
$S=1+1+1-1=2$: right at the boundary, not exceeding it, exactly as the
inequality requires ($|S|\leq2$, and this trivial model saturates it
without violating it).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking Bell's theorem proves "there are no hidden variables."** It
proves something more specific and more powerful: no *local*
hidden-variable model can reach $|S|>2$. Nonlocal hidden-variable
theories are a separate category, untouched by this proof.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Believing $|S(\lambda)|=2$ for a fixed $\lambda$ contradicts
$|S|\le2$ for the averaged, measured $S$.** They're consistent: the
proof shows each individual $\lambda$ gives exactly $\pm2$, and
averaging values that are each exactly $\pm2$ can only ever produce
something with magnitude *at most* 2 (it can't average to something
outside the range of the values being averaged), never more.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A local hidden-variable (LHV) model has each party's outcome
  determined by a shared variable $\lambda$ and *only that party's own*
  setting: this locality assumption is precise and essential to the
  proof.
- For any fixed $\lambda$, the CHSH combination $S(\lambda)$ is exactly
  $\pm2$, a direct consequence of both parties' outcomes being $\pm1$.
- Averaging over $\lambda$'s distribution can only ever produce
  $|S|\leq2$: the CHSH inequality, proven for every LHV model without
  exception.
- Bell's theorem rules out *local* hidden-variable theories specifically;
  it says nothing about nonlocal hidden-variable theories like Bohmian
  mechanics.
