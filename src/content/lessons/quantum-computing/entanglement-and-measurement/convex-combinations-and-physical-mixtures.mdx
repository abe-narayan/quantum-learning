## Not a coincidence about those four states

The last lesson's worked example, where 50/50 mixtures of two different
bases both produced $I/2$, was not a coincidence about those four states.
It is the general rule, and it deserves stating on its own rather than
being left as a curiosity: **different physical preparation procedures can
produce states no experiment can tell apart.**

Take that seriously for a moment. Two laboratories run visibly different
protocols, using different equipment and different basis states, and hand
you qubits. There is no measurement, however clever, that distinguishes
one lab's output from the other's. That is a claim about what a quantum
state is, and this lesson proves it.

## The general mixture, and when two ensembles collide

### The general mixture

For any finite set of pure states $|\psi_1\rangle,\dots,|\psi_n\rangle$
(not necessarily orthogonal, not necessarily related in any way) with
probabilities $p_1,\dots,p_n\geq0$ summing to 1:

$$
\rho = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|
$$

This is last lesson's two-component construction with $n$ terms instead of
2. The engine's `convexCombination` implements it, checking both
constraints (probabilities nonnegative, summing to 1) and rejecting a call
that violates either. That check is not pedantry: a set of "probabilities"
that does not sum to 1, or that includes a negative entry, corresponds to
no physical ensemble, and the function refuses to hand back a matrix for
it.

### Two different ensembles, the same ρ, proven in general

Take the $Z$-basis ensemble $\{(\tfrac12,|0\rangle),(\tfrac12,|1\rangle)\}$
and the $X$-basis ensemble $\{(\tfrac12,|+\rangle),(\tfrac12,|-\rangle)\}$.
Both give $\rho=I/2$, computed in the last lesson. This generalizes: for
*any* <Term id="orthonormal-basis">orthonormal basis</Term> $\{|a\rangle,|b\rangle\}$ of a qubit,

$$
\tfrac12|a\rangle\langle a| + \tfrac12|b\rangle\langle b| = \tfrac12(|a\rangle\langle a|+|b\rangle\langle b|) = \tfrac12 I
$$

The last equality is the **completeness relation**,
$|a\rangle\langle a|+|b\rangle\langle b|=I$ for any orthonormal basis.
This course has not needed it before, so here it is in one line. Any
qubit state expands in the basis as
$|v\rangle=\langle a|v\rangle|a\rangle+\langle b|v\rangle|b\rangle$, and
applying the left-hand side to that same $|v\rangle$ gives
$|a\rangle\langle a|v\rangle+|b\rangle\langle b|v\rangle$, which is the
identical expansion. An operator that returns every vector unchanged is
the identity, so the two sides are equal as operators. (The Bra-Ket
Formalism, in Mathematical Foundations, derives the same relation for a
space of any dimension, if you want the general version.) So
*every* orthonormal basis, mixed 50/50, gives $I/2$: infinitely many
different physical recipes ("flip a fair coin, prepare $|0\rangle$ or
$|1\rangle$"; "flip a fair coin, prepare $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$"; "flip
a fair coin, prepare $|{+i}\rangle$ or $|{-i}\rangle$"; ...), all producing
the identical <Term id="density-matrices">density matrix</Term>.

### Why this matters: ρ is the complete physical description

If two different preparation recipes yield the same $\rho$, then by
$\langle A\rangle=\text{Tr}(\rho A)$ (Lesson 1) they give *identical*
predictions for every <Term id="observable">observable</Term>, every measurement, every experiment,
with no exception. No experiment reveals which recipe was used. This is
not a limitation of current technology; it is a statement about what
"physical state" *means* in quantum mechanics. The ensemble, the story
about coins and preparation, is not part of the physics. Only $\rho$ is,
and two ensembles producing the same $\rho$ are the same state.

Classical probability does not behave this way. A distribution keeps its
labels: "70% heads" and "30% heads" are different distributions, and
mixtures over distinguishable classical outcomes do not collide the way
these do. The quantum mixture forgets its preparation recipe across a
whole continuum of bases, and there is no classical analogue to compare
that to.

## A question about physics, or about bookkeeping

Given a density matrix $\rho$, asking "but which ensemble was it *really*
prepared from?" is not a question quantum mechanics declines to answer. It
is a question quantum mechanics says is not about physics. $\rho$ is the
state, and the description ends there.

That reframes what a density matrix is. Not a record of an experimenter's
uncertainty about some hidden true ket, but the complete physical
description, on the same footing $|\psi\rangle$ holds for pure states.

## One three-component ensemble, checked and computed

**Show that the ensemble $\{(0.5,|0\rangle),(0.25,|1\rangle),(0.25,|+\rangle)\}$
is a valid mixture (i.e., that its probabilities are legitimate), and
compute its density matrix.**

Probabilities: $0.5+0.25+0.25=1$, all nonnegative: a valid ensemble
(three components this time, not two; the formalism doesn't care).

$$
\rho = 0.5\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + 0.25\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} + 0.25\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}0.625&0.125\\0.125&0.375\end{pmatrix}
$$

Unlike the earlier examples, this one is *not* $I/2$: a nonuniform
mixture of non-mutually-orthogonal states has no reason to collapse to
the maximally mixed state, and it doesn't here (verified directly
against `convexCombination`'s output).

## Components need not be orthogonal

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming a mixture's components must be orthogonal.** They don't have
to be: the worked example above mixes $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$, and
$|+\rangle$, and $|+\rangle$ is not orthogonal to either of the other
two. The only requirement is that the probabilities are nonnegative and
sum to 1.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing $\rho$ "secretly remembers" which ensemble prepared it.** It
does not, and that is this lesson's whole point. Two ensembles giving the
same $\rho$ are the same physical state, not two states that happen to be
hard to tell apart.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify the completeness relation $|{+i}\rangle\langle{+i}|+|{-i}\rangle\langle{-i}|=I$
   for the $Y$-eigenbasis $|{+i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$,
   $|{-i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)$, and conclude that
   a 50/50 mixture of $|{+i}\rangle,|{-i}\rangle$ also gives $I/2$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Build each projector. Remember the bra conjugates, so $|{+i}\rangle$'s
   second component $i$ becomes $-i$ in $\langle{+i}|$:

   $$
   |{+i}\rangle\langle{+i}| = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1\\i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\end{pmatrix} = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |{-i}\rangle\langle{-i}| = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   Adding them, the diagonals give $\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$ twice and the
   off-diagonals cancel in conjugate pairs, $-\tfrac{i}{2}+\tfrac{i}{2}=0$:

   $$
   |{+i}\rangle\langle{+i}|+|{-i}\rangle\langle{-i}| = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = I
   $$

   The engine returns exactly the identity for this sum, and the 50/50
   mixture, which is half of it, comes back as $\mathrm{diag}(0.5,0.5)$.
   That conclusion is one line: a mixture with equal weights is
   $\tfrac12(|{+i}\rangle\langle{+i}|+|{-i}\rangle\langle{-i}|)=\tfrac12 I$.

   Notice what actually did the work. The two off-diagonal entries are
   complex conjugates of each other with opposite signs, so they cancel,
   and the diagonals are equal, so they add to one. Nothing about the
   specific value $i$ mattered; any orthonormal pair does the same thing,
   which is the general statement the lesson proves. This is the third
   distinct recipe for $I/2$ after the $Z$ and $X$ bases, and there are
   infinitely many more, one for every axis through the Bloch sphere.

   The slip to watch for is dropping the conjugate and writing
   $\langle{+i}| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, i)$. That produces a matrix with
   $+i$ in both off-diagonal slots, which is not Hermitian and therefore
   not a projector at all, and the two "projectors" would then fail to sum
   to $I$. The conjugate is what makes $|v\rangle\langle v|$ Hermitian for
   complex $|v\rangle$.

   </details>

2. Compute the density matrix for the ensemble
   $\{(0.6,|0\rangle),(0.4,|1\rangle)\}$, and compare it to
   $\{(0.6,|0\rangle),(0.1,|1\rangle),(0.3,|1\rangle)\}$ (splitting the
   $|1\rangle$ weight into two identical-state terms). Explain why these
   two ensembles must give the same $\rho$ even though they list a
   different number of components.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Both give

   $$
   \rho = \begin{pmatrix}0.6&0\\0&0.4\end{pmatrix},
   $$

   confirmed by running `convexCombination` on each list.

   The reason is that the defining sum $\rho=\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$
   is linear in the weights, so two terms carrying the same state simply
   add their coefficients:
   $0.1\,|1\rangle\langle1| + 0.3\,|1\rangle\langle1| = 0.4\,|1\rangle\langle1|$.
   The three-term list is the two-term list with one entry written as a sum,
   and matrix addition does not care how a coefficient was assembled. Both
   ensembles are valid, since $0.6+0.1+0.3=1$ with all weights nonnegative.

   This is a small instance of the lesson's central result, and a useful one
   because the two ensembles really are different procedures. Imagine the
   second one implemented as "roll a die: on 1 through 6 of a ten-sided
   roll prepare $|0\rangle$, on 7 prepare $|1\rangle$, on 8 through 10
   prepare $|1\rangle$ from a different machine". The lab notebooks differ,
   the equipment differs, and the physics does not. Where the lesson's
   $Z$-versus-$X$ example makes the point with genuinely different states,
   this one makes it with genuinely different bookkeeping, and the
   formalism is equally indifferent to both.

   The wrong turn is expecting a three-component ensemble to be somehow
   "more mixed" than a two-component one, on the intuition that more
   possibilities means more uncertainty. Both have purity
   $0.36+0.16=0.52$ and both have von Neumann entropy $0.971$ bits. The
   number of terms in the list is a property of the description, and the
   entropy is a property of $\rho$, which has forgotten the list entirely.

   </details>

3. Why does `convexCombination` reject an ensemble with probabilities
   $\{0.7,0.5\}$ (summing to 1.2) even though both are individually valid
   probabilities?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because no physical preparation procedure produces those weights. The
   $p_i$ are the probabilities of mutually exclusive, exhaustive
   possibilities for what the source prepared, so they must sum to exactly
   $1$. A total of $1.2$ says the machine prepares something $120\%$ of the
   time, which is not a statement about a system. The engine refuses with
   `convexCombination requires probabilities summing to 1, got 1.2`.

   The consequence in the matrix is concrete rather than philosophical. The
   trace of the result would be $\sum_i p_i = 1.2$, not $1$, so
   $\text{Tr}(\rho P)$ over a complete set of projectors would sum to
   $1.2$: measurement probabilities adding to more than one. Every formula
   downstream, expectation values, purity thresholds, entropy, is built on
   $\text{Tr}(\rho)=1$, so a matrix that violates it silently poisons all
   of them. Rejecting at construction is cheaper than debugging an entropy
   of $1.15$ bits three lessons later.

   The check has two halves and both are needed. Negative weights are
   rejected as well, and they are the more insidious failure: an ensemble
   like $\{1.5, -0.5\}$ sums to $1$ and would pass a trace test, yet it can
   produce a matrix with a negative eigenvalue, meaning a negative
   probability for some measurement outcome. Nonnegative and summing to one
   are exactly the two conditions that make the combination *convex*, which
   is what guarantees the result lands inside the Bloch ball rather than
   outside it.

   The wrong turn is thinking the constraint is a normalization convention
   that could be fixed by dividing through by $1.2$. Rescaling does produce
   a valid density matrix, $\{0.583, 0.417\}$, but it is a different
   ensemble from the one that was asked for, and silently substituting it
   would hide a genuine error in the caller's reasoning about their own
   experiment.

   </details>

4. Two experimenters prepare qubits by different physical processes but
   both end up with $\rho=I/2$. Can any measurement, however precise,
   determine which process was used on a given qubit? Justify your answer
   using this lesson's central result. Then say what *would* distinguish
   the two procedures, and why that thing is not a property of the qubit.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No measurement on the qubit can. Every prediction quantum mechanics
   makes about it is computed from $\rho$ through
   $\langle A\rangle=\text{Tr}(\rho A)$ or $\text{Tr}(\rho P)$, and both
   experimenters hand you the same $\rho$. Identical input, identical
   output, for every observable in every basis, on any number of copies.
   The ensemble, the coins, the equipment: none of it appears anywhere in
   the formalism after $\rho$ is formed.

   What does distinguish the procedures is a record, and the record is not
   part of the qubit. Suppose the first experimenter wrote down which of
   $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ she prepared and the second wrote down which of
   $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$ he prepared. Those notes are correlated with their
   respective qubits in different ways, and an experiment on the *pair*,
   note plus qubit, separates the two situations easily: reading note one
   and then measuring in the $Z$ basis gives perfect agreement, while
   reading note two and measuring in $Z$ gives a coin flip. The
   distinguishing information exists; it lives in a second system.

   That is the honest resolution, and it says something about what a
   reduced description is. $I/2$ is what you have when you hold the qubit
   and not the record. Hand over the record too and the joint object is no
   longer $I/2$ on its own; it is a correlated two-system state, and $I/2$
   is what you get by tracing the record away, which is the operation the
   next lesson builds. Destroy the record and there is nothing left to
   find, in principle and not merely in practice.

   The reasoning to avoid is "one of them is really $|0\rangle$ or
   $|1\rangle$, so the fact of the matter exists and we simply cannot get
   at it". Once the record is gone, the two preparations are the same state
   in the strongest sense the theory allows, and treating the ensemble as a
   hidden fact about the qubit predicts a distinguishing experiment that
   provably does not exist. Also worth avoiding is the opposite
   overcorrection, that nothing anywhere could ever tell them apart: the
   record does, which is why the qubit alone does not.

   </details>

## Summary

- A general mixture is $\rho=\sum_ip_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$ for
  any number of components, with $p_i\geq0$ summing to 1; the components
  need not be orthogonal.
- Every orthonormal basis, mixed with equal weight, produces $I/2$, a
  direct consequence of the completeness relation.
- Different ensembles producing the same $\rho$ are physically identical:
  every observable's expectation value depends on $\rho$ alone, so no
  experiment can distinguish them.
- $\rho$ is the complete physical description of a quantum state; the
  preparation ensemble is not independently physically meaningful.
