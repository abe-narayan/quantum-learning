ρ can be evolved by a gate and measured, the way a state vector can, and with the same formulas. The difference shows up in one case a state vector cannot reach: measuring a state that has no state vector at all.

## Fixed states are not enough

Every density-matrix tool built so far in this course has described a
*fixed* state, sitting still. Real qubits get acted on by gates and
measured, so the formalism needs both operations before it is usable.

Neither requires a new postulate. Both fall out of the state-vector rules
you already have, extended to mixtures by linearity, and the derivations
below are four lines each. The payoff is at the end, where the same
formulas keep working on $I/2$, a state no ket describes.

## Evolution and the Born rule, both by linearity

### Unitary evolution: ρ' = UρU†

For a pure state, $|\psi\rangle\to U|\psi\rangle$ under a gate $U$.
Its density matrix transforms as:

$$
\rho' = (U|\psi\rangle)(U|\psi\rangle)^\dagger = U|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|U^\dagger = U\rho U^\dagger
$$

For a mixture $\rho=\sum_ip_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$, every
component evolves under the same $U$ independently, and the sum stays
linear in each $|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|\to U|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|U^\dagger$
term:

$$
\rho' = \sum_i p_i\, U|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|U^\dagger = U\left(\sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|\right)U^\dagger = U\rho U^\dagger
$$

The same formula holds for *any* valid density matrix, mixed or pure:
not a new postulate, but a direct consequence of every component obeying
the ordinary Schrödinger-picture evolution rule. Trace is preserved
($\text{Tr}(U\rho U^\dagger)=\text{Tr}(\rho U^\dagger U)=\text{Tr}(\rho)$
by cyclic trace and $U^\dagger U=I$), and <Term id="hermitian-operator">Hermiticity</Term> is preserved
directly ($(U\rho U^\dagger)^\dagger=U\rho^\dagger U^\dagger=U\rho U^\dagger$
since $\rho^\dagger=\rho$), so $\rho'$ is guaranteed to still be a valid
density matrix. `evolveDensityMatrix` implements exactly $U\rho U^\dagger$,
and this module's tests confirm trace, Hermiticity, purity, *and*
entropy are all unchanged by evolution under $X,Y,Z,H$: unitary
evolution can rotate a mixture around, but it cannot make it more or
less mixed.

### The generalized Born rule

For a projector $P$ onto some measurement outcome, the ordinary <Term id="born-rule">Born
rule</Term> is $p=\langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle$. By the same trace trick as
Lesson 1:

$$
p = \langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle = \text{Tr}(P|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|) = \text{Tr}(P\rho) = \text{Tr}(\rho P)
$$

For a mixture, linearity again extends this immediately:
$p=\sum_ip_i\langle\psi_i|P|\psi_i\rangle=\sum_ip_i\text{Tr}(P\rho_i)=\text{Tr}(P\rho)$
using $\rho=\sum_ip_i\rho_i$. So $p=\text{Tr}(\rho P)$ is the *same*
formula for pure and mixed states alike. `densityMatrixMeasurementProbability`
implements exactly this, and agrees with the ordinary
`StateVector`-based Born rule to numerical precision for every pure
state tested (a direct regression test, not a one-off check).

### Collapse

For a pure state, measuring outcome $P$ <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term>
$|\psi\rangle\to\frac{P|\psi\rangle}{\sqrt p}$. Its density matrix:

$$
\rho_{\text{after}} = \frac{P|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|P^\dagger}{p} = \frac{P\rho P}{p}
$$

(using $P^\dagger=P$, since projectors are Hermitian, and $p=\text{Tr}(P\rho)$
from above). `densityMatrixCollapse` implements exactly $P\rho P/p$, and
for a pure state this reproduces the ordinary state-vector collapse
exactly, checked directly in this module's tests.

### Measuring a genuinely mixed state

Here is the case with no state-vector equivalent at all. Take
$\rho=I/2$, the maximally mixed qubit, and measure the computational
basis projector $P_0=|0\rangle\langle0|$. Before working it out, predict
both numbers: the probability of outcome 0, and what the state becomes
afterward.

$$
p_0 = \text{Tr}(P_0\,\rho) = \text{Tr}\!\left(|0\rangle\langle0|\cdot\tfrac12I\right) = \tfrac12
$$

Using $P_0 I P_0 = P_0$ (a projector times itself is itself):

$$
\rho_{\text{after}} = \frac{P_0\,(I/2)\,P_0}{0.5} = \frac{\tfrac12 P_0}{0.5} = P_0 = |0\rangle\langle0|
$$

So after outcome 0, the state collapses to the pure state
$|0\rangle\langle0|$, and it does so with probability $0.5$. There is no
$|\psi\rangle$ this calculation could have used instead. $I/2$ has no state
vector, and yet its measurement statistics and its post-measurement state
are both well-defined, and both match what intuition says: measuring a
totally random qubit in the computational basis gives 0 or 1 on even odds,
and afterward you know which one you got.

Laid out as two short pipelines, evolution and measurement are each a
handful of steps, not a wall of algebra to re-derive every time:

## No new postulates were needed

Gates and measurement extend to density matrices with no new physical
postulates. Every formula here is an algebraic consequence of the ordinary
state-vector rules, extended to mixtures by linearity. What is new is
scope: the formulas apply to states like $I/2$, or one qubit of an
entangled pair, that no state vector represents.

## Hadamard applied to the maximally mixed state

**Apply the Hadamard gate to $\rho=I/2$, and explain the result.**

$H(I/2)H^\dagger = \tfrac12HH^\dagger = \tfrac12HH^{-1}=\tfrac12I$
(since $H$ is both Hermitian and unitary, $H^\dagger=H^{-1}=H$, so
$HH^\dagger=I$). The maximally mixed state is unchanged by any unitary
gate, not only by $H$. That makes physical sense. $I/2$ represents total
ignorance about which of any orthonormal basis's two states the qubit is
in, and rotating total ignorance leaves total ignorance, re-expressed in a
basis that looks identical to $I/2$ anyway.

## The dagger is what keeps rho Hermitian

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the dagger in ρ'=UρU†.** It's $U\rho U^\dagger$, not
$U\rho U$ or $U^\dagger\rho U$: the dagger on the right is what
guarantees $\rho'$ stays Hermitian. Dropping or misplacing it produces a
matrix that generally isn't even a valid density matrix.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming Tr(ρP) needs ρ to come from a single ψ.** It does not. The
derivation above shows $p=\text{Tr}(\rho P)$ holds for *any* valid density
matrix, mixed or pure, which is what makes the $I/2$ example meaningful.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Apply $X$ to $\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.75&0\\0&0.25\end{pmatrix}$ using
   $\rho'=X\rho X^\dagger$, and interpret the result physically (Hint:
   $X$ swaps $|0\rangle\leftrightarrow|1\rangle$). Then say what happens
   to the state's purity and entropy, and why that had to happen.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $X$ is its own adjoint, so $\rho' = X\rho X$:

   $$
   X\rho X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.75&0\\0&0.25\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0.75\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   `evolveDensityMatrix` returns exactly this. Physically, the ensemble was
   "$|0\rangle$ three quarters of the time, $|1\rangle$ one quarter"; $X$
   turns every $|0\rangle$ into $|1\rangle$ and vice versa, so the new
   ensemble is "$|1\rangle$ three quarters, $|0\rangle$ one quarter". The
   two weights have traded places, which is what a bit flip should do to a
   biased coin. On the Bloch ball the point moves from $(0,0,0.5)$ to
   $(0,0,-0.5)$, a half turn about the $x$-axis.

   Purity and entropy are unchanged: $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=0.5625+0.0625=0.625$
   before and after, and the entropy is $0.8113$ bits both times, which the
   engine confirms. That had to happen because a unitary conjugation
   preserves eigenvalues. $X\rho X^\dagger$ is a similarity transformation,
   so its characteristic polynomial is the same as $\rho$'s, and every
   function of the eigenvalues alone, purity and entropy included, comes
   out identical.

   Read geometrically it is the same statement: a unitary rotates the Bloch
   ball rigidly about its centre, so radii are preserved, and purity and
   entropy both depend only on $|\vec r|$. A gate can point a state
   anywhere on its sphere of fixed radius and cannot move it to a different
   sphere.

   The wrong turn is writing $\rho' = X\rho$ and stopping. That gives
   $\begin{pmatrix}0&0.25\\0.75&0\end{pmatrix}$, which is not Hermitian,
   has trace $0$, and is not a state at all. The sandwich is not decoration:
   $\rho$ carries a ket *and* a bra, so a gate has to act on both sides,
   $U$ on the ket and $U^\dagger$ on the bra.

   </details>

2. Measure $P_1=|1\rangle\langle1|$ on $\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.75&0\\0&0.25\end{pmatrix}$:
   find $p_1$ and the post-measurement state.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The generalized Born rule gives

   $$
   p_1 = \text{Tr}(\rho P_1) = \text{Tr}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0.25\end{pmatrix} = 0.25 ,
   $$

   and the collapse rule gives

   $$
   \rho_{\text{after}} = \frac{P_1\rho P_1}{p_1} = \frac{1}{0.25}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&0.25\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = |1\rangle\langle1| .
   $$

   `densityMatrixMeasurementProbability` returns $0.25$ and
   `densityMatrixCollapse` returns $\mathrm{diag}(0,1)$.

   Two things happened at once and they are worth separating. The
   probability came straight off the diagonal, because $\rho$ is diagonal
   and $P_1$ picks out one entry; $0.25$ is exactly the classical
   probability the ensemble assigned to $|1\rangle$, as it must be for a
   mixture of computational basis states. And the state went from mixed,
   with purity $0.625$ and entropy $0.811$ bits, to pure, with purity $1$
   and entropy $0$.

   That second part is the point worth carrying forward. The last question
   showed that no gate can change a state's entropy. Measurement can, and
   it did: this one destroyed $0.811$ bits of uncertainty. There is no
   contradiction, because measurement is not a unitary. It selects a branch
   and renormalizes, which is exactly the operation a rigid rotation of the
   Bloch ball cannot perform.

   The wrong turn is expecting a mixed state to stay mixed, on the
   reasoning that classical ignorance cannot be removed by a quantum
   measurement. It can, when the measurement asks the question the
   ignorance was about. Ask a different question and it does not: question
   3 measures $I/2$ in the $X$ basis and also lands on a pure state, while
   measuring $\rho=\mathrm{diag}(0.75,0.25)$ in the $X$ basis leaves you
   with $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$, again pure, but having learned nothing
   about the original bias.

   </details>

3. Show that measuring $\rho=I/2$ in the $X$-basis instead of the $Z$-basis
   also gives 50/50 odds, using $P_+=|+\rangle\langle+|$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Use the Born rule with $\rho=\tfrac12 I$ and pull the scalar out:

   $$
   p_+ = \text{Tr}\left(\tfrac12 I \cdot P_+\right) = \tfrac12\text{Tr}(P_+) = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac12
   $$

   using the fact that a projector onto a one-dimensional subspace has
   trace $1$. The engine returns $0.5$. The same two lines give
   $p_- = \tfrac12$, and the two sum to $1$ as they must.

   Notice how little the calculation used. It never mentioned $|+\rangle$,
   only that $P_+$ is a rank-one projector, so the identical argument gives
   $\tfrac12$ for a projector onto *any* single-qubit state whatsoever: the
   $Y$ basis, some axis at $37^\circ$, anything. $I/2$ is featureless in
   every basis at once, which is what "maximally mixed" means and why no
   measurement can reveal which ensemble prepared it.

   The collapse is the surprise. $P_+ (I/2) P_+ / p_+ = |+\rangle\langle+|$,
   a pure state with entropy $0$, and the engine confirms it. So a
   measurement on a completely featureless state still produces a
   completely definite one. Nothing was learned about the past, since both
   outcomes were equally likely whatever the preparation had been, and yet
   the future is now fully determined. Measurement's two roles, extracting
   information and preparing a state, come apart cleanly here: this one
   does the second and none of the first.

   The wrong turn is expecting $I/2$ to give $50/50$ in the $Z$ basis but
   something else in $X$, by analogy with $\rho_{|+\rangle}$, which is
   $50/50$ in $Z$ and certain in $X$. That analogy is exactly backwards.
   The two states share a diagonal and differ in their coherences, and it
   is the coherences that make $|+\rangle$ certain in the $X$ basis. $I/2$
   has none, so it has no basis in which it is certain of anything.

   </details>

4. Prove, in general, that $U(I/2)U^\dagger=I/2$ for *any* unitary $U$
   (not just $H$), using only $UU^\dagger=I$ and linearity of scalar
   multiplication over matrices. Then say what this implies about whether
   any gate can lower a qubit's entropy, and name an operation that can.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Pull the scalar through and use unitarity:

   $$
   U\left(\tfrac12 I\right)U^\dagger = \tfrac12\,U I U^\dagger = \tfrac12\,U U^\dagger = \tfrac12 I
   $$

   Three steps, none of which needs to know anything about $U$ beyond
   $UU^\dagger=I$. The engine's check with $H$ is one instance; the proof
   covers every gate at once, including gates nobody has built.

   $I/2$ is therefore a fixed point of every unitary. Geometrically it is
   the centre of the Bloch ball, and a rotation about the centre leaves the
   centre alone, which is the same fact without the algebra.

   The implication for entropy is a hard no. Unitary conjugation preserves
   eigenvalues, so it preserves every function of them, entropy included:
   $S(U\rho U^\dagger)=S(\rho)$ for every $\rho$ and every $U$. No gate,
   and no circuit of gates however long, can lower a qubit's entropy by
   even a fraction of a bit. A maximally mixed qubit stays maximally mixed
   forever under any closed-system evolution.

   Two operations can lower it, and both step outside "unitary on this
   qubit alone". Measurement does, as question 2 showed, taking $0.811$
   bits to $0$ by selecting a branch. And interaction with a second system
   does: entangle the qubit with a fresh one and then discard the partner,
   and the qubit's entropy changes freely, which is exactly how a
   refrigerated qubit gets reset to $|0\rangle$ in real hardware. The
   entropy has to go somewhere, and in both cases it goes into a system you
   stopped tracking.

   The wrong turn is verifying the claim for $X$, $Y$, $Z$ and $H$ and
   calling it proved. Those are four matrices out of a continuum, and the
   pattern they exhibit is real but their agreement is not an argument. The
   three-line proof above is shorter than checking even one of them by
   direct multiplication.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\rho'=U\rho U^\dagger$ follows directly from $|\psi\rangle\to U|\psi\rangle$,
  extended to mixtures by linearity; trace and Hermiticity are
  automatically preserved.
- $p=\text{Tr}(\rho P)$ and $\rho_{\text{after}}=P\rho P/p$ are the
  density-matrix versions of the Born rule and collapse: the same
  formulas as the state-vector case, re-derived via the trace trick.
- Both formulas apply to any valid density matrix, including genuinely
  mixed states with no state-vector description at all.
- The maximally mixed state $I/2$ is invariant under every unitary gate:
  "total ignorance" cannot be rotated away.
