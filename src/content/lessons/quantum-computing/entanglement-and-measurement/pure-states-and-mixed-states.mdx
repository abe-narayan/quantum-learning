## A coin flip, and a qubit handed over

Lesson 1 built $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ from a single known state
vector.

Now suppose a technician flips a coin and prepares a qubit as $|0\rangle$
on heads and $|1\rangle$ on tails, then hands it to you without saying
which. This is not a superposition. A definite pure state was prepared;
you were not told which one.

There is no ket for this situation. It is not
$\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ for any $\alpha,\beta$, because that
expression *is* a superposition, a specific quantum state, rather than a
description of ignorance about which state you hold. The state-vector
formalism has no way to write it down.

The <Term id="density-matrices">density matrix</Term> does, and the payoff is larger than this toy example
suggests. A few lessons from now, one qubit of an entangled pair will turn
out to need exactly this kind of description, with nobody flipping any
coins.

## Building a mixture, then telling it from a superposition

### Building a mixture

If a system is prepared in state $|\psi_i\rangle$ with probability $p_i$
(the $p_i\geq0$ summing to 1, and this is *classical* ignorance about
preparation, not quantum superposition), its density matrix is the
probability-weighted sum of each possibility's own density matrix:

$$
\rho = \sum_i p_i |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|
$$

This is called a **convex combination** of the pure states'
density matrices (convex: weights nonnegative and summing to 1, the
same structure as an ordinary weighted average). Two structural facts
carry over immediately from the single-state case, by linearity:

$$
\rho^\dagger = \sum_i p_i(|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|)^\dagger = \sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i| = \rho
$$

$$
\text{Tr}(\rho) = \sum_i p_i\,\text{Tr}(|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|) = \sum_i p_i \cdot 1 = \sum_i p_i = 1
$$

Hermitian, trace 1: the same two properties every pure-state $\rho$ has,
now shown to survive mixing (a third property, positivity, also survives
mixing, since a sum of positive-semidefinite matrices is
positive-semidefinite, but proving that in general needs machinery this
course doesn't build; the engine's `validateDensityMatrix` checks it
directly for the 2×2 case this course uses).

### The classical mixture, computed

For the 50/50 mix of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ described above:

$$
\rho_{\text{mix}} = \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|
= \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}
$$

This is $I/2$ itself: the **maximally mixed state**, the density
matrix furthest from describing any single definite pure state. It says:
"measured in the computational basis, 0 and 1 are equally likely" and
nothing more.

### The key comparison: ρ_mix vs. ρ_+

Now compare $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ to $\rho_{|+\rangle}$ from Lesson 1:

$$
\rho_{|+\rangle} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\rho_{\text{mix}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}
$$

`pureStateDensityMatrix` and one through `convexCombination`. The
highlighted diagonals are what a computational-basis measurement can see:

**Identical diagonal entries.** Both predict $P(0)=P(1)=0.5$ in the
computational basis. No number of computational-basis measurements tells
them apart, because the statistics they generate are identical, not
merely similar. But the **off-diagonal entries differ**: $0.5$
for $|+\rangle$, $0$ for the mixture. Those off-diagonal terms
are called **coherences**, and they carry real physical content: measure
$X$ instead of $Z$. $|+\rangle$ is an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> of $X$ with eigenvalue
$+1$, so it gives $P(+)=1$ deterministically in the $X$ basis. The
maximally mixed state gives $P(+)=P(-)=0.5$ in *every* basis, $X$
included: no measurement basis distinguishes its two "halves." Switching
to the $X$ basis and collecting a modest number of shots separates the
two states, which the computational basis cannot do with any number of
shots at all.

### Purity foreshadowed

One number captures this difference directly: $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$.

$$
\text{Tr}(\rho_{|+\rangle}^2) = 1 \qquad \text{Tr}(\rho_{\text{mix}}^2) = 0.5
$$

$\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=1$ for every pure state and nothing else. Anything less
signals a mixture, with $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=1/d$ the
minimum, reached only by the maximally mixed state. Both numbers above come
from the engine's `purity` function, which returns {purityPlus.toFixed(2)}
and {purityMix.toFixed(2)} for these two matrices, and they are checked as
regression tests. The next section proves the pure-state claim for a single
qubit; Lesson 6, Purity, Entropy and Information, handles the general
$d$-dimensional case and the entropy that goes with it.

### The Bloch ball, derived

The explorer below, and several interactives later in this course,
describe a mixed state as a point that pulls inward off the Bloch
sphere's surface. That picture deserves a derivation, and a few lines of
Pauli algebra deliver it. Write $\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z$ for the
gates this curriculum has been calling $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ (the $\sigma$
notation is standard once they are being treated as observables rather
than as gates). Together with the identity they span the 2×2 Hermitian
matrices with real coefficients, so every qubit density matrix can be
written in that basis:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Any 2×2 Hermitian matrix is a real combination of I and the three Pauli matrices. Fixing the I coefficient at 1/2 (forced by the trace, next step) leaves three real numbers: the Bloch vector r = (r_x, r_y, r_z).">
$$
\rho = \tfrac12\left(I + r_x\sigma_x + r_y\sigma_y + r_z\sigma_z\right) = \tfrac12\left(I + \vec r\cdot\vec\sigma\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Each Pauli matrix is traceless, so the trace condition costs nothing: it holds automatically for every r.">
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho) = \tfrac12\left(\operatorname{Tr} I + \textstyle\sum_i r_i\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_i\right) = \tfrac12(2+0) = 1
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Use Tr(sigma_i sigma_j) = 2 delta_ij: distinct Paulis multiply to a traceless matrix, and each Pauli squares to I. So r is not an abstract label; each component is a measurable expectation value.">
$$
\langle\sigma_i\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\,\sigma_i) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_i + \tfrac12\textstyle\sum_j r_j\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_i) = \tfrac12(2r_i) = r_i
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Square the expansion. The anticommutation relation sigma_i sigma_j + sigma_j sigma_i = 2 delta_ij I kills every cross term, leaving (r·sigma)² = |r|² I.">
$$
\rho^2 = \tfrac14\left(I + 2\,\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma + |\vec r|^2 I\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Take the trace. The Pauli term drops out (traceless again), leaving purity as a function of the Bloch vector's length alone.">
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2) = \tfrac14\left(2 + 0 + 2|\vec r|^2\right) = \frac{1+|\vec r|^2}{2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Read the last line in both directions. $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)=1$
forces $|\vec r|=1$: pure states sit on the sphere's surface.
$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)<1$ forces $|\vec r|<1$: every mixed state
lives strictly inside the ball. Both examples above check out.
$\rho_{|+\rangle}$ has $\vec r=(1,0,0)$, on the surface, while
$\rho_{\text{mix}}=I/2$ has $\vec r=0$, dead center, giving
$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)=(1+0)/2=0.5$ as computed. Positivity is what
closes the ball. Since $(\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma)^2=|\vec r|^2I$, the
operator $\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma$ has eigenvalues $\pm|\vec r|$, so
$\rho=\tfrac12(I+\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma)$ has eigenvalues
$(1\pm|\vec r|)/2$. Those are nonnegative only when $|\vec r|\leq1$, so a
point outside the unit ball is not a state at all.

## Two words that do not mean the same thing

A quantum superposition and a classical mixture are not two words for the
same idea. $|+\rangle$ is one definite quantum state, in which "0 or 1"
has no fixed answer until measured, and <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> (the X
measurement above) is fully available. $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ describes a
qubit that *is* $|0\rangle$ or *is* $|1\rangle$ the whole time, with you
not knowing which, and no measurement in any basis shows interference,
because there is none to show. The density matrix is the first tool this
course has built that can tell these two situations apart.

## Mixing plus and minus, and what comes out

**A qubit is prepared as $|+\rangle$ with probability 0.5 and $|-\rangle$
with probability 0.5. Find $\rho$, and identify what familiar state it
equals.**

$|+\rangle\langle+|=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$,
$|-\rangle\langle-|=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$.
Averaging: $\rho=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}+\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}=I/2$.
A 50/50 mixture of $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$ is *also* the maximally mixed
state: the same $\rho$ as a 50/50 mixture of $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. This
is a physical fact, not a coincidence of notation. $I/2$ has no memory of
*which* basis it was "mixed in," because it is featureless in every basis
at once. There is no experiment
that can tell a $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$-prepared maximally mixed qubit
from a $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$-prepared one: they are the same
physical state.

## A mixture is not a weighted sum of kets

<Callout type="warning">
**Writing a mixture as a weighted sum of kets, such as
$\tfrac12|0\rangle+\tfrac12|1\rangle$ for the 50/50 mix.** That
expression is not even a normalized state, and if you fix the
normalization you get $|+\rangle$, which is the *other* state in this
lesson's comparison. A superposition $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$
is one specific pure ket. It has its own density matrix
$|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, generally with nonzero coherence terms. A
mixture is a probability-weighted sum of *entire density matrices*, not
a sum of kets: there is no ket for $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming equal diagonal entries means equal states.** As shown above,
$\rho_{|+\rangle}$ and $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ share every diagonal entry but
are entirely different physical states, distinguishable by measuring in
a different basis (or, equivalently, by their different <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term>).
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A qubit is $|0\rangle$ with probability 0.8 and $|1\rangle$ with
   probability 0.2. Write down $\rho$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Weight each possibility's own density matrix by its probability and add:

   $$
   \rho = 0.8\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + 0.2\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.8&0\\0&0.2\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   The engine's `convexCombination` returns exactly this. Trace is
   $0.8+0.2=1$, as the general proof in the lesson requires, and the matrix
   is diagonal, so trivially Hermitian.

   The zeros off the diagonal are the content here. They say this qubit has
   no coherence between $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$: the technician's coin
   landed one way or the other, and there is no phase relationship between
   two branches because there are not two branches, only two possibilities
   one of which is the case. Compare the superposition
   $\sqrt{0.8}|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.2}|1\rangle$, which has the same diagonal
   and off-diagonal entries $\sqrt{0.16}=0.4$. Identical
   computational-basis statistics, different states, and the difference
   shows up the moment you measure in the $X$ basis.

   The wrong turn is writing $0.8|0\rangle+0.2|1\rangle$ and calling it the
   state. That vector is not normalized, and normalizing it gives
   $\sqrt{0.8}|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.2}|1\rangle$, which is the superposition,
   the other object in this comparison. Probabilities weight density
   matrices; amplitudes weight kets, and the two weightings are not the same
   operation.

   </details>

2. Compute $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$ for the state in question 1, and confirm
   it's strictly between 0.5 and 1. Then locate its Bloch vector, and check
   both numbers against the identity $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=(1+|\vec r|^2)/2$
   derived above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Squaring a diagonal matrix squares its entries, so
   $\rho^2 = \mathrm{diag}(0.64, 0.04)$ and

   $$
   \text{Tr}(\rho^2) = 0.64 + 0.04 = 0.68 .
   $$

   Strictly between $0.5$ and $1$, so the state is mixed but not maximally
   mixed: partial ignorance, not total. The engine's `purity` returns
   $0.68$.

   The Bloch vector comes from the three Pauli expectation values.
   $\langle X\rangle = \text{Tr}(\rho X) = 0$ and $\langle Y\rangle = 0$,
   because both Paulis are purely off-diagonal and $\rho$ has nothing
   there, while $\langle Z\rangle = 0.8 - 0.2 = 0.6$. So
   $\vec r = (0,0,0.6)$, pointing along $+z$ toward $|0\rangle$ but stopping
   well short of the surface.

   The identity checks out on the nose: $(1+0.6^2)/2 = (1+0.36)/2 = 0.68$.
   Read it the other way and it says the purity alone fixes $|\vec r|$,
   here $|\vec r| = \sqrt{2(0.68)-1} = 0.6$, which happens to be exactly the
   probability gap $0.8-0.2$. That is not a coincidence for a
   $Z$-basis mixture: $|\vec r|$ is the imbalance between the two
   probabilities, and it goes to $0$ precisely when they are equal.

   The wrong turn is computing $\text{Tr}(\rho)^2 = 1$ instead of
   $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$. Squaring the trace gives $1$ for every density
   matrix, pure or mixed, so it can never distinguish anything. The
   ordering matters: square the matrix first, then trace.

   </details>

3. A qubit is $|+\rangle$ with probability $p$ and $|-\rangle$ with
   probability $1-p$. Predict, then verify: for which values of $p$ does
   $\rho$ equal $I/2$? (Compute $\rho$ for general $p$ and find its
   off-diagonal entry; the worked example above is the special case where
   that entry vanishes. Locate the state's Bloch vector for general $p$
   too.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Only $p = \tfrac12$.

   Combine the two matrices with weights $p$ and $1-p$:

   $$
   \rho(p) = p\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix} + (1-p)\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5 & p-0.5\\ p-0.5 & 0.5\end{pmatrix}
   $$

   The diagonal is $0.5$ for every $p$, which is worth pausing on: no
   mixture of $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ has any $Z$-basis preference at
   all, since neither ingredient does. All the $p$-dependence sits in the
   coherence $p-\tfrac12$, which vanishes exactly at $p=\tfrac12$ and
   nowhere else. The engine confirms the whole family:
   $p = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1$ give off-diagonals
   $-0.5, -0.25, 0, 0.25, 0.5$ and purities $1, 0.625, 0.5, 0.625, 1$.

   The Bloch vector is $\vec r = (2p-1, 0, 0)$: the expectation
   $\langle X\rangle = 2(p-\tfrac12) = 2p-1$, with $\langle Y\rangle$ and
   $\langle Z\rangle$ both zero. So the state slides along the $x$-axis
   from $|-\rangle$ at $(-1,0,0)$ through the center at $p=\tfrac12$ to
   $|+\rangle$ at $(1,0,0)$, and the purity identity gives
   $\text{Tr}(\rho^2) = (1+(2p-1)^2)/2$, matching all five engine values.

   The trap is answering "every $p$", reasoning that any mixture of two
   orthogonal states has lost the phase information and must be maximally
   mixed. Mixing does not destroy coherence; it averages it. At $p=0.75$
   the mixture is still $75\%$ $|+\rangle$, and a measurement in the $X$
   basis returns $+$ three times in four, which $I/2$ would never do. Only
   the perfectly balanced average cancels the two contributions exactly.

   </details>

4. Explain why no single measurement, in any one fixed basis, can ever
   distinguish $\rho_{\text{mix}}=I/2$ from any other $I/2$-producing
   mixture.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the object a measurement's statistics depend on is $\rho$, and
   the two preparations produce the same $\rho$. Every prediction the
   theory makes runs through $\langle A\rangle = \text{Tr}(\rho A)$, or the
   outcome probability $\text{Tr}(\rho P)$ for a projector $P$, and both
   formulas take $\rho$ as their only input. Feed the same matrix in and
   the same numbers come out, for every observable and every basis, with no
   exception to look for.

   The question's phrasing is worth sharpening, because the truth is
   stronger than "no single measurement in one fixed basis". No sequence of
   measurements in any set of bases, on any number of identically prepared
   copies, distinguishes them either. Restricting to one basis would leave
   the door open, since $\rho_{|+\rangle}$ and $I/2$ agree in the $Z$ basis
   and are told apart by the $X$ basis, so a one-basis limitation would be
   a weak claim. The claim here is that two preparations giving the same
   $\rho$ agree in *all* bases at once, and no amount of statistics on
   either side helps.

   Concretely, $I/2$ arises from mixing $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ equally, from
   mixing $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$ equally, from mixing $|{+i}\rangle,|{-i}\rangle$
   equally, and from every other orthonormal pair mixed equally. All of
   these are the same physical state. Its Bloch vector is the origin, so it
   has no preferred direction for a measurement to detect, which is the
   geometric version of the same statement.

   The reasoning to avoid is "you cannot tell because you would need to
   know which state each individual qubit is really in, and measurement
   disturbs it". That treats the answer as an epistemic limitation and
   predicts, wrongly, that a gentler probe might succeed. The lesson's
   result is not about the limits of measurement. It is that the question
   has no answer to find: the ensemble story is not part of the physics,
   and $\rho$ is the whole of the physical description.

   </details>

## Summary

- A classical mixture's density matrix is $\rho=\sum_ip_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$:
  a probability-weighted sum of pure-state density matrices, not a
  weighted sum of kets.
- Hermiticity and trace 1 survive mixing by linearity; positivity does
  too (verified directly by the engine for the 2×2 case).
- $|+\rangle$ and a 50/50 mixture of $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ share every
  computational-basis prediction but differ in their off-diagonal
  coherence terms and in $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$: different
  physical states, distinguishable by measuring in a different basis.
- The maximally mixed state $I/2$ results from mixing *any* orthogonal
  pair with equal weight, in any basis: it has no memory of which basis
  produced it.
