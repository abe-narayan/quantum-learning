## Motivation

Lesson 1 built $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ from a single known state
vector.

Now suppose a technician flips a coin and prepares a qubit as $|0\rangle$
on heads and $|1\rangle$ on tails, then hands it to you without saying
which. This is not a superposition. A definite pure state was prepared;
you were not told which one.

There is no ket for this situation. It is not
$\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ for any $\alpha,\beta$, because that
expression *is* a superposition, a specific quantum state, rather than a
description of ignorance about which state you hold. The state-vector
formalism has no way to write it down.

The <Term id="density-matrices">density matrix</Term> does, and the payoff is larger than this toy example
suggests. A few lessons from now, one qubit of an entangled pair will turn
out to need exactly this kind of description, with nobody flipping any
coins.

## Mathematical Development

### Building a mixture

If a system is prepared in state $|\psi_i\rangle$ with probability $p_i$
(the $p_i\geq0$ summing to 1, and this is *classical* ignorance about
preparation, not quantum superposition), its density matrix is the
probability-weighted sum of each possibility's own density matrix:

$$
\rho = \sum_i p_i |\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|
$$

This is called a **convex combination** of the pure states'
density matrices (convex: weights nonnegative and summing to 1, the
same structure as an ordinary weighted average). Two structural facts
carry over immediately from the single-state case, by linearity:

$$
\rho^\dagger = \sum_i p_i(|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|)^\dagger = \sum_i p_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i| = \rho
$$

$$
\text{Tr}(\rho) = \sum_i p_i\,\text{Tr}(|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|) = \sum_i p_i \cdot 1 = \sum_i p_i = 1
$$

Hermitian, trace 1: the same two properties every pure-state $\rho$ has,
now shown to survive mixing (a third property, positivity, also survives
mixing, since a sum of positive-semidefinite matrices is
positive-semidefinite, but proving that in general needs machinery this
course doesn't build; the engine's `validateDensityMatrix` checks it
directly for the 2×2 case this course uses).

### The classical mixture, computed

For the 50/50 mix of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ described above:

$$
\rho_{\text{mix}} = \tfrac12|0\rangle\langle0| + \tfrac12|1\rangle\langle1|
= \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}
= \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}
$$

This is $I/2$ itself: the **maximally mixed state**, the density
matrix furthest from describing any single definite pure state. It says:
"measured in the computational basis, 0 and 1 are equally likely" and
nothing more.

### The key comparison: ρ_mix vs. ρ_+

Now compare $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ to $\rho_{|+\rangle}$ from Lesson 1:

$$
\rho_{|+\rangle} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\rho_{\text{mix}} = \begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}
$$

**Identical diagonal entries.** Both predict $P(0)=P(1)=0.5$ in the
computational basis, so a single computational-basis measurement can
never tell them apart. But the **off-diagonal entries differ**: $0.5$
for $|+\rangle$, $0$ for the mixture. Those off-diagonal terms
are called **coherences**, and they carry real physical content: measure
$X$ instead of $Z$. $|+\rangle$ is an <Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> of $X$ with eigenvalue
$+1$, so it gives $P(+)=1$ deterministically in the $X$ basis. The
maximally mixed state gives $P(+)=P(-)=0.5$ in *every* basis, $X$
included: no measurement basis distinguishes its two "halves." A single
$X$-basis measurement, repeated enough times to build statistics,
cleanly separates the two states, something the computational basis
alone could never do.

### Purity foreshadowed

One number captures this difference directly: $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$.

$$
\text{Tr}(\rho_{|+\rangle}^2) = 1 \qquad \text{Tr}(\rho_{\text{mix}}^2) = 0.5
$$

$\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=1$ for every pure state and nothing else (proven next
lesson). Anything less signals a mixture, with $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)=1/d$ the
minimum, reached only by the maximally mixed state. Both numbers above come
from the engine's `purity` function and are checked as regression tests.
Lesson 6 derives *why* $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$ behaves this way in general,
rather than for this one pair of examples.

### The Bloch ball, derived

The explorer below, and several interactives later in this course,
describe a mixed state as a point that pulls inward off the Bloch
sphere's surface. That picture deserves a derivation, and a few lines of
Pauli algebra deliver it. Write a general qubit density matrix in the
basis $\{I,\sigma_x,\sigma_y,\sigma_z\}$, which spans the 2×2 Hermitian
matrices with real coefficients:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Any 2×2 Hermitian matrix is a real combination of I and the three Pauli matrices. Fixing the I coefficient at 1/2 (forced by the trace, next step) leaves three real numbers: the Bloch vector r = (r_x, r_y, r_z).">
$$
\rho = \tfrac12\left(I + r_x\sigma_x + r_y\sigma_y + r_z\sigma_z\right) = \tfrac12\left(I + \vec r\cdot\vec\sigma\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Each Pauli matrix is traceless, so the trace condition costs nothing: it holds automatically for every r.">
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho) = \tfrac12\left(\operatorname{Tr} I + \textstyle\sum_i r_i\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_i\right) = \tfrac12(2+0) = 1
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Use Tr(sigma_i sigma_j) = 2 delta_ij: distinct Paulis multiply to a traceless matrix, and each Pauli squares to I. So r is not an abstract label; each component is a measurable expectation value.">
$$
\langle\sigma_i\rangle = \operatorname{Tr}(\rho\,\sigma_i) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Tr}\sigma_i + \tfrac12\textstyle\sum_j r_j\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_i) = \tfrac12(2r_i) = r_i
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Square the expansion. The anticommutation relation sigma_i sigma_j + sigma_j sigma_i = 2 delta_ij I kills every cross term, leaving (r·sigma)^2 = |r|^2 I.">
$$
\rho^2 = \tfrac14\left(I + 2\,\vec r\cdot\vec\sigma + |\vec r|^2 I\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Take the trace. The Pauli term drops out (traceless again), leaving purity as a function of the Bloch vector's length alone.">
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2) = \tfrac14\left(2 + 0 + 2|\vec r|^2\right) = \frac{1+|\vec r|^2}{2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Read the last line in both directions. $\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)=1$
forces $|\vec r|=1$: pure states sit on the sphere's surface.
$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)<1$ forces $|\vec r|<1$: every mixed state
lives strictly inside the ball. Both examples above check out.
$\rho_{|+\rangle}$ has $\vec r=(1,0,0)$, on the surface, while
$\rho_{\text{mix}}=I/2$ has $\vec r=0$, dead center, giving
$\operatorname{Tr}(\rho^2)=(1+0)/2=0.5$ as computed. Positivity is what
closes the ball: the eigenvalues of $\rho$ work out to
$(1\pm|\vec r|)/2$, nonnegative only when $|\vec r|\leq1$, so a point
outside the unit ball is not a state at all.

## Physical Interpretation

A quantum superposition and a classical mixture are not two words for the
same idea. $|+\rangle$ is one definite quantum state, in which "0 or 1"
has no fixed answer until measured, and <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> (the X
measurement above) is fully available. $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ describes a
qubit that *is* $|0\rangle$ or *is* $|1\rangle$ the whole time, with you
not knowing which, and no measurement in any basis shows interference,
because there is none to show. The density matrix is the first tool this
course has built that can tell these two situations apart.

## Worked Example

**A qubit is prepared as $|+\rangle$ with probability 0.5 and $|-\rangle$
with probability 0.5. Find $\rho$, and identify what familiar state it
equals.**

$|+\rangle\langle+|=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$,
$|-\rangle\langle-|=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}$.
Averaging: $\rho=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}+\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0.5&-0.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0\\0&0.5\end{pmatrix}=I/2$.
A 50/50 mixture of $|+\rangle,|-\rangle$ is *also* the maximally mixed
state: the same $\rho$ as a 50/50 mixture of $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$. This
is a physical fact, not a coincidence of notation. $I/2$ has no memory of
*which* basis it was "mixed in," because it is featureless in every basis
at once. There is no experiment
that can tell a $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$-prepared maximally mixed qubit
from a $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$-prepared one: they are the same
physical state.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\tfrac12|0\rangle+\tfrac1{\sqrt2}|1\rangle$-style thinking as
the same as a mixture.** A superposition $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$
is one specific pure ket. It has its own density matrix
$|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, generally with nonzero coherence terms. A
mixture is a probability-weighted sum of *entire density matrices*, not
a sum of kets: there is no ket for $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming equal diagonal entries means equal states.** As shown above,
$\rho_{|+\rangle}$ and $\rho_{\text{mix}}$ share every diagonal entry but
are entirely different physical states, distinguishable by measuring in
a different basis (or, equivalently, by their different <Term id="von-neumann-entropy-purity">purity</Term>).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A classical mixture's density matrix is $\rho=\sum_ip_i|\psi_i\rangle\langle\psi_i|$:
  a probability-weighted sum of pure-state density matrices, not a
  weighted sum of kets.
- Hermiticity and trace 1 survive mixing by linearity; positivity does
  too (verified directly by the engine for the 2×2 case).
- $|+\rangle$ and a 50/50 mixture of $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ share every
  computational-basis prediction but differ in their off-diagonal
  coherence terms and in $\text{Tr}(\rho^2)$: different
  physical states, distinguishable by measuring in a different basis.
- The maximally mixed state $I/2$ results from mixing *any* orthogonal
  pair with equal weight, in any basis: it has no memory of which basis
  produced it.
