## Motivation

The last lesson showed both Grover building blocks are reflections, and
composing two reflections is a rotation. This lesson makes that rotation
exact, turning "search gets faster" into a specific formula for the
success probability after exactly $k$ iterations, and from that, the exact
optimal number of iterations to run.

## Mathematical Development

### The 2D subspace

For a single marked item among $N=2^n$, split the uniform superposition
$|s\rangle$ into two orthogonal pieces: $|\beta\rangle$, the marked item
itself, and $|\alpha\rangle$, the (renormalized) uniform superposition
over the remaining $N-1$ unmarked items. Since
$|s\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_x|x\rangle$ has amplitude $\frac1{\sqrt
N}$ on every state including the marked one:

$$
|s\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N}}|\alpha\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}|\beta\rangle = \cos\theta\,|\alpha\rangle+\sin\theta\,|\beta\rangle, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

Both the oracle and diffusion operator act *entirely within* this 2D span
of $\{|\alpha\rangle,|\beta\rangle\}$: the oracle reflects about
$|\alpha\rangle$ (flipping only $|\beta\rangle$'s sign), diffusion reflects
about $|s\rangle$. Composing two reflections in a plane, with an angle
$\theta$ between the reflection axes, is a rotation by $2\theta$, a
standard fact of plane geometry, applying here because both operators are
literally reflections within this specific 2D subspace.

### The closed form

Starting at $|s\rangle=\cos\theta|\alpha\rangle+\sin\theta|\beta\rangle$
and rotating by $2\theta$ per iteration, after $k$ iterations the state is
at angle $(2k+1)\theta$ from $|\alpha\rangle$:

$$
\boxed{\text{state after }k\text{ iterations} = \cos\big((2k+1)\theta\big)|\alpha\rangle + \sin\big((2k+1)\theta\big)|\beta\rangle}
$$

so the probability of measuring the marked item is exactly

$$
P(\text{marked}) = \sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big), \qquad \theta=\arcsin\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

Checked directly against the engine across $k=0,1,2,5,8$ for $N=16$: the
formula and `runGrover`'s actual output agree to 6 decimal places at every
one of those iteration counts, not just near the optimum.

### The optimal iteration count

$P(\text{marked})=1$ exactly when $(2k+1)\theta=\pi/2$, i.e.
$k=\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$. Since $\theta=\arcsin(1/\sqrt N)\approx
1/\sqrt N$ for large $N$, this gives
$k_{\text{opt}}\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N-\frac12$, the source of
Grover's famous $O(\sqrt N)$ query count. Because $k$ must be an integer,
`optimalGroverIterations` rounds this to the nearest whole number, and the
resulting success probability is generally close to but not always exactly
$1$ (exactly $1$ only when $\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$ happens to already
be an integer, e.g. $N=4,M=1$: $\theta=\pi/6$, giving
$\frac{\pi}{4(\pi/6)}-\frac12=\frac32-\frac12=1$ exactly, matching the
engine's $P=1.0$ at $k=1$ noted below. Most $N$ aren't this lucky: rounding
to the nearest integer iteration count generally leaves a small gap below
certainty, confirmed directly by the engine's $94$–$99\%$ success rates at
$N=8$ through $64$, not the misleading "exactly 100%" a purely asymptotic
formula might suggest).

### More iterations isn't always better

Because $P(\text{marked})=\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$ is periodic in $k$,
running *past* the optimal count doesn't keep improving the odds: it
oscillates back down and later back up, exactly like an ordinary rotation
overshooting a target angle and coming back around. Grover's algorithm
needs the *right* number of iterations, not simply "more."

## Physical Interpretation

Grover's speedup comes entirely from geometry: a fixed rotation angle
$2\theta\approx2/\sqrt N$ per iteration means reaching the marked state's
neighborhood (angle $\approx\pi/2$) takes $O(\sqrt N)$ steps, each costing
one oracle query, quadratically fewer than the $O(N)$ a classical search
needs, with no shortcut through the oracle's black-box structure required
or exploited.

## Worked Example

**For $N=16$ ($n=4$), one marked item, compute $\theta$ and the exact
success probability at $k=3$ iterations.**

$\theta=\arcsin(1/4)\approx0.2527$ rad. At $k=3$:
$(2(3)+1)\theta=7\theta\approx1.769$ rad, so
$P=\sin^2(1.769)\approx\sin^2(101.4°)\approx0.961$. This matches
`runGrover(4, [marked], 3)`'s actual output exactly (to floating-point
precision): $96.1\%$, not $100\%$, since $k=3$ is close to but not
exactly the continuous optimum for $N=16$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming Grover's algorithm always finds the marked item with
certainty.** Only when the continuous optimum happens to land on an
integer iteration count. In general there's a rounding gap, typically
leaving success probability in the mid-90s to high-90s percent rather than
exactly 100%, still overwhelmingly likely, not guaranteed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Running extra iterations "to be safe."** Past the optimum, success
probability *decreases* (the rotation overshoots), not saturates: running
significantly more iterations than optimal can make the algorithm
substantially worse, not better.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Both Grover operators act within the 2D span of the marked/unmarked
  superpositions, composing into a rotation by $2\theta$,
  $\theta=\arcsin(1/\sqrt N)$.
- Success probability after $k$ iterations is exactly
  $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, a closed form checked directly against the
  engine, not just derived.
- The optimal iteration count $\approx\frac\pi4\sqrt N$ is where this
  probability peaks, the source of Grover's $O(\sqrt N)$ query complexity.
- Because $k$ must be an integer and the probability is periodic, success
  is generally high but not exactly certain, and more iterations isn't
  always better.
