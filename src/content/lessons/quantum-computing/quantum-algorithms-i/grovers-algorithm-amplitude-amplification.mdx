Two reflections compose into a rotation by a fixed angle. Pin that angle down and Grover's whole behaviour becomes trigonometry: how many queries it needs, how confident you can be when you stop, and what running longer does to your odds.

## Making the rotation exact

The last lesson showed that both Grover building blocks are reflections,
and that composing two reflections is a rotation. This lesson makes the
rotation exact.

That is worth more than a tidier description. Once you know the rotation
angle, the success probability after $k$ iterations is a closed-form
trigonometric expression, and every practical question about the algorithm
becomes arithmetic: how many iterations to run, how confident you can be
when you stop, and what happens if you run too many. The last of those has
an answer most people guess wrong.

<ResearchConnection title="A Fast Quantum Mechanical Algorithm for Database Search" source="L. K. Grover, STOC '96 (1996), arXiv:quant-ph/9605043" url="https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9605043">
The original paper. A quadratic speedup over classical unstructured search, from a fixed rotation angle applied once per query.
</ResearchConnection>

## A 2D subspace, a closed form, and an optimum

### The 2D subspace

For a single marked item among $N=2^n$, split the uniform superposition
$|s\rangle$ into two orthogonal pieces: $|\beta\rangle$, the marked item
itself, and $|\alpha\rangle$, the (renormalized) uniform superposition
over the remaining $N-1$ unmarked items. Since
$|s\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_x|x\rangle$ has <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> $\frac1{\sqrt
N}$ on every state including the marked one:

$$
|s\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{N-1}{N}}|\alpha\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt N}|\beta\rangle = \cos\theta\,|\alpha\rangle+\sin\theta\,|\beta\rangle, \qquad \sin\theta=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

Both the oracle and diffusion operator act *entirely within* this 2D <Term id="span">span</Term>
of $\{|\alpha\rangle,|\beta\rangle\}$: the oracle reflects about
$|\alpha\rangle$ (flipping only $|\beta\rangle$'s sign), diffusion reflects
about $|s\rangle$. Composing two reflections in a plane, with an angle
$\theta$ between the reflection axes, is a rotation by $2\theta$: reflecting
a vector at angle $\psi$ about a line at angle $\phi$ sends
$\psi\mapsto2\phi-\psi$, so reflecting first about $|\alpha\rangle$ (angle
$0$: $\psi\mapsto-\psi$) and then about $|s\rangle$ (angle $\theta$:
$-\psi\mapsto2\theta-(-\psi)=\psi+2\theta$) advances every angle by exactly
$2\theta$, a standard fact of plane geometry, applying here because both
operators are literally reflections within this specific 2D subspace.

### The closed form

Starting at $|s\rangle=\cos\theta|\alpha\rangle+\sin\theta|\beta\rangle$
and rotating by $2\theta$ per iteration, after $k$ iterations the state is
at angle $(2k+1)\theta$ from $|\alpha\rangle$:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "k", symbol: "k", gloss: "The number of Grover iterations run so far." },
    { id: "theta", symbol: "θ", gloss: "arcsin(1/√N). Each iteration rotates the state by 2θ." },
    { id: "alpha", symbol: "|α⟩", gloss: "The renormalized uniform superposition over all unmarked items." },
    { id: "beta", symbol: "|β⟩", gloss: "The marked item being searched for." },
  ]}
>
$$
\boxed{\text{state after }k\text{ iterations} = \cos\big((2k+1)\theta\big)|\alpha\rangle + \sin\big((2k+1)\theta\big)|\beta\rangle}
$$
</EquationReveal>

so the probability of measuring the marked item is exactly

$$
P(\text{marked}) = \sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big), \qquad \theta=\arcsin\frac{1}{\sqrt N}
$$

Checked directly against the engine across $k=0,1,2,5,8$ for $N=16$: the
formula and `runGrover`'s actual output agree to 6 decimal places at every
one of those iteration counts, and at every count away from the optimum too.

### The optimal iteration count

$P(\text{marked})=1$ exactly when $(2k+1)\theta=\pi/2$, i.e.
$k=\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$. Since $\theta=\arcsin(1/\sqrt N)\approx
1/\sqrt N$ for large $N$, this gives
$k_{\text{opt}}\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N-\frac12$, the source of
Grover's famous $O(\sqrt N)$ query count. Because $k$ must be an integer,
`optimalGroverIterations` rounds this to the nearest whole number, and the
resulting success probability is generally close to but not always exactly
$1$ (exactly $1$ only when $\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$ happens to already
be an integer, e.g. $N=4,M=1$: $\theta=\pi/6$, giving
$\frac{\pi}{4(\pi/6)}-\frac12=\frac32-\frac12=1$ exactly, matching the
engine's $P=1.0$ at $k=1$ noted below. Most $N$ aren't this lucky: rounding
to the nearest integer iteration count generally leaves a small gap below
certainty, confirmed directly by the engine's success rates from
$94.5\%$ at $N=8$ to $99.9\%$ at $N=32$, none of them the "exactly 100%"
a purely asymptotic formula might suggest).

### What the closed form says about running longer

The closed form settles what happens past the optimum without any further
argument, so read $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$ as a function of $k$ and commit
to an answer before the paragraph after this one gives it.

running *past* the optimal count doesn't keep improving the odds: it
oscillates back down and later back up, like an ordinary rotation
overshooting a target angle and coming back around. Grover's algorithm
needs the *right* number of iterations, not "more."

## Geometry, and nothing else

Grover's speedup comes from geometry and nothing else. Each iteration
rotates by a fixed $2\theta\approx2/\sqrt N$, so reaching the marked
state's neighbourhood (angle $\approx\pi/2$) takes $O(\sqrt N)$ steps at
one oracle query apiece: quadratically fewer than the $O(N)$ a classical
search needs.

Note what the algorithm never does. It never looks inside the oracle, and
it never exploits structure in the search space, because there is none to
exploit. The speedup is what remains available when a problem offers no
handholds at all, which is also why it is only quadratic.

## Success probability at three iterations

**For $N=16$ ($n=4$), one marked item, compute $\theta$ and the exact
success probability at $k=3$ iterations.**

$\theta=\arcsin(1/4)\approx0.2527$ rad. At $k=3$:
$(2(3)+1)\theta=7\theta\approx1.769$ rad, so
$P=\sin^2(1.769)\approx\sin^2(101.3°)\approx0.961$. This matches
`runGrover(4, [marked], 3)`'s actual output exactly (to floating-point
precision): $96.1\%$, not $100\%$, since $k=3$ is close to but not
exactly the continuous optimum for $N=16$.

## When the optimum lands between two integers

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming Grover's algorithm always finds the marked item with
certainty.** Only when the continuous optimum happens to land on an
integer iteration count. In general there's a rounding gap, typically
leaving success probability in the mid-90s to high-90s percent rather than
exactly 100%, still overwhelmingly likely, not guaranteed.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Running extra iterations "to be safe."** Past the optimum, success
probability *decreases* (the rotation overshoots), not saturates: running
significantly more iterations than optimal can make the algorithm
substantially worse, not better.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $\theta=\arcsin(1/\sqrt N)$ for $N=64$, and find the optimal
   (real-valued) iteration count $\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $1/\sqrt{64} = 1/8 = 0.125$, so
   $\theta = \arcsin(0.125) = 0.1253278$ radians, about $7.18°$. Then

   $$
   \frac{\pi}{4\theta} - \frac12 = \frac{0.7853982}{0.1253278} - 0.5 = 6.26675 - 0.5 = 5.76675 .
   $$

   Iterations have to be whole numbers, so `optimalGroverIterations(6)`
   rounds to $6$, and the closed form gives
   $P = \sin^2(13\theta) = 0.996586$ there. The engine's `runGrover` agrees
   to eight decimals, and confirms $0.963515$ at $k=5$: rounding down would
   also work but is a little worse.

   Two checks on the number. The asymptotic shorthand
   $k_{\text{opt}}\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N - \frac12$ gives
   $\frac{\pi}{4}(8) - 0.5 = 5.7832$, within $0.02$ of the exact value,
   because $\arcsin u \approx u$ is already good at $u=0.125$. And
   $\sqrt{64}=8$ against the classical $64$ is the quadratic speedup in
   miniature: about six queries against an average of thirty-two.

   The reason $\theta$ is defined by $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt N$ rather than
   $1/N$ is worth keeping straight. $|s\rangle$ has *amplitude*
   $1/\sqrt N$ on the marked item, and $\theta$ is the angle whose sine is
   that amplitude, so the initial success probability is
   $\sin^2\theta = 1/N$. Using $1/N$ for the sine gives an angle roughly
   $\sqrt N$ times too small and an iteration count of order $N$, which
   silently reproduces the classical cost.

   </details>

2. Using the closed form, compute $P(\text{marked})$ at $k=0$ (zero
   iterations) and confirm it equals $1/N$, the plain guessing
   probability, as it should before any amplification happens.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Put $k=0$ into $P(k)=\sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big)$:

   $$
   P(0) = \sin^2(\theta) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N}
   $$

   by the definition $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt N$. For $N=64$ that is
   $0.015625$, and the engine's `runGrover` with zero iterations returns
   exactly $0.01562500$. For $N=16$ it is $0.0625$.

   This is the sanity check the closed form has to pass, and it is worth
   seeing why it is not trivial. Zero iterations means measuring
   $|s\rangle$ itself, the uniform superposition, which gives every one of
   the $N$ items probability $1/N$: the same odds as picking an item at
   random with no quantum computer at all. A formula that gave anything
   else at $k=0$ would be wrong before it started.

   The other endpoint is worth checking at the same time. At the optimum
   $(2k+1)\theta$ reaches $\pi/2$ and $P\to1$, so the algorithm interpolates
   between "no better than guessing" and "certain", and the whole content
   of the $O(\sqrt N)$ claim is how many iterations that trip takes.

   The wrong turn is reading $P(0)=1/N$ as a defect, or expecting the first
   iteration to be worth $1/\sqrt N$ rather than a modest bump. At $N=64$
   the first iteration takes $P$ from $0.0156$ to $0.1348$, an
   eightfold-ish gain, and the gains keep coming at a fixed angular rate
   rather than a fixed probability rate. Amplitude, not probability, is
   what grows linearly.

   </details>

3. For $N=4$, $M=1$, show the closed form gives *exactly* $P=1$ at
   $k=1$, the one case where Grover's algorithm succeeds with total
   certainty.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $N=4$, $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt4 = 1/2$, so $\theta = \pi/6$ exactly.
   At $k=1$ the angle is $(2\cdot1+1)\theta = 3\times\pi/6 = \pi/2$, and

   $$
   P(1) = \sin^2\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 1
   $$

   exactly, not approximately. The engine's `runGrover(2, [2], 1)` returns
   probability $1.0000000000000004$ on the marked item, which is $1$ to
   floating-point precision.

   What makes this case exact is that $\frac{\pi}{4\theta}-\frac12$ lands
   on a whole number: $\frac{\pi}{4(\pi/6)} - \frac12 = \frac32 - \frac12 = 1$.
   For every other $N$ the optimum falls between integers and the rounded
   iteration count leaves a small residual, which is why $N=16$ tops out at
   $0.9613$ and $N=64$ at $0.9966$. There is nothing special about $N=4$
   physically; it is an arithmetic coincidence that $\arcsin(1/2)$ is a
   rational multiple of $\pi$ with the right denominator.

   The single iteration is also the whole search: two items' worth of
   information from one query, on a set of four. A classical algorithm
   needs up to three queries to be certain, or $2.25$ on average, so even
   this smallest nontrivial case shows a real gap.

   The wrong turn is reading "exactly $1$" as the general promise. Grover's
   algorithm is usually a high-probability algorithm rather than a certain
   one, and the honest statement is that $P$ can be pushed as close to $1$
   as you like by choosing $N$ conveniently or by adding a final partial
   rotation, and is exactly $1$ only when the optimum happens to be an
   integer.

   </details>

4. Explain, using the rotation picture, why running $2k_{\text{opt}}$
   iterations (double the optimum) does not give a "doubly confident"
   result.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because each iteration rotates the state by a fixed angle $2\theta$ in
   the $\{|\alpha\rangle,|\beta\rangle\}$ plane, and the optimum is where
   the state has arrived at $|\beta\rangle$, at angle $\pi/2$. Rotating
   past that point does not push further into $|\beta\rangle$; it carries
   the state back down the other side, and the amplitude on the marked
   item starts shrinking again. At double the optimum the total angle is
   about $\pi$, which is $|\alpha\rangle$ negated, and the marked item is
   back to essentially zero probability.

   The numbers are stark. For $N=64$, $k_{\text{opt}}=6$ gives
   $P=0.9966$. At $k=12$ the closed form gives
   $\sin^2(25\theta) = 0.0000705$, and the engine returns the identical
   value: worse than the $0.0156$ you started with, and about fourteen
   thousand times worse than stopping on time. Doubling the work turned a
   near-certain answer into a near-impossible one.

   The behaviour is periodic, not monotonic, and that is the fact to carry
   away. $P(k)=\sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big)$ oscillates forever, coming
   back up to near $1$ at around $3k_{\text{opt}}$ and falling again. The
   engine shows the climb back: $P=0.0656$ at $k=11$ and $0.0576$ at
   $k=13$, either side of the trough. So "run it longer to be safer",
   which is sound advice for essentially every classical randomized
   algorithm, is actively harmful here.

   The practical consequence is that Grover's algorithm needs $N$, or at
   least the number of marked items, known in advance to set $k$. When it
   is not known, you cannot just run long; the standard fix is to try
   exponentially increasing iteration counts with randomization, which
   preserves the $O(\sqrt N)$ scaling at the cost of a constant factor.

   The wrong turn is reasoning that more iterations must at worst waste
   time, by analogy with repeating a coin flip. Grover's iterations are not
   independent trials whose successes accumulate; they are one continuous
   rotation, and where you stop is the answer.

   </details>

## Summary

- Both Grover operators act within the 2D span of the marked/unmarked
  superpositions, composing into a rotation by $2\theta$,
  $\theta=\arcsin(1/\sqrt N)$.
- Success probability after $k$ iterations is exactly
  $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, a closed form checked directly against the
  engine as well as derived on paper.
- The optimal iteration count $\approx\frac\pi4\sqrt N$ is where this
  probability peaks, the source of Grover's $O(\sqrt N)$ <Term id="query-complexity-black-box-model">query complexity</Term>.
- Because $k$ must be an integer and the probability is periodic, success
  is generally high but not exactly certain, and more iterations isn't
  always better.
