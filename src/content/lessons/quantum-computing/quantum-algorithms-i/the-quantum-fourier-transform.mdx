## Motivation

Deutsch-Jozsa used $H^{\otimes n}$ as an interference step: turn phases into
measurable amplitudes. The **<Term id="quantum-fourier-transform">quantum Fourier transform (QFT)</Term>** is the
natural generalization of that idea, the same "Hadamard trick," but general
enough to extract not just a single yes/no bit but an entire *periodic
pattern* out of a phase, which is exactly what phase estimation (next
lesson) and, eventually, Shor's algorithm need.

## Mathematical Development

### The definition

For an $n$-qubit register ($N=2^n$), the QFT acts on basis states as:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "j", symbol: "|j⟩", gloss: "The input basis state being transformed." },
    { id: "N", symbol: "N = 2^n", gloss: "The number of basis states for an n-qubit register." },
    { id: "phase", symbol: "e^(2πijk/N)", gloss: "A phase that winds faster around the unit circle as k increases, at a rate set by j." },
    { id: "k", symbol: "|k⟩", gloss: "Each output basis state, summed over to build the transformed amplitude pattern." },
  ]}
>
$$
\text{QFT}|j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} e^{2\pi i jk/N}|k\rangle
$$
</EquationReveal>

This is the ordinary discrete Fourier transform, applied directly to a
state vector's <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term> instead of a classical array, and it's the
closed-form definition, not a circuit yet; the circuit comes next.

### Checking it reduces to H

For $n=1$ ($N=2$): $\text{QFT}|0\rangle =
\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(e^{0}|0\rangle+e^{0}|1\rangle\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)=H|0\rangle$,
and $\text{QFT}|1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle+e^{i\pi}|1\rangle\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)=H|1\rangle$.
So the QFT *is* $H$ for a single qubit, confirmed against the engine as
well as algebraically. That is not a coincidence of small numbers.
$H^{\otimes n}$, Deutsch-Jozsa's tool, and the QFT are the same
construction at $n=1$, and they diverge only once $n>1$ calls for finer
phase resolution than a Hadamard's single sign flip can provide.

### The product form: why a circuit exists at all

The definition is a sum over $N=2^n$ terms, which is the wrong shape for a
circuit. Gates act on one or two qubits at a time, so a circuit can only
exist if that sum secretly factorizes into something per-qubit. It does,
and the factorization is what the circuit below is built from.

Write $j$'s binary digits as $j_1j_2\ldots j_n$ (most significant first),
and likewise for $k$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Expand k in binary. Because k = k₁2^{n-1} + ⋯ + kₙ2⁰, dividing by N = 2ⁿ turns each bit's weight into a negative power of two: k/N = k₁/2 + k₂/4 + ⋯ + kₙ/2ⁿ.">
$$
\frac{k}{N} = \sum_{l=1}^{n} k_l\,2^{-l}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A sum in the exponent is a product of exponentials. Each factor now involves only one bit of k.">
$$
e^{2\pi ijk/N} = \prod_{l=1}^{n} e^{2\pi i j k_l/2^{l}}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Summing over all k means summing each bit k_l over {0,1} independently. A sum of products over independent indices is a product of sums, which is exactly a tensor product of one-qubit states: the k_l=0 term contributes |0⟩ with no phase, the k_l=1 term contributes |1⟩ with its phase.">
$$
\text{QFT}|j\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\left(|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i j/2^{l}}|1\rangle\right)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Finally, e^{2πij/2^l} only cares about j modulo 2^l, i.e. about j's last l bits. Writing 0.j_a…j_n for the binary fraction j_a/2 + ⋯ + j_n/2^{n-a+1} gives the standard form.">
$$
\text{QFT}|j\rangle = \bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\frac{|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-l+1}\ldots j_n}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

**The output is a product state.** No entanglement anywhere in it, despite
the input being an arbitrary basis state and the transform mixing all $N$
amplitudes. That is the fact that makes an efficient circuit possible.

Two features of the circuit fall straight out of this form. First, output
qubit $l$ carries the phase $0.j_{n-l+1}\ldots j_n$, whose leading digit
is the single bit $j_{n-l+1}$. One Hadamard on that input qubit produces
$|0\rangle+e^{i\pi j_{n-l+1}}|1\rangle$, exactly the leading-digit part of
the phase, and one controlled rotation is then needed for each further bit
$j_{n-l+2},\ldots,j_n$ that contributes a finer correction. Hence the
$H$-then-rotation-chain pattern.

Second, note *which* input bit leads each output qubit's phase. Output
qubit $1$ depends on $j_n$ alone, output qubit $n$ is led by $j_1$: the
factors are ordered by their leading bit in reverse. The circuit, though,
builds each phase *in place* on the qubit whose own bit leads it, so it
naturally deposits $0.j_1\ldots j_n$ on qubit $1$ and $0.j_n$ on qubit
$n$, which is the product form's ordering reversed. A layer of swaps at
the end puts each factor back on the qubit the product form assigns it to.

### The circuit

Implementing the $N\times N$ DFT matrix directly would cost $O(N^2)$ gate
operations, exponential in $n$. The product form above says that cost is
avoidable, and the standard QFT circuit instead uses
only $O(n^2)$ gates: apply $H$ to qubit 0, then a chain of controlled-phase
rotations $\text{CPHASE}(2\pi/2^k)$ from each later qubit onto qubit 0 (each
adding a progressively finer correction to qubit 0's phase); repeat for
qubit 1 using only the *remaining* qubits; and so on down to the last
qubit, which gets only $H$. A final layer of swaps reverses the qubit
order, since this construction naturally produces the output qubits in
reverse, a bookkeeping step, not new physics. Every rotation is a single
`applyControlledGate(phaseGate(angle), control, target)` call; no
multi-qubit or multi-controlled gate is needed anywhere in the circuit.
`quantumFourierTransform` implements exactly this, and its output is
checked directly against the closed-form DFT sum above for *every* basis
state, $n=2$ through $n=4$: not spot-checked, exhaustively checked.

Reading it left to right matches the derivation exactly: $H$ on $q_0$,
then two progressively finer controlled-phase corrections onto $q_0$ from
$q_1$ and $q_2$; $H$ on $q_1$, then one correction onto it from $q_2$;
$H$ on $q_2$ alone (no qubits left to correct from); finally the swap that
undoes the bit-reversal this construction produces. Every rotation shown
is one `applyControlledGate(phaseGate(angle), control, target)` call, and
every angle is exactly the $2\pi/2^k$ the circuit section above derives,
not a rounded or representative value.

### Inverse

$\text{QFT}^{-1}$ reverses every step in the opposite order, with every
rotation angle negated. `inverseQuantumFourierTransform` composed with
`quantumFourierTransform` returns the original state exactly (checked to
$10^{-9}$ on an arbitrary normalized 2-qubit state), the defining property
of an inverse, not just a name.

## Physical Interpretation

The QFT is a change of <Term id="basis">basis</Term>: from "which computational basis state" to
"which frequency of phase oscillation across computational basis states."
The circuit above needs only $O(n^2)$ gates on $n$ qubits to do it, versus
the $O(N^2)$ cost of the direct $N\times N$ DFT matrix derived above.

Getting the transformed values back out of that quantum state still
requires a specific measurement strategy, not a printout, exactly what
phase estimation (next lesson) builds. The point of the next several
lessons is what this change of basis buys you when a phase already
encodes something you want to know.

## Worked Example

**Compute $\text{QFT}|01\rangle$ for $n=2$ ($j=1$, $N=4$) directly from the
formula, and compare to the engine's output.**

$$
\text{QFT}|01\rangle = \frac12\sum_{k=0}^3 e^{2\pi i(1)k/4}|k\rangle = \frac12\left(|00\rangle + i|01\rangle - |10\rangle - i|11\rangle\right)
$$

using $e^{0}=1,\ e^{i\pi/2}=i,\ e^{i\pi}=-1,\ e^{i3\pi/2}=-i$. The engine's
output for `quantumFourierTransform(StateVector.basis(2, 1))` is
$(0.5,\ 0.5i,\ -0.5,\ -0.5i)$, the identical four amplitudes. Splitting
those four amplitudes into real and imaginary parts shows the "equal
magnitude, evenly-spaced phase" fan directly: every bar has the same
height, $0.5$, but which axis (real or imaginary, positive or negative)
it lands on cycles through all four possibilities exactly once:

imaginary part); at $j=3$ the fan reappears running the opposite direction.

$e^{2\pi ijk/4}$, evaluated at a different $j$, not four separate facts.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing the QFT with the classical FFT conceptually.** Both compute a
discrete Fourier transform, but the QFT acts on $\log_2N$ qubits via
$O(n^2)$ gates and outputs a *quantum state* you cannot read off:
extracting the transformed values back out requires a specific measurement
strategy (exactly what phase estimation builds next), not a printout.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the final swap.** Without it, the circuit still computes a
valid, invertible unitary, but not the QFT as conventionally defined
(the amplitudes land on the *bit-reversed* index). `quantumFourierTransform`
includes it; dropping it would silently break the direct-DFT-formula match
this lesson's worked example relies on.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\text{QFT}|j\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sum_k e^{2\pi ijk/N}|k\rangle$:
  the discrete Fourier transform acting on amplitudes.
- Reduces exactly to $H$ for a single qubit, verified, not asserted.
- Implemented as $O(n^2)$ Hadamard and controlled-phase gates plus a final
  swap, not the $O(N^2)$ direct matrix, exhaustively checked against the
  closed-form DFT sum for every basis state up to 4 qubits.
- `inverseQuantumFourierTransform` undoes it exactly, checked directly by
  round-trip.
