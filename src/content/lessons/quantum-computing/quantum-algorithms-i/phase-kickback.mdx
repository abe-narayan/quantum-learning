<LessonHook eyebrow="Why this matters">
The last lesson's oracle writes f(x) into a second qubit. Every algorithm from here on wants it somewhere else: as a phase multiplying |x⟩ directly, with the second qubit left over and unchanged.
</LessonHook>

## Motivation

The last lesson's <Term id="oracle">oracle</Term>, $U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle$,
is the honest definition, and it puts $f(x)$ in the wrong place. A value
sitting in a second register is a classical bit: measure it and you get
one answer, exactly as in the last lesson's disappointment.

What algorithms want instead is $f(x)$ expressed as a *phase* on
$|x\rangle$, because phases are what interference acts on. Writing that
down as a wish is easy. The question is whether the oracle you were given
can be made to produce it, without a different oracle and without extra
queries.

It can, and the whole trick is choosing what to put in the output register
before you call $U_f$. One line of algebra below does it.

## Mathematical Development

### The derivation

Prepare the output qubit not in $|0\rangle$, but in
$|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$, and apply $U_f$. As a
circuit, with the concrete $f(x)=x$ oracle this lesson uses throughout
(`kickbackF`, implemented as a plain CNOT: qubit 0 as control, ancilla
qubit 1 as target), input qubit 0 prepared in $|+\rangle$ and ancilla
qubit 1 walked from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$ to $|-\rangle$ via $X$ then
$H$:

/>

The highlighted last column is $U_f$ itself, drawn as an ordinary CNOT
because $f(x)=x$ means $U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus x\rangle$
exactly matches CNOT's action, with $q0$ (control) untouched and $q1$
(target) flipped whenever $q0=1$. Everything below derives what that gate
does algebraically once the ancilla is prepared in $|-\rangle$ specifically:

$$
U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle = U_f|x\rangle\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{U_f|x\rangle|0\rangle - U_f|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|x\rangle|0\oplus f(x)\rangle - |x\rangle|1\oplus f(x)\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

Two cases, both worth writing out fully. If $f(x)=0$:
$0\oplus0=0,\ 1\oplus0=1$, giving
$\frac{|x\rangle|0\rangle-|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|x\rangle|-\rangle$,
unchanged. If $f(x)=1$: $0\oplus1=1,\ 1\oplus1=0$, giving
$\frac{|x\rangle|1\rangle-|x\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt2}=-|x\rangle|-\rangle$,
picks up an overall minus sign. Both cases combine into one formula:

$$
\boxed{U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle = (-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle}
$$

### Why this is called "kickback"

The ancilla started in $|-\rangle$ and **ends in $|-\rangle$, unchanged**.
The phase $(-1)^{f(x)}$ that should have landed on the ancilla, the qubit
$U_f$ acts on, shows up attached to $|x\rangle$ instead: the register $U_f$
was never defined to modify. Hence *kickback*. The effect of the
computation is kicked back onto the control-like register rather than
staying on the target.

Equivalently: for fixed $x$, $U_f$ acts on the ancilla alone as
$X^{f(x)}$, the identity when $f(x)=0$ and a bit flip when $f(x)=1$,
straight from $y\oplus f(x)$, and $|-\rangle$ is exactly $X$'s
$-1$-<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> ($|+\rangle$ its $+1$-eigenstate). That eigenstate framing
is what the callouts below mean by "$|-\rangle$ is an eigenstate of the
apply-$X$-conditioned-on-$f(x)$ operation": applying an operator to its own
eigenstate only ever multiplies it by a number, never moves it.

### Linearity extends this to a superposition over x

$U_f$ is linear, so applying it to $\left(\sum_x c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle$
distributes the identity above over every term:

$$
U_f\left(\sum_x c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle = \left(\sum_x (-1)^{f(x)}c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle
$$

The ancilla factors out and stays $|-\rangle$ throughout; after
this step it can be ignored (or discarded) entirely, and what remains is
exactly `applyPhaseOracle`'s action: $\sum_x c_x|x\rangle \to \sum_x
(-1)^{f(x)}c_x|x\rangle$, i.e. a sign flip on exactly the $x$ where $f(x)=1$.
This is not a second, independent definition: it's the same $U_f$, checked
directly in `oracles.test.ts` by preparing $|+\rangle|-\rangle$, applying
`applyBitOracle`, and confirming the reduced state on the $x$ register
matches `applyPhaseOracle` applied directly, to floating-point precision.

Only the $|-\rangle$ frame shows the alternating $+,-,-,+$ sign pattern
(magnitude $0.5$ on every bar, sign flipped exactly on the two basis states
where $x=1$): the actual, computed signature of phase kickback, not merely
asserted. The $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ frames instead show an ordinary
bit flip (amplitude moves to a different basis state entirely, no sign
change), and the $|+\rangle$ frame is left unchanged, since
$|+\rangle$ is the *other* eigenstate of the bit-flip-conditioned-on-$f(x)$
operation, with eigenvalue $+1$ instead of $-1$.

## Physical Interpretation

Phase kickback turns "compute $f$" into "multiply each branch of a
superposition by a sign depending on $f$." The second form is a property of
the whole superposition rather than a bit stored per branch, and that
change of form is the entire point.

Once $f(x)$ is a phase, <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> can act on it, via Hadamards or the
QFT. Phases can cancel; bits in a register cannot. That is why this trick
is the first step inside both Deutsch-Jozsa and Grover's algorithm, and why
the previous lesson's dead end (one query, one random sample) does not
apply to them.

## Worked Example

**Confirm the kickback identity directly for $x$ such that $f(x)=1$, using
$|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$ and the oracle's action on
each basis term separately.**

$U_f|x\rangle|0\rangle = |x\rangle|0\oplus1\rangle=|x\rangle|1\rangle$.
$U_f|x\rangle|1\rangle=|x\rangle|1\oplus1\rangle=|x\rangle|0\rangle$. So:

$$
U_f|x\rangle\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|x\rangle|1\rangle - |x\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt2} = -\frac{|x\rangle|0\rangle-|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = -|x\rangle|-\rangle
$$

Exactly $(-1)^1|x\rangle|-\rangle = -|x\rangle|-\rangle$, matching the boxed
formula for $f(x)=1$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking the ancilla's state changes.** It doesn't: $|-\rangle$ is an
eigenstate of the "apply $X$ conditioned on $f(x)$" operation with
eigenvalue $(-1)^{f(x)}$, and applying an operator to one of its own
eigenstates never changes the state, only multiplies it by the eigenvalue.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming this trick works for *any* ancilla state, not just $|-\rangle$
or $|0\rangle$.** Preparing the ancilla in $|0\rangle$ gives the ordinary
bit-flip oracle from the last lesson; preparing it in, say, $|+\rangle$
leaves the state alone, since $|+\rangle$ is $X$'s $+1$ eigenstate.
$|-\rangle$ is the one preparation that turns the oracle's action into a
$-1$, which is what this derivation needs.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle$, derived directly
  from $U_f$'s XOR definition, not asserted.
- The ancilla, prepared in $|-\rangle$, is left unchanged; the
  effect of $f(x)$ is "kicked back" onto $|x\rangle$ as a phase instead.
- By linearity, this extends to any superposition over $x$, reproducing
  exactly `applyPhaseOracle`'s action, verified directly against
  `applyBitOracle`, not just defined to match.
