<LessonHook eyebrow="Putting the answer where algorithms want it">
The last lesson's oracle writes f(x) into a second qubit. Every algorithm from here on wants it somewhere else: as a phase multiplying |x⟩ directly, with the second qubit left over and unchanged.
</LessonHook>

## The right value, in the wrong place

The last lesson's <Term id="oracle">oracle</Term>, $U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle$,
is the honest definition, and it puts $f(x)$ in the wrong place. A value
sitting in a second register is a classical bit: measure it and you get
one answer, exactly as in the last lesson's disappointment.

What algorithms want instead is $f(x)$ expressed as a *phase* on
$|x\rangle$, because phases are what interference acts on. Writing that
down as a wish is easy. The question is whether the oracle you were given
can be made to produce it, without a different oracle and without extra
queries.

It can, and the whole trick is choosing what to put in the output register
before you call $U_f$. One line of algebra below does it.

## Prepare the ancilla differently and watch the sign move

### The derivation

Prepare the output qubit not in $|0\rangle$, but in
$|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$, and apply $U_f$. As a
circuit, with the concrete $f(x)=x$ oracle this lesson uses throughout
(`kickbackF`, implemented as a plain CNOT: qubit 0 as control, ancilla
qubit 1 as target), input qubit 0 prepared in $|+\rangle$ and ancilla
qubit 1 walked from $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$ to $|-\rangle$ via $X$ then
$H$:

  ariaLabel="Two-qubit phase kickback circuit: H prepares the input qubit (q0) in the plus state; X then H prepare the ancilla (q1) in the minus state; then CNOT, the oracle U_f for f(x)=x, with q0 as control and q1 as target, kicks the resulting phase back onto q0 instead of changing q1"
/>

The highlighted last column is $U_f$ itself, drawn as an ordinary CNOT
because $f(x)=x$ means $U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus x\rangle$
exactly matches CNOT's action, with $q0$ (control) untouched and $q1$
(target) flipped whenever $q0=1$. Everything below derives what that gate
does algebraically once the ancilla is prepared in $|-\rangle$ specifically:

$$
U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle = U_f|x\rangle\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{U_f|x\rangle|0\rangle - U_f|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|x\rangle|0\oplus f(x)\rangle - |x\rangle|1\oplus f(x)\rangle}{\sqrt2}
$$

Two cases, both worth writing out fully. If $f(x)=0$:
$0\oplus0=0,\ 1\oplus0=1$, giving
$\frac{|x\rangle|0\rangle-|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}=|x\rangle|-\rangle$,
unchanged. If $f(x)=1$: $0\oplus1=1,\ 1\oplus1=0$, giving
$\frac{|x\rangle|1\rangle-|x\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt2}=-|x\rangle|-\rangle$,
picks up an overall minus sign. Both cases combine into one formula:

$$
\boxed{U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle = (-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle}
$$

### Why this is called "kickback"

The ancilla started in $|-\rangle$ and **ends in $|-\rangle$, unchanged**.
The phase $(-1)^{f(x)}$ that should have landed on the ancilla, the qubit
$U_f$ acts on, shows up attached to $|x\rangle$ instead: the register $U_f$
was never defined to modify. Hence *kickback*. The effect of the
computation is kicked back onto the control-like register rather than
staying on the target.

Equivalently: for fixed $x$, $U_f$ acts on the ancilla alone as
$X^{f(x)}$, the identity when $f(x)=0$ and a bit flip when $f(x)=1$,
straight from $y\oplus f(x)$, and $|-\rangle$ is exactly $X$'s
$-1$-<Term id="eigenvalue-eigenvector">eigenstate</Term> ($|+\rangle$ its $+1$-eigenstate). That eigenstate framing
is what the callouts below mean by "$|-\rangle$ is an eigenstate of the
apply-$X$-conditioned-on-$f(x)$ operation": applying an operator to its own
eigenstate only ever multiplies it by a number, never moves it.

### Linearity extends this to a superposition over x

$U_f$ is linear, so applying it to $\left(\sum_x c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle$
distributes the identity above over every term:

$$
U_f\left(\sum_x c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle = \left(\sum_x (-1)^{f(x)}c_x|x\rangle\right)|-\rangle
$$

The ancilla factors out and stays $|-\rangle$ throughout; after
this step it can be ignored (or discarded) entirely, and what remains is
exactly `applyPhaseOracle`'s action: $\sum_x c_x|x\rangle \to \sum_x
(-1)^{f(x)}c_x|x\rangle$, i.e. a sign flip on exactly the $x$ where $f(x)=1$.
This is not a second, independent definition: it's the same $U_f$, checked
directly in `oracles.test.ts` by preparing $|+\rangle|-\rangle$, applying
`applyBitOracle`, and confirming the reduced state on the $x$ register
matches `applyPhaseOracle` applied directly, to floating-point precision.

Only the $|-\rangle$ frame shows the alternating $+,-,-,+$ sign pattern
(magnitude $0.5$ on every bar, the two bars carrying a minus being
$|01\rangle$ and $|10\rangle$): the actual, computed signature of phase
kickback, not merely asserted. Compare it against the input
$|+\rangle|-\rangle$, whose four amplitudes run $+,-,+,-$, and what the
oracle flipped is the whole $x=1$ block, $|10\rangle$ and $|11\rangle$, by
exactly the $(-1)^{f(1)}=-1$ the boxed identity predicts. The $|0\rangle$
and $|1\rangle$ frames instead show an ordinary
bit flip (amplitude moves to a different basis state entirely, no sign
change), and the $|+\rangle$ frame is left unchanged, since
$|+\rangle$ is the *other* eigenstate of the bit-flip-conditioned-on-$f(x)$
operation, with eigenvalue $+1$ instead of $-1$.

## A property of the whole superposition

Phase kickback turns "compute $f$" into "multiply each branch of a
superposition by a sign depending on $f$." The second form is a property of
the whole superposition rather than a bit stored per branch, and that
change of form is the entire point.

Once $f(x)$ is a phase, <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> can act on it, via Hadamards or the
QFT. Phases can cancel; bits in a register cannot. That is why this trick
is the first step inside both Deutsch-Jozsa and Grover's algorithm, and why
the previous lesson's dead end (one query, one random sample) does not
apply to them.

## The identity checked on one input

**Confirm the kickback identity directly for $x$ such that $f(x)=1$, using
$|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$ and the oracle's action on
each basis term separately.**

$U_f|x\rangle|0\rangle = |x\rangle|0\oplus1\rangle=|x\rangle|1\rangle$.
$U_f|x\rangle|1\rangle=|x\rangle|1\oplus1\rangle=|x\rangle|0\rangle$. So:

$$
U_f|x\rangle\frac{|0\rangle-|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|x\rangle|1\rangle - |x\rangle|0\rangle}{\sqrt2} = -\frac{|x\rangle|0\rangle-|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = -|x\rangle|-\rangle
$$

Exactly $(-1)^1|x\rangle|-\rangle = -|x\rangle|-\rangle$, matching the boxed
formula for $f(x)=1$.

## The ancilla really does come back unchanged

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking the ancilla's state changes.** It doesn't: $|-\rangle$ is an
eigenstate of the "apply $X$ conditioned on $f(x)$" operation with
eigenvalue $(-1)^{f(x)}$, and applying an operator to one of its own
eigenstates never changes the state, only multiplies it by the eigenvalue.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming this trick works for *any* ancilla state, not just $|-\rangle$
or $|0\rangle$.** Preparing the ancilla in $|0\rangle$ gives the ordinary
bit-flip oracle from the last lesson; preparing it in, say, $|+\rangle$
leaves the state alone, since $|+\rangle$ is $X$'s $+1$ eigenstate.
$|-\rangle$ is the one preparation that turns the oracle's action into a
$-1$, which is what this derivation needs.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Redo the worked-example derivation for $f(x)=0$ directly (rather than
   citing the boxed formula), confirming the sign comes out $+1$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Start from the oracle's definition and distribute over the ancilla's two
   terms:

   $$
   U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle = \frac{U_f|x\rangle|0\rangle - U_f|x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = \frac{|x\rangle|0\oplus f(x)\rangle - |x\rangle|1\oplus f(x)\rangle}{\sqrt2}
   $$

   Now substitute $f(x)=0$. XOR with $0$ leaves a bit alone, so
   $0\oplus0=0$ and $1\oplus0=1$:

   $$
   \frac{|x\rangle|0\rangle - |x\rangle|1\rangle}{\sqrt2} = |x\rangle|-\rangle = (+1)\,|x\rangle|-\rangle
   $$

   The sign is $+1$: the state is returned untouched. The engine agrees.
   Running `applyBitOracle` with the constant-$0$ function on
   $|+\rangle|-\rangle$ returns amplitudes $0.5, -0.5, 0.5, -0.5$, which is
   $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$ unchanged, while the constant-$1$ function
   returns $-0.5, 0.5, -0.5, 0.5$, the same state with every sign flipped.

   The two cases are worth holding side by side because they are the whole
   mechanism. When $f(x)=0$ the ancilla's two branches keep their labels
   and the minus sign stays where it was. When $f(x)=1$ the labels swap,
   which moves the minus sign from the second branch to the first, and
   pulling it back out front is exactly the factor of $-1$. Nothing else
   happens, and in particular the ancilla is in $|-\rangle$ in both cases.

   The wrong turn is expecting a constant-$0$ oracle to be a no-op that is
   somehow uninteresting. It is a no-op, and that is precisely what makes
   the algorithm work: Deutsch-Jozsa distinguishes constant from balanced
   by whether the phases it writes are all the same, and "all the same"
   includes all $+1$. A gate that does nothing on every input is
   information, not the absence of it.

   </details>

2. Explain why $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle$, obtained after
   discarding the ancilla, is still a valid normalized state on its own.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the phases have magnitude $1$ and normalization only sees
   magnitudes. For each $x$, $\left|(-1)^{f(x)}c_x\right|^2 = \left|(-1)^{f(x)}\right|^2|c_x|^2 = |c_x|^2$,
   so the sum of squared magnitudes is unchanged by the sign flips
   whatever $f$ does. Here every $|c_x|$ is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and there
   are two terms, giving $\tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$.

   The deeper reason the *discarding* is legitimate is the one the lesson's
   derivation supplies: the post-oracle state factorizes as
   $\left(\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}c_x|x\rangle\right)\otimes|-\rangle$, a genuine
   product state. The ancilla is not entangled with the input register, so
   dropping it is not tracing away part of a correlated whole; the input
   register already has a state vector of its own, and it is normalized
   because a tensor factor of a normalized product state always is.

   That distinction matters, and it is where the answer earns its keep. If
   the ancilla had come out entangled with the input register, discarding
   it would leave the input register in a *mixed* state with no state
   vector at all, and the algorithm's later Hadamard interference step
   would have nothing coherent to act on. Preparing the ancilla in
   $|-\rangle$ is exactly what avoids that: $|-\rangle$ is an eigenstate of
   the operation the oracle performs on the ancilla, and applying an
   operator to its own eigenstate cannot entangle it with anything.

   The wrong turn is checking normalization and stopping there. A
   normalized vector for the input register would also exist if you
   measured the ancilla and conditioned on the outcome, and that route
   would destroy the superposition the algorithm needs. What makes this
   discard free is separability, not arithmetic.

   </details>

3. Suppose $f$ is the constant-1 function. What does phase kickback do to
   $\sum_x c_x|x\rangle$ as a whole? (Compare to a global phase on the
   entire register.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It multiplies every term by $-1$, giving $-\sum_x c_x|x\rangle$, which
   is a global phase and therefore physically no change at all.

   Every $x$ has $f(x)=1$, so every amplitude picks up the same factor
   $(-1)^1$, and a factor common to all terms comes out front. The engine
   shows it directly: the constant-$1$ oracle turns $|+\rangle|-\rangle$
   into $-|+\rangle|-\rangle$, with every amplitude negated and every
   measurement probability identical.

   The comparison the question asks for is the useful part. A *relative*
   phase, where some amplitudes are flipped and others are not, is
   physical and is what interference acts on. A *global* phase is a
   redundancy in the state-vector description, invisible to every
   measurement in every basis. Phase kickback writes the pattern
   $(-1)^{f(x)}$, and whether that pattern is physical depends entirely on
   whether $f$ is constant across the register.

   This is why the constant case is not a failure of the mechanism but the
   thing Deutsch-Jozsa is built to detect. Constant $f$ leaves the uniform
   superposition unchanged up to global phase, so the second layer of
   Hadamards returns it to $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ exactly, and the
   measurement is certain. Balanced $f$ flips exactly half the signs,
   producing a genuinely different state whose amplitude on
   $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ cancels to zero. The algorithm distinguishes
   "phase pattern is trivial" from "phase pattern is not", and constant-$1$
   is the case where it is trivial while still being $-1$ everywhere.

   The wrong turn is expecting constant-$1$ and constant-$0$ to be
   distinguishable by this circuit. They are not, and they should not be:
   they differ by a global sign, so no experiment separates their outputs.
   Deutsch-Jozsa reports "constant" for both and cannot say which, which is
   an honest limit of what it was asked to decide.

   </details>

4. Phase kickback is sometimes demonstrated with the control qubit of a
   controlled-$U$ gate, not an oracle. Explain the structural similarity:
   what plays the role of "$|-\rangle$" in that more general setting?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   An eigenstate of $U$ plays the role of $|-\rangle$, and the general
   phase $e^{2\pi i\varphi}$ plays the role of $-1$.

   Line the two up. In the oracle case, $U_f$ acts on the ancilla as
   $X^{f(x)}$ for each fixed $x$, and $|-\rangle$ is $X$'s eigenstate with
   eigenvalue $-1$, so the ancilla comes back unchanged and the eigenvalue
   appears as a factor multiplying $|x\rangle$. In the controlled-$U$ case,
   put the target register in an eigenstate $|u\rangle$ with
   $U|u\rangle = e^{2\pi i\varphi}|u\rangle$, and the controlled gate acts
   as $U$ on the branch where the control is $|1\rangle$ and does nothing
   on the branch where it is $|0\rangle$:

   $$
   \text{C-}U\,\frac{|0\rangle+|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}|u\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + e^{2\pi i\varphi}|1\rangle}{\sqrt2}\,|u\rangle
   $$

   The target is unchanged and the control has acquired a relative phase.
   Same mechanism, more general phase: $(-1)$ is the $\varphi=1/2$ case.

   The single sentence covering both: **applying an operator to its own
   eigenstate multiplies by a number, and in a controlled setting that
   number lands on the control**. The eigenstate is what makes the target
   factor out, and the factoring out is what leaves the phase nowhere to go
   except onto the other register.

   This is not a curiosity, it is the next-but-one lesson's engine. Quantum
   phase estimation is nothing but this identity repeated with
   $U^{2^k}$ for a ladder of $k$, writing successive binary digits of
   $\varphi$ onto successive control qubits, and it is why phase estimation
   insists on being handed a genuine eigenstate rather than an arbitrary
   input.

   The wrong turn is answering "$|-\rangle$ generalizes to $|+\rangle$ or
   to some other fixed state". There is no fixed state that works, because
   which state is left alone depends on $U$. What generalizes is the
   *property* of being an eigenstate, and a target prepared in a
   non-eigenstate gets genuinely changed and entangled with the control
   instead, which is exactly the failure mode phase estimation's type
   signature is written to prevent.

   </details>

## Summary

- $U_f|x\rangle|-\rangle=(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle|-\rangle$, derived directly
  from $U_f$'s XOR definition, not asserted.
- The ancilla, prepared in $|-\rangle$, is left unchanged; the
  effect of $f(x)$ is "kicked back" onto $|x\rangle$ as a phase instead.
- By linearity, this extends to any superposition over $x$, reproducing
  exactly `applyPhaseOracle`'s action, verified directly against
  `applyBitOracle`, not just defined to match.
