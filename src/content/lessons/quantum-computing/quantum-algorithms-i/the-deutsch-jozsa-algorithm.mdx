## Constant or balanced, and nothing in between

$f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}$ is *promised* to be either constant (same output on
every input) or balanced (exactly half 0, half 1), never anything in
between. Classically, distinguishing these with certainty needs
$2^{n-1}+1$ queries in the worst case: check that many inputs, and if
they're all equal, it could still be balanced with the very next query
breaking the pattern.

That is what <Term id="deutsch-jozsa">Deutsch-Jozsa</Term> achieves: **one** query
to $U_f$, using the phase-kickback trick from last lesson plus a second
layer of Hadamards to turn phases back into a directly measurable answer.

Before the derivation, it is worth pinning down what that one query is and
is not going to hand you. The previous lesson queried $U_f$ on a
superposition of all $2^n$ inputs and came away with one useless random
sample. This lesson runs the same single query and comes away with a
certain answer, so something has to have been given up in exchange.

## Tracking one amplitude through the whole circuit

### The one-query circuit

Prepare $n$ input qubits at $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ and one ancilla at
$|1\rangle$. Apply $H$ to every qubit (input becomes the uniform
superposition; ancilla $|1\rangle\to|-\rangle$). Apply $U_f$: by last
lesson's identity, this multiplies each $|x\rangle$ by $(-1)^{f(x)}$ and
leaves the ancilla at $|-\rangle$, decoupled. Apply $H^{\otimes n}$ to the
input register again. Measure the input register.

### Tracking the amplitude on |0...0⟩

After phase kickback, the input register is
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle$. One identity unlocks the
rest of the derivation: how $H^{\otimes n}$ acts on an arbitrary basis
state $|x\rangle$. Build it up from the single-qubit rules you already
know.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Restate the n=1 case as one formula. H|0⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 and H|1⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2; the minus sign appears only on the |1⟩ component of H|1⟩, that is, only when x = z = 1, which the exponent xz encodes.">
$$
H|x\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{z\in\{0,1\}}(-1)^{xz}|z\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Work n=2 by tensoring the n=1 formula with itself. Multiplying the two sums multiplies the signs, so the exponents add: each two-bit output |z₁z₂⟩ picks up (−1) raised to x₁z₁ + x₂z₂.">
$$
H^{\otimes 2}|x_1x_2\rangle = \big(H|x_1\rangle\big)\otimes\big(H|x_2\rangle\big) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{z_1,z_2}(-1)^{x_1z_1+x_2z_2}|z_1z_2\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The same tensoring, n times: one factor of 1/√2 per qubit (multiplying to an overall 1/√(2ⁿ)) and one sign per bit position, accumulating to the bitwise dot product x·z = x₁z₁ + ⋯ + xₙzₙ mod 2. This general pattern is the Hadamard transform of a basis state.">
$$
H^{\otimes n}|x\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_z(-1)^{x\cdot z}|z\rangle
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Applying this identity to every term of the post-kickback state:

$$
H^{\otimes n}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}|x\rangle\right) = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x\sum_z(-1)^{f(x)}(-1)^{x\cdot z}|z\rangle
$$

The amplitude on $|z=0\rangle$ specifically: since $x\cdot0=0$ for every
$x$, that factor is always $1$, leaving

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "n", symbol: "2^n", gloss: "The number of possible n-bit inputs, and so the number of terms in the sum." },
    { id: "sum", symbol: "Σ_x (-1)^f(x)", gloss: "A sum of +1s and -1s, one per input x, with sign set by f(x)." },
  ]}
>
$$
\boxed{\text{amplitude on }|0\rangle^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}}
$$
</EquationReveal>

### Constant vs. balanced, resolved

If $f$ is constant, every term $(-1)^{f(x)}$ is the same sign, so the sum
is $\pm2^n$, giving amplitude $\pm1$, probability $|{\pm1}|^2=1$ of
measuring $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, **with certainty**. If $f$ is balanced,
half the terms are $+1$ and half are $-1$ by the promise itself, so
the sum is exactly $0$: probability $0$ of measuring
$|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, **also with certainty**. There is no ambiguous
middle case, because the promise ruled it out from the start. One
measurement, one query, a definite answer either way:

$$
\text{measure } |0\rangle^{\otimes n} \iff f \text{ is constant}
$$

Both cases are verified directly against the engine (`applyBitOracle` +
`HADAMARD` composed as derived above): a balanced function built
from `balancedFunction` gives $P(0^n)=0$; either constant function
gives $P(0^n)=1$, to floating-point precision, not approximately.
Running the full circuit for real, for both cases, makes the "no middle
ground" claim visible rather than just asserted:

## Never querying an individual input

The classical lower bound is about *worst-case adversarial inputs*, and the
quantum algorithm sidesteps it by never querying an individual input. It
queries the oracle once, in superposition, and lets <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term> between all
$2^n$ branches decide: constructive for constant $f$, perfectly
destructive for balanced $f$.

The derivation also settles the question posed before it. Nowhere in the
sum does any single $f(x)$ survive on its own: the algorithm reads the
one number $\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}$ and every individual value is spent
producing it. That is the whole trade, and it is why the promise matters
so much. A global question with a two-valued answer is the only kind this
machinery can answer, which makes Deutsch-Jozsa a clean demonstration of a
mechanism rather than a useful tool.

## Running it on the parity function

**Run Deutsch-Jozsa on the balanced function $f(x)=x_1\oplus x_2$ (parity
of the two input bits) for $n=2$. Confirm $P(00)=0$.**

$f(00)=0,\,f(01)=1,\,f(10)=1,\,f(11)=0$, two 0s, two 1s: balanced, as
the promise requires. Sum: $(-1)^0+(-1)^1+(-1)^1+(-1)^0 = 1-1-1+1=0$. Amplitude on
$|00\rangle$ is $0/4=0$, so $P(00)=0$: measuring anything *but*
$|00\rangle$ correctly reports "balanced." This is exactly
`balancedFunction(0b11)` (parity against a fixed bitmask, here both input
bits) as this course's engine builds it, and the $0$ result matches the
engine's output.

## The promise is not optional

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking Deutsch-Jozsa works for an arbitrary (non-promised) function.**
It doesn't: a function that's neither constant nor balanced can land
anywhere between amplitude $-1$ and $1$ on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, giving
no reliable answer at all. The promise is what pins the two cases to
$0$ and $\pm1$, with nothing in between.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the second layer of Hadamards.** Phase kickback alone leaves
the answer sitting as a *phase*, which no measurement can read directly;
the second $H^{\otimes n}$ is what converts that phase pattern back into a
measurable probability difference.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the constant-vs-balanced amplitude sum for the constant-1
   function on $n=1$, and confirm $P(0)=1$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The boxed result is that the amplitude on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ is
   $\tfrac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}$. For $n=1$ the sum runs over
   $x\in\{0,1\}$, and $f$ is constant-$1$, so both terms are $(-1)^1=-1$:

   $$
   \text{amplitude on }|0\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\big((-1)+(-1)\big) = \frac{-2}{2} = -1
   $$

   $P(0) = |-1|^2 = 1$. Certainty, and the engine's full circuit returns
   $1.0000000000000004$ for the probability that the input register reads
   all zeros.

   The minus sign is the detail worth pausing on. The amplitude is $-1$,
   not $+1$, so the final state is $-|0\rangle$ rather than $|0\rangle$.
   Constant-$0$ gives $+1$ instead, and the two differ by a global phase
   that no measurement can see. Both report $P(0)=1$, and the algorithm
   answers "constant" without saying which constant, exactly as its promise
   allows.

   The mechanism is that all $2^n$ terms in the sum are the same sign, so
   they reinforce completely, and the normalization $1/2^n$ divides by
   exactly the number of them. Constructive interference on
   $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ means, by conservation of probability,
   destructive interference everywhere else: all $2^n-1$ other amplitudes
   must be zero, and they are.

   The wrong turn is squaring the sum before dividing, or dividing by
   $2^{n/2}$ out of habit from Hadamard normalizations. The factor is
   $1/2^n$ because two layers of $H^{\otimes n}$ each contributed
   $2^{-n/2}$. Getting it wrong gives an amplitude of magnitude $2^{n/2}$
   and a probability above $1$, which is the fastest way to notice.

   </details>

2. For $n=1$, is $f(0)=0,f(1)=1$ (the identity function) constant or
   balanced? Compute the resulting amplitude on $|0\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Balanced: it returns $0$ on one of its two inputs and $1$ on the other,
   which is exactly half and half. The amplitude on $|0\rangle$ is

   $$
   \frac{1}{2}\big((-1)^0 + (-1)^1\big) = \frac{1+(-1)}{2} = 0
   $$

   so $P(0)=0$, and the engine confirms $0$ exactly. The measurement is
   certain to return $|1\rangle$ rather than $|0\rangle$, and the algorithm
   reports "balanced" with no possibility of error.

   The two cancelling terms are the whole story. Balanced means the number
   of $+1$s equals the number of $-1$s in $\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}$, so the sum
   is exactly zero, so the amplitude on the all-zeros string is exactly
   zero. Not small, not usually zero: zero, by an exact count. That is why
   the algorithm is certain rather than probabilistic, and why the promise
   of *exactly* half is doing real work rather than being a convenience.

   Note also that "balanced" is a global property no single query could
   detect classically, whereas the quantum circuit reads it off the total
   of $2^n$ signs in one shot. What it does not do is tell you *which*
   balanced function $f$ is: the identity, the negation, and every other
   balanced $f$ on one bit all give amplitude zero on $|0\rangle$.

   The wrong turn is calling the identity function constant because it
   "does nothing to its input". Constant refers to the *output*: $f$ is
   constant when $f(x)$ is the same value for every $x$. The identity's
   output changes with $x$, so it is as balanced as a function on one bit
   can be.

   </details>

3. Explain why $2^{n-1}+1$ classical queries are needed in the worst case
   to be *certain* of constant-vs-balanced, using a specific adversarial
   sequence of "all queries so far returned 0."

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Suppose you have made $2^{n-1}$ queries and every one returned $0$. Both
   hypotheses are still alive. $f$ could be constant-$0$, which agrees with
   everything you have seen. Or $f$ could be balanced, with its $2^{n-1}$
   zeros sitting on exactly the inputs you happened to choose and its
   $2^{n-1}$ ones on all the inputs you have not touched. Nothing you have
   observed separates the two, so you cannot yet answer with certainty.

   One more query settles it, and it must. Query $2^{n-1}+1$ lands on an
   input outside the all-zeros block you have already seen. If it returns
   $1$, the function is not constant, so it is balanced. If it returns $0$,
   then $f$ has at least $2^{n-1}+1$ zeros, which is more than half of
   $2^n$, so it cannot be balanced, so it is constant. Either answer is
   decisive, so $2^{n-1}+1$ suffices and $2^{n-1}$ does not.

   Thinking of it as an adversary is what makes the bound feel inevitable
   rather than pessimistic. Imagine an opponent who does not fix $f$ in
   advance but answers each query as it comes, always saying $0$ while a
   consistent balanced function and a consistent constant function both
   remain available. That opponent can keep it up for exactly $2^{n-1}$
   queries and no longer. At $n=20$ this is $524{,}289$ queries against the
   quantum algorithm's one.

   Two wrong turns are worth separating. The first is arguing that you need
   to query *every* input, $2^n$ queries. You do not: seeing a majority of
   zeros already rules balanced out. The second, and the more important
   one, is treating this bound as the honest classical cost of the problem.
   It is the cost of *certainty*. A classical algorithm that queries a
   dozen random inputs and answers "constant" if they all agree is wrong
   with probability under one in a thousand, and takes constant time
   regardless of $n$. Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential separation is against
   deterministic classical algorithms only, which is precisely the gap
   Simon's algorithm later closes.

   </details>

4. Deutsch-Jozsa's promise is somewhat artificial: real-world problems
   rarely come pre-labeled "constant or balanced." What does this suggest
   about its value as a *demonstration* of <Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum advantage</Term> versus a
   *practically useful* algorithm?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>A defensible answer</summary>

   Marked on the case made, not on the verdict. Here is one position
   argued in full, then the alternatives, then what does not count.

   A worked version: Deutsch-Jozsa is a first-rate demonstration and a
   worthless tool, and both halves are worth saying. As a demonstration it
   is unimprovable, because the mechanism is visible in four lines of
   algebra: kick $f$ into a phase, interfere, and read a global property
   off a single measurement. Nothing is asymptotic, nothing is
   approximate, and the exact zero amplitude in the balanced case makes the
   answer certain. As a tool it is useless twice over. The promise almost
   never holds for a function anyone cares about, and even when it does, a
   randomized classical algorithm answers correctly with probability
   $1-2^{-k}$ after $k$ queries, so the exponential separation evaporates
   the moment you accept a one-in-a-billion error rate.

   Equally defensible. You can argue that the promise's artificiality is
   the *point* rather than a flaw, since a first proof of principle should
   engineer the cleanest possible instance, the way Carnot's engine is
   useless and indispensable. You can argue that its real value is
   pedagogical and historical: it is the template, Hadamards then oracle
   then Hadamards, that Simon's and Shor's algorithms scale up, and its
   worth is measured by what it led to rather than by what it computes. You
   can also take the harder line that "quantum advantage" should be
   reserved for separations against randomized classical algorithms, in
   which case Deutsch-Jozsa demonstrates nothing and Simon's algorithm is
   the first real result.

   What a bad answer looks like: "it proves quantum computers are
   exponentially faster than classical ones", full stop. That is the claim
   the question is testing, and it is wrong in two directions at once,
   since the separation is only against deterministic classical algorithms
   and only for this promise. Also insufficient is dismissing it as a toy
   without saying what it demonstrates: the interference mechanism it
   exhibits is real and is the same one every later algorithm uses.
   Any answer needs to separate "the speedup is real for this problem"
   from "this problem is worth solving", and hold both.

   </details>

## Summary

- Deutsch-Jozsa distinguishes constant from balanced $f$ with one
  oracle query, versus up to $2^{n-1}+1$ classically.
- The amplitude on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ after the full circuit is
  $\frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)}$, derived from $H^{\otimes n}$'s
  general action, not asserted.
- This amplitude is $\pm1$ (constant) or $0$ (balanced),
  no ambiguous middle case, because the promise rules it out.
- Both cases are checked directly against the engine, not just derived on
  paper.
