## Motivation

Put a number on each claim before comparing them. At $n=20$,
Deutsch-Jozsa replaces $2^{19}+1 = 524{,}289$ worst-case classical
queries with $1$. Grover replaces about a million with about $804$. One
advantage annihilates the problem size; the other tames it. This
capstone puts the two algorithms side by side and asks the question
neither individual lesson addressed: faster at what, compared to what,
and under what assumptions? The answer to each turns out to be far more
specific than "quantum computers win."

<HistoricalMoment date="October 2019">
Google announces that its 53-qubit Sycamore processor has sampled the output of random circuits faster than a classical supercomputer could simulate them. IBM disputes the classical cost estimate within days. The claim that survives the argument is narrow and real: one engineered sampling task, on one device, with nothing promised about useful problems. Read every "advantage" claim in this lesson with that same fine print in mind.

| Deutsch-Jozsa | $2^{n-1}+1$ | $1$ | Exponential |
| Grover's search | $N$ (i.e. $2^n$) | $O(\sqrt N)=O(2^{n/2})$ | Quadratic |

### Why Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential gap depends entirely on the promise

The $2^{n-1}+1$ classical bound is only a *worst-case adversarial* bound,
and it only applies because the problem is a **promise problem**: $f$ is
guaranteed to be constant or balanced, nothing else. Remove the promise
(ask "is $f$ constant?" for an arbitrary $f$ with no guarantee), and the
quantum algorithm from this course gives no reliable answer at all (its
derivation used the promise explicitly to rule out every amplitude value
except $0$ and $\pm1$). Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential separation is real, but
it's a separation for a problem *engineered* to showcase interference, not
evidence that exponential speedups are available whenever you want one.

### Why Grover's speedup is provably optimal

Unlike Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover's quadratic speedup isn't just "the best
algorithm found so far": it's been proven (a result outside this course's
scope, due to Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, and Vazirani) that **no**
quantum algorithm can search an unstructured space of $N$ items in fewer
than $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ oracle queries. Grover's algorithm doesn't just
happen to be fast; unstructured search cannot go faster than
quadratically, quantum or otherwise. This is a meaningfully different, and
stronger, kind of result than "we haven't found a better classical
algorithm yet."

### What "quantum advantage" requires beyond a query count

Both results share a hidden assumption worth stating explicitly: the
speedup is counted in **<Term id="query-complexity-black-box-model">oracle queries</Term>**, not real-world running time.
Building a working quantum oracle for some function of interest (encoding
"is this database entry a match," say) as an efficient reversible circuit
is a separate, often much harder engineering problem than the query-count
theorems address. A quadratic (or even exponential) query-count advantage
does not translate into a faster *wall-clock* algorithm for a real problem
unless an efficient oracle exists for it. The gap between "provably fewer
queries" and "useful today" is where most of the difficulty in quantum
algorithm design still lives.

## Physical Interpretation

<Term id="phase-interference">Interference</Term> is the entire mechanism behind both results: Deutsch-Jozsa's
amplitude on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ collapsing to exactly $0$ or $\pm1$,
Grover's rotation concentrating amplitude on the marked state. Interference
is a quantum resource with no classical counterpart, and both speedups are
spent out of that one account.

What differs between the two algorithms is how much structure the *problem
itself* offers for interference to exploit: an engineered promise (total,
exponential advantage) versus a truly unstructured search (partial,
quadratic advantage, but provably the most available). That difference is
what the charts earlier in this lesson make visible: Grover's line
bends but never flattens the way Deutsch-Jozsa's constant, single-query
line does, because unstructured search leaves interference less to work
with, not because Grover's algorithm is doing anything wrong.

## Worked Example

**For $n=10$ ($N=1024$), compare Deutsch-Jozsa's and Grover's query
counts to the corresponding classical worst case.**

Deutsch-Jozsa: classical $2^9+1=513$ queries vs. quantum $1$, a
$513\times$ reduction.

Grover: classical $1024$ queries (average case $\approx512$) vs. quantum
$\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{1024}=\frac{\pi}{4}(32)\approx25$ queries,
about a $20\times$ reduction, not the $513\times$ Deutsch-Jozsa achieves
at the same $n$.

$n=10$ sits near the left edge of the charts plotted earlier, where both
algorithms' query counts are still small in absolute terms. Even here the
gap between "exponential" and "quadratic" is not a small difference, and
it only widens as $n$ grows further.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "quantum computers are exponentially faster" as a general
fact.** It's true for specially-constructed promise problems like
Deutsch-Jozsa; it's false for unstructured search (Grover, quadratic,
provably optimal) and false in general: most problems have no known
<Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum speedup</Term> at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Equating query complexity with real-world speed.** Both results count
oracle calls, not wall-clock time. A real speedup additionally requires an
efficient way to actually build the oracle for the problem you care about:
a separate, often harder, engineering question this course's toy oracles
sidestepped by construction.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Deutsch-Jozsa: exponential separation ($2^{n-1}+1$ vs. $1$), but only for
  a promise problem engineered to make it possible.
- Grover: quadratic separation ($N$ vs. $O(\sqrt N)$), proven optimal for
  unstructured search specifically, a smaller but structurally stronger
  result.
- Both speedups are measured in oracle queries; a real-world speedup
  additionally requires an efficient oracle construction for the actual
  problem at hand.
- Interference is the shared mechanism; how much of it a problem allows
  depends on how much structure the problem itself offers.
