## Putting a number on each claim

Put a number on each claim before comparing them. At $n=20$,
Deutsch-Jozsa replaces $2^{19}+1 = 524{,}289$ worst-case classical
queries with $1$. Grover replaces about a million with about $804$. One
advantage annihilates the problem size; the other tames it. This
capstone puts the two algorithms side by side and asks the question
neither individual lesson addressed: faster at what, compared to what,
and under what assumptions? The answer to each turns out to be far more
specific than "quantum computers win."

### Why Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential gap depends entirely on the promise

The $2^{n-1}+1$ classical bound is only a *worst-case adversarial* bound,
and it only applies because the problem is a **promise problem**: $f$ is
guaranteed to be constant or balanced, nothing else. Remove the promise
(ask "is $f$ constant?" for an arbitrary $f$ with no guarantee), and the
quantum algorithm from this course gives no reliable answer at all (its
derivation used the promise explicitly to rule out every amplitude value
except $0$ and $\pm1$). Deutsch-Jozsa's exponential separation is real, but
it's a separation for a problem *engineered* to showcase interference, not
evidence that exponential speedups are available whenever you want one.

### Why Grover's speedup is provably optimal

Unlike Deutsch-Jozsa, Grover's quadratic speedup isn't just "the best
algorithm found so far": it's been proven (a result outside this course's
scope, due to Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, and Vazirani) that **no**
quantum algorithm can search an unstructured space of $N$ items in fewer
than $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ oracle queries. Grover's algorithm doesn't just
happen to be fast; unstructured search cannot go faster than
quadratically, quantum or otherwise. This is a meaningfully different, and
stronger, kind of result than "we haven't found a better classical
algorithm yet."

### What "quantum advantage" requires beyond a query count

Both results share a hidden assumption worth stating explicitly: the
speedup is counted in **<Term id="query-complexity-black-box-model">oracle queries</Term>**, not real-world running time.
Building a working quantum oracle for some function of interest (encoding
"is this database entry a match," say) as an efficient reversible circuit
is a separate, often much harder engineering problem than the query-count
theorems address. A quadratic (or even exponential) query-count advantage
does not translate into a faster *wall-clock* algorithm for a real problem
unless an efficient oracle exists for it. The gap between "provably fewer
queries" and "useful today" is where most of the difficulty in quantum
algorithm design still lives.

## Interference as the resource

<Term id="phase-interference">Interference</Term> is the entire mechanism behind both results: Deutsch-Jozsa's
amplitude on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ collapsing to exactly $0$ or $\pm1$,
Grover's rotation concentrating amplitude on the marked state. Interference
is a quantum resource with no classical counterpart, and both speedups are
spent out of that one account.

What differs between the two algorithms is how much structure the *problem
itself* offers for interference to exploit: an engineered promise (total,
exponential advantage) versus a truly unstructured search (partial,
quadratic advantage, but provably the most available). That difference is
what the charts earlier in this lesson make visible: Grover's line
bends but never flattens the way Deutsch-Jozsa's constant, single-query
line does, because unstructured search leaves interference less to work
with, not because Grover's algorithm is doing anything wrong.

## Both algorithms at ten qubits

**For $n=10$ ($N=1024$), compare Deutsch-Jozsa's and Grover's query
counts to the corresponding classical worst case.**

Deutsch-Jozsa: classical $2^9+1=513$ queries vs. quantum $1$, a
$513\times$ reduction.

Grover: classical $1024$ queries vs. quantum
$\approx\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt{1024}=\frac{\pi}{4}(32)\approx25$ queries,
about a $41\times$ reduction against the same worst case the table uses
(about $20\times$ against the classical average case of $\approx512$).
Deutsch-Jozsa manages $513\times$ at the same $n$.

$n=10$ sits near the left edge of the charts plotted earlier, where both
algorithms' query counts are still small in absolute terms. Even here the
gap between "exponential" and "quadratic" is not a small difference, and
it only widens as $n$ grows further.

## Exponentially faster is not a general fact

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "quantum computers are exponentially faster" as a general
fact.** It's true for specially-constructed promise problems like
Deutsch-Jozsa; it's false for unstructured search (Grover, quadratic,
provably optimal) and false in general: most problems have no known
<Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum speedup</Term> at all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Equating query complexity with real-world speed.** Both results count
oracle calls, not wall-clock time. A real speedup additionally requires an
efficient way to actually build the oracle for the problem you care about:
a separate, often harder, engineering question this course's toy oracles
sidestepped by construction.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $n=20$, compute Deutsch-Jozsa's classical query bound and Grover's
   approximate optimal quantum query count, and compare their respective
   speedup factors.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Deutsch-Jozsa: the classical worst case is $2^{n-1}+1 = 2^{19}+1 = 524{,}289$
   queries against the quantum algorithm's $1$, a speedup factor of
   $524{,}289$.

   Grover: $N = 2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$ classical queries in the worst case
   against about $\frac{\pi}{4}\sqrt N = \frac{\pi}{4}(1024) \approx 804.2$
   quantum queries. `optimalGroverIterations(20)` returns $804$ exactly. The
   speedup factor is $1{,}048{,}576/804 \approx 1{,}304$.

   So at this one problem size Deutsch-Jozsa's factor is about $400$ times
   larger than Grover's. What matters is not that ratio but how the two
   behave as $n$ grows: Deutsch-Jozsa's factor is $2^{n-1}+1$, doubling with
   every added qubit, while Grover's is $\sqrt N \cdot 4/\pi$, growing by
   only $\sqrt2 \approx 1.41$ per qubit. Twenty more qubits multiplies
   Deutsch-Jozsa's advantage by a million and Grover's by a thousand. One
   advantage compounds, the other merely grows.

   The comparison is nevertheless unfair in the direction that flatters
   Deutsch-Jozsa, which the next question is about. Its $524{,}289$ is the
   cost of *certainty* for a deterministic classical algorithm; a
   randomized one answers correctly with overwhelming probability in about
   thirty queries at any $n$, so the honest factor is closer to $30$ and
   does not grow at all. Grover's $N$ is the real cost for any classical
   algorithm, randomized or not, and its factor of $1{,}304$ survives every
   objection.

   The wrong turn is comparing the two speedups as though the numerators
   were commensurable. They are counts of queries to different oracles
   solving different problems, and the useful comparison is between each
   algorithm and its own best classical rival, not between the two factors.

   </details>

2. Explain, in your own words, why "provably optimal" (Grover) is a
   stronger claim than "exponential separation for one engineered problem"
   (Deutsch-Jozsa), even though the numerical speedup is smaller. Then
   name a place where Grover's guarantee has already changed a real-world
   recommendation.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the two claims quantify over different things. Deutsch-Jozsa's
   separation says: *there exists* a problem on which a quantum algorithm
   beats every deterministic classical one exponentially. That is a
   statement about one engineered promise, and it collapses when the
   classical side is allowed randomness. Grover's optimality says: *for all*
   quantum algorithms, unstructured search costs $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ queries.
   It is a lower bound, proved against every algorithm anyone will ever
   write, so the $\sqrt N$ is not a current best effort waiting on someone
   cleverer. Existence claims are cheap to improve on and easy to
   over-read; universal claims are the ones you can build on.

   The practical consequence is planning. If a quadratic speedup is
   provably the ceiling for unstructured search, then any future algorithm
   promising more must be exploiting structure, and the honest question
   about a candidate problem becomes "what structure does it have" rather
   than "how fast can search get". That reframing is worth more than a
   larger number for a problem nobody has.

   Cryptography is where the guarantee has already changed a
   recommendation. Brute-forcing a symmetric key is exactly unstructured
   search, so Grover halves the effective key length: a $128$-bit key
   offers about $64$ bits of security against a quantum attacker, and
   standards bodies now recommend $256$-bit keys for anything needing
   long-term confidentiality. Note what the optimality proof buys here.
   Without it, doubling the key length would be a guess; with it, doubling
   is known to be enough, because no quantum algorithm can do better than
   the square root.

   The wrong turn is reading "provably optimal" as "provably useful".
   Grover's bound is in the query model, which charges nothing for
   implementing the oracle, and a real search problem has to build that
   oracle as a circuit. That cost is invisible to the theorem and is
   frequently what decides whether the speedup survives, which is the next
   question but one.

   </details>

3. Why doesn't Deutsch-Jozsa's algorithm work at all once the
   constant-or-balanced promise is dropped? Point to the specific step in
   its derivation that relied on the promise.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The step is the interpretation of the boxed amplitude

   $$
   \text{amplitude on }|0\rangle^{\otimes n} = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)} .
   $$

   The derivation up to here is valid for every $f$; nothing about the
   promise is used in getting it. The promise enters immediately
   afterwards, when the sum is declared to be either $\pm2^n$ (constant, so
   $P(0)=1$) or $0$ (balanced, so $P(0)=0$). Only those two cases exist
   because the promise says the number of $1$-outputs is either $0$, or
   $2^n$, or exactly $2^{n-1}$.

   Drop the promise and the sum can be anything in between. A function with
   $2^{n-1}+1$ ones on $n=3$ gives $\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)} = 8 - 2(5) = -2$, so
   the amplitude is $-2/8$ and $P(0) = 1/16$: small, nonzero, and
   compatible with neither of the two verdicts. A single run returning "not
   all zeros" now tells you almost nothing, because both a balanced
   function and a nearly balanced one produce that outcome with high
   probability, and one measurement cannot separate $P(0)=0$ from
   $P(0)=1/16$.

   Read positively, the circuit measures whether the $\pm1$ pattern of
   $f$ sums to zero. The promise is what turns that one bit into a complete
   answer, by guaranteeing there is nothing between "sums to zero" and
   "sums to everything". Without it you would need many runs to estimate
   $P(0)$, and estimating a probability to precision $\varepsilon$ takes
   $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ runs, which destroys the one-query claim
   completely.

   The wrong turn is expecting a graceful degradation, where the algorithm
   returns "roughly balanced" for a roughly balanced function. It does not
   degrade gracefully in the sense that matters: certainty is lost at the
   first departure from the promise, and $P(0)=1/16$ is much closer to the
   balanced answer than the function is to being balanced.

   </details>

4. Name one thing a real quantum algorithm needs, beyond a good query
   complexity bound, before it can outperform a classical computer on an
   actual task. Then say which of those costs the oracle model
   deliberately hides.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The one to name, because it is the one the model hides: **the cost of
   implementing the oracle as an actual circuit**. Query complexity counts
   calls to $U_f$ and charges nothing for building it. In a real search
   problem $U_f$ is a reversible circuit that evaluates the predicate you
   are searching for, and it may take thousands of gates per call. Grover's
   $804$ queries at $n=20$ become $804$ times that gate count, and if the
   classical alternative can test an item in a handful of instructions, the
   quadratic query advantage can be entirely eaten by the per-query
   constant.

   Several other requirements matter and are worth listing, since any of
   them can be the binding constraint. Enough logical qubits, which after
   error correction means a far larger number of physical ones. A circuit
   depth that fits inside the machine's coherence time, or a
   fault-tolerant architecture, whose $T$-gate cost is the real currency.
   A problem where the input can be loaded into a quantum state at all,
   which for a database search is often the killer, since writing $N$
   classical items into amplitudes already costs $O(N)$. And a classical
   competitor measured honestly, at its own best algorithm on modern
   hardware, rather than at the textbook worst case.

   The oracle model hides all of the first kind by construction, and it is
   not a flaw: abstracting the oracle is what makes a lower bound provable
   at all, and Grover's $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ would not exist without it. The
   error is in reading a query bound as a runtime, or a runtime as a
   wall-clock win.

   The wrong turn is to conclude from this that query complexity is
   academic. It is the right tool for the question it answers, which is
   whether a speedup is possible in principle and how large it can be. What
   it never answers is whether a speedup will be realized, and the honest
   reading of every "quantum advantage" headline starts by asking which of
   the two claims is being made.

   </details>

## Summary

- Deutsch-Jozsa: exponential separation ($2^{n-1}+1$ vs. $1$), but only for
  a promise problem engineered to make it possible.
- Grover: quadratic separation ($N$ vs. $O(\sqrt N)$), proven optimal for
  unstructured search specifically, a smaller but structurally stronger
  result.
- Both speedups are measured in oracle queries; a real-world speedup
  additionally requires an efficient oracle construction for the actual
  problem at hand.
- Interference is the shared mechanism; how much of it a problem allows
  depends on how much structure the problem itself offers.
