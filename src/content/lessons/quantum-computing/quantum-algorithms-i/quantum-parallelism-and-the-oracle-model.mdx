## Motivation

Put a register into superposition, apply a computation once, then read
something useful out at the end. That "somehow" in the middle is the whole
subject of this course, and it is worth understanding what it has to
overcome before building anything on top of it.

The popular version of the story stops one step early. Yes, one query to
$f$ on a superposition of $2^n$ inputs produces a state containing every
value of $f$ at once. No, you cannot read them. This lesson works out
exactly how much a measurement of that state gives you (one value, chosen
at random), and that disappointing answer is what every algorithm in the
rest of the course is designed to route around.

## Mathematical Development

### H^⊗n creates the uniform superposition

Applying $H$ to a single qubit gives
$H|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$. Applying $H$ to *each*
qubit of an $n$-qubit <Term id="quantum-register">register</Term> independently, starting from
$|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$, gives a tensor product of $n$ such states:

$$
H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n} = \bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_{x=0}^{2^n-1}|x\rangle
$$

Expanding the tensor product term by term reproduces every possible $n$-bit
string exactly once, each with amplitude $1/\sqrt{2^n}$, a direct
consequence of distributing the product over the sum in each factor, not a
separate fact about "parallelism." For $n=2$:

<QuantumStateDisplay state={twoQubitPlus} label="H⊗H|00⟩: all four 2-bit strings, equal amplitude" />

### The oracle model

To use this superposition to say anything about a function $f$, you need a
way to apply $f$ *quantum mechanically*. A classical function is often
irreversible ($\text{AND}(0,0)=\text{AND}(0,1)=0$: two different inputs, one
output, no way to run it backward), but every quantum gate must be unitary,
hence reversible. The standard fix is the **<Term id="oracle">oracle model</Term>**: represent $f$ as
a unitary $U_f$ acting on an input register $|x\rangle$ plus one extra output
qubit $|y\rangle$:

$$
U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle = |x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle
$$

This is reversible for *any* $f$, including non-invertible ones: applying
$U_f$ twice returns the original state exactly, since $y\oplus f(x)\oplus
f(x) = y$ (XOR-ing the same value twice cancels). `applyBitOracle` implements
exactly this, and this involution property is checked directly in its tests.

### Why superposition alone isn't enough

Apply $U_f$ to $H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}\,|0\rangle$:

$$
U_f\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x|x\rangle\right)|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle
$$

Every value of $f$ is encoded in this one state. That part of "quantum
parallelism" is true, and it is the part the headlines report.

But **measuring this state <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term> it to
one random $x$ and its one value $f(x)$**, with probability $1/2^n$ each,
exactly like measuring any other uniform superposition. You learn $f$ at one
point, chosen randomly, having spent the same one query a classical computer
would have spent to learn one point. No speedup has been extracted yet:
superposition alone gives you a single random sample, dressed up. For the
$n=2$ case above, measurement collapses the four-branch superposition to
exactly one of them, each equally likely:

Only one of these four branches survives measurement. The other three
values of $f$, still sitting in the pre-measurement superposition a moment
earlier, are gone the instant you look.

The $n=2$ case above is just one point on a pattern that holds for any
register size: $H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ always spreads equally
over $2^n$ branches, each with probability exactly $1/2^n$, real numbers
from `applySingleQubitGate` applied $n$ times in a row, not just algebra:

Every bar in every frame sits at exactly $1/2^n$: doubling $n$ halves each
branch's share of the total probability, even as the number of branches
doubles. Total probability always sums to $1$; only its distribution across
an exponentially larger set of branches changes.

The entire content of Deutsch-Jozsa, phase estimation, and Grover's algorithm
is a specific way to make the *<Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term>* between these $2^n$ branches
concentrate the measurement probability onto an answer that reveals a
**global** property of $f$ (constant vs. balanced; a period; a marked item)
that no single query (classical or quantum) could reveal on its own. That
constructive/destructive interference step, not the superposition step, is
where the real work happens.

## Physical Interpretation

"Quantum computers try every input at once" is the half of the story that
makes headlines, and on its own it is useless. A superposition over $2^n$
branches is not $2^n$ independent computations you get to read out. It is
one probabilistic system that yields one measurement's worth of
information, the same as any other. Every algorithm in this course earns
its speedup by arranging interference on purpose, never by superposition
alone.

## Worked Example

**A classical algorithm queries $f:\{0,1\}^2\to\{0,1\}$ on all 4 inputs to
learn the full truth table. Show that querying $U_f$ once on a uniform
superposition gives a state that, if measured immediately, reveals no more
than one classical query would.**

$U_f\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x=0}^3|x\rangle\right)|0\rangle =
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x=0}^3|x\rangle|f(x)\rangle$. Measuring this state collapses
it to one specific $x$ (each with probability $1/4$) together with the
correct $f(x)$, exactly one $(x,f(x))$ pair, the same information a single
classical query $f(x)$ would give for a chosen $x$. The other three values of
$f$ remain unknown after this one query, quantum or classical.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Believing one query to $U_f$ on a superposition reveals the full truth
table.** It doesn't: measurement still collapses to one branch. The
speedups in this course come from clever interference *before* measurement,
never from reading out multiple branches at once.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating the oracle model as free.** $U_f$ is assumed to exist as a given
unitary; building one for a specific real-world $f$ can itself be
expensive. Query complexity (how many calls to $U_f$ an algorithm needs) is
what these algorithms bound, not the cost of constructing $U_f$ itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n}}\sum_x|x\rangle$:
  the uniform superposition over every $n$-bit string, derived directly from
  distributing the tensor product.
- The oracle model $U_f|x\rangle|y\rangle=|x\rangle|y\oplus f(x)\rangle$
  makes any function reversible, hence implementable as a unitary.
- A superposition carrying every value of $f$ still collapses to one
  random sample upon measurement, no speedup without deliberate
  interference.
- Every algorithm in this course is a specific interference pattern
  designed to extract a *global* property of $f$ that a single query
  otherwise couldn't reveal.
