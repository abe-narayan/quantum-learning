## How many guesses should you expect to need?

Handed a million unsorted items and one condition to test, how many
guesses should you expect
to need? With no structure to
exploit, up to a million checks in the worst case, half a million on
average. That's the general fact for a "black box" that marks one
specific item among $N=2^n$ unsorted possibilities: a classical algorithm
needs $O(N)$ queries on average, with no way around it.

**<Term id="grovers-algorithm">Grover's algorithm</Term>** finds it in $O(\sqrt N)$ queries. That is the
quadratic speedup, and it is provably the best possible for unstructured
search (proven elsewhere, not claimed here), so the remaining gap is not
waiting on someone cleverer.

This lesson builds the two operators the algorithm alternates between. The

## The oracle, the diffusion operator, and a real bug

### The oracle

Grover's <Term id="oracle">oracle</Term> marks the target directly with a sign, using
`applyPhaseOracle`: $|x\rangle\to-|x\rangle$ for the marked $x$, unchanged
otherwise. This is the phase-oracle form Phase Kickback derived
from the bit-oracle model with the ancilla in $|-\rangle$, reused here
directly, not redefined.

### The diffusion operator, derived

Let $|s\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ be the uniform
<Term id="superposition">superposition</Term>. Grover's **diffusion operator** is $D=2|s\rangle\langle
s|-I$, a reflection about $|s\rangle$. Substitute $|s\rangle=H^{\otimes
n}|0\rangle$ directly:

$$
D = 2|s\rangle\langle s| - I = 2H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle\langle0|H^{\otimes n} - I
$$

Since $H^{\otimes n}$ is its own inverse ($H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes
n}=I$), insert $I=H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes n}$ into the second term:

$$
D = H^{\otimes n}\big(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I\big)H^{\otimes n}
$$

$2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ is a **reflection about $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$**:
acting on $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ itself gives
$2|0\rangle\langle0|0\rangle-|0\rangle=2|0\rangle-|0\rangle=|0\rangle$
(unchanged); acting on any *other* basis state $|k\neq0\rangle$ gives
$2|0\rangle\langle0|k\rangle-|k\rangle=0-|k\rangle=-|k\rangle$ (sign
flipped, since $\langle0|k\rangle=0$). So $D$'s full circuit is: $H^{\otimes
n}$, flip the sign of every basis state *except* $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$,
$H^{\otimes n}$ again.

The middle frame is where the mechanism hides. After the oracle every
measurement probability is still $1/8$, so the marked item is no more
likely than any other and measuring at that point would tell you nothing.
The information sits in a sign, invisible to the Born rule, until
diffusion converts it into a difference in magnitude.

### A real bug this distinction caught

An earlier version of `groverDiffusion` built the middle step by reusing
`applyPhaseOracle(s, [0])`, which flips *only* index $0$ and leaves
everything else alone: precisely the **opposite** reflection,
$I-2|0\rangle\langle0|$, not $2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$. The two differ by an
overall factor of $-1$, an unobservable global phase, so every
success-probability test (measuring $P(\text{marked item})$ after running
full Grover iterations) passed anyway, since a global phase never affects
Born-rule probabilities. The bug was caught only by testing the reflection
*in isolation*: $D|s\rangle$ must equal $|s\rangle$ **exactly** (not
$-|s\rangle$), and the buggy version failed that specific check. This is
why `grover.test.ts` tests `groverDiffusion` against $D|s\rangle=|s\rangle$
directly rather than only Grover's end-to-end success rate. Some bugs are
invisible to outcome-only tests, and a global phase is the classic one.

## Two reflections compose into a rotation

Geometrically, both the oracle and the diffusion operator are reflections:
the oracle reflects about the "unmarked" subspace, diffusion reflects
about $|s\rangle$, and composing two reflections is always a rotation.
The next lesson makes this rotation picture exact and uses it to derive
Grover's $O(\sqrt N)$ query count directly.

## Checking the reflection on one basis state

**Verify $2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ acting on $|1\rangle$ (a 1-qubit basis
state, $k\neq0$) gives $-|1\rangle$.**

$\langle0|1\rangle=0$ (orthogonal basis states), so
$2|0\rangle\langle0|1\rangle-|1\rangle=2|0\rangle(0)-|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$.
Matches the general rule: every state except $|0\rangle$ itself picks up a
sign flip.

## The sign convention that hides a bug

<Callout type="warning">
**Building the reflection by flipping $|0\rangle$'s sign instead of
everything else's.** This is the bug described above: the
resulting operator is $-(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I)$, differing from the
correct diffusion operator by an unobservable-in-aggregate but
structurally wrong global phase.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Testing only end-to-end success probability.** A global-phase bug can
hide indefinitely behind measurement statistics that only ever depend on
$|{\rm amplitude}|^2$. Testing an intermediate operator's exact action on
a known input (as the `groverDiffusion` check $D|s\rangle=|s\rangle$ does)
catches what an outcome-only test cannot.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Show that $2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ acting on $|0\rangle$ (for any number
   of qubits) leaves it exactly unchanged.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \big(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I\big)|0\rangle = 2|0\rangle\langle0|0\rangle - |0\rangle = 2|0\rangle(1) - |0\rangle = |0\rangle
   $$

   using $\langle0|0\rangle=1$. The engine confirms it on three qubits:
   the operator returns $|000\rangle$ with amplitude $1$, while any other
   basis state, say $|101\rangle$, comes back as $-|101\rangle$, since
   $\langle 0|101\rangle = 0$ kills the first term.

   The two cases together are the definition of a reflection. In the
   language of projectors, $2P-I$ with $P=|0\rangle\langle0|$ acts as $+1$
   on the subspace $P$ projects onto and $-1$ on everything orthogonal to
   it, which is exactly what a mirror does: fixed points on the mirror,
   sign flip along the perpendicular. Nothing here is special to
   $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ or to any particular qubit count; the same two
   lines work for $2|v\rangle\langle v|-I$ for any unit vector $|v\rangle$,
   which is how the lesson gets $D = 2|s\rangle\langle s| - I$.

   The wrong turn is expecting the $2$ to double something. It does not:
   the factor of $2$ is exactly what is needed to turn "project onto
   $|0\rangle$" into "keep $|0\rangle$ and flip the rest", and any other
   coefficient gives an operator that is not even unitary. Check it: with a
   coefficient $\lambda$, the operator has eigenvalues $\lambda-1$ and
   $-1$, and unitarity needs $|\lambda-1|=1$, so $\lambda=2$ is the only
   real choice other than the trivial $\lambda=0$.

   </details>

2. Using $D=H^{\otimes n}(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I)H^{\otimes n}$, explain why
   $D$ applied twice returns the original state (i.e. $D$ is its own
   inverse).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Square it and let the inner Hadamards cancel:

   $$
   D^2 = H^{\otimes n}R\,H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes n}R\,H^{\otimes n} = H^{\otimes n}R\,I\,R\,H^{\otimes n} = H^{\otimes n}R^2H^{\otimes n}
   $$

   writing $R = 2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ and using $H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes n}=I$.
   And $R^2 = I$ from question 1: $R$ fixes $|0\rangle$ and negates every
   other basis state, so applying it twice fixes everything. That leaves
   $D^2 = H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes n} = I$.

   The engine agrees. `groverDiffusion` applied to $|101\rangle$ gives
   amplitude $-0.75$ on $|101\rangle$ and $+0.25$ on the other seven, and
   applying it again returns $|101\rangle$ with amplitude $1$ and nothing
   elsewhere.

   The geometric reading is shorter and worth having. $D$ is a reflection
   about $|s\rangle$, and every reflection is its own inverse: reflect
   twice in the same mirror and you are back where you started. The
   sandwich form is just that same reflection written in the basis where
   the mirror is $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ rather than $|s\rangle$, and
   conjugation by a unitary cannot change whether an operator squares to
   the identity.

   The wrong turn is inferring that Grover's whole iteration is its own
   inverse, since both of its pieces are. It is not. The oracle and $D$ are
   each involutions, but their *product* is a rotation by $2\theta$, and
   applying it twice rotates by $4\theta$ rather than returning anywhere.
   That the composition of two reflections is a rotation, not a reflection,
   is precisely what makes the algorithm work, and it is the next lesson's
   subject.

   </details>

3. Confirm algebraically that $-(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I)$ and
   $2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ give identical measurement probabilities for
   any input state, using $|{-z}|^2=|z|^2$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Let $R = 2|0\rangle\langle0|-I$ and take any state
   $|\psi\rangle$ with $R|\psi\rangle = \sum_k z_k|k\rangle$. Then
   $-R|\psi\rangle = \sum_k(-z_k)|k\rangle$, and the probability of
   outcome $k$ is

   $$
   |-z_k|^2 = (-1)^2|z_k|^2 = |z_k|^2
   $$

   the same number, for every $k$ and every input. More generally
   $|{-z}|^2 = \overline{(-z)}(-z) = \bar z z = |z|^2$ for complex $z$, so
   the argument does not depend on the amplitudes being real.

   The two operators differ by an overall factor of $-1$, which is a global
   phase, and global phase is invisible to the Born rule by construction.
   That makes them the same physical operation, and either one can be
   called "the reflection" without changing any prediction of the
   algorithm.

   This is the fact behind the bug the lesson describes, and it is worth
   being precise about why the bug was hard to catch. An implementation
   that flips $|0\rangle$'s sign and leaves the rest alone computes $-R$
   instead of $R$. Every probability-based test passes, because
   probabilities cannot see the difference. What such a bug does break is
   any *later* use of the operator inside a controlled operation or on one
   branch of a superposition, where the overall sign becomes a relative
   sign between branches and is fully measurable. The test suite was
   testing the right numbers and the wrong level.

   The wrong turn is concluding that sign errors are harmless in general.
   A sign is harmless exactly when it multiplies the entire state, and
   catastrophic when it multiplies part of it. Grover's own oracle is the
   example: it flips one amplitude out of $N$, every probability is
   unchanged at that instant, and the whole algorithm depends on the
   flip.

   </details>

4. Why is "the marked-item oracle reflects about the unmarked subspace" an
   accurate description, given that it only ever flips the sign of the
   *marked* item (not every unmarked one)? (Hint: a reflection about a
   subspace and a sign flip on its orthogonal complement have the same
   overall effect up to global phase; connect this to question 3.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a reflection about a subspace *is* a sign flip on the orthogonal
   complement. Writing $P$ for the projector onto the unmarked subspace,
   the reflection about it is

   $$
   2P - I = 2(I - P_{\text{marked}}) - I = I - 2P_{\text{marked}},
   $$

   which fixes every unmarked basis state ($|k\rangle \mapsto |k\rangle$)
   and negates the marked one ($|m\rangle \mapsto -|m\rangle$). That is the
   oracle, exactly, with no global phase to reconcile. Flipping the sign of
   the one marked item and reflecting about the $N-1$-dimensional unmarked
   subspace are the same operator written two ways.

   The intuition that has to be dropped is that a reflection must move most
   of the vectors it acts on. A mirror leaves everything *in* the mirror
   alone, and the mirror here is $N-1$ dimensional, so almost everything is
   left alone. Only the single direction perpendicular to it is flipped.

   Question 3 supplies the other half of the picture. The oracle is also
   often described as "reflecting about the marked item", and that
   description corresponds to $2P_{\text{marked}} - I$, which is
   $-(I - 2P_{\text{marked}})$: the same operator times $-1$. So the two
   descriptions differ by a global phase and are physically identical, and
   the choice between them is a convention rather than a fact. The lesson
   uses the unmarked version because it is the one that matches the
   amplitude picture, where the marked amplitude is the one that goes
   negative.

   The wrong turn is thinking the two reflections are different operations
   that happen to agree on probabilities. In the two-dimensional
   $\{|\alpha\rangle, |\beta\rangle\}$ picture the next lesson uses, they
   are the reflection about the $|\alpha\rangle$ axis and the reflection
   about the $|\beta\rangle$ axis, which in the plane really are the same
   map up to sign. Composed with the diffusion operator, both give a
   rotation by the same $2\theta$, which is why the algorithm's iteration
   count does not depend on which convention you picked.

   </details>

## Summary

- Grover's oracle marks the target with a sign flip via `applyPhaseOracle`,
  reusing the same phase-oracle primitive Deutsch-Jozsa and phase
  kickback established.
- The diffusion operator $D=2|s\rangle\langle s|-I$ is derived exactly as
  $H^{\otimes n}(2|0\rangle\langle0|-I)H^{\otimes n}$, a reflection about
  $|0\rangle^{\otimes n}$ sandwiched between two Hadamard layers.
- A real implementation bug, reflecting the wrong basis state, produced
  a global-phase error invisible to success-probability tests, caught only
  by testing $D|s\rangle=|s\rangle$ directly.
