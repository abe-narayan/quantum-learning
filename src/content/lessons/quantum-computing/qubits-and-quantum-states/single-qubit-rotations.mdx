Nothing physical distinguishes a rotation by π/4 from a rotation by 17°. The six named gates you've met so far are convenient cases of one continuous family, and hardware has always been able to run the rest of it.

## Motivation

Six named gates is a start, but it's a strange place to stop. Nothing
physical distinguishes a rotation by $\pi/4$ from a rotation by $17°$, and
a microwave pulse of any duration you like is no harder to generate than a
pulse of one particular duration. So the hardware was never restricted to
six operations; only our vocabulary was. This lesson replaces the six fixed
gates with one continuous family parametrized by any angle, and shows the
six were special cases of it all along.

## Intuition

If every gate is a rotation of the Bloch vector (last lesson's closing
claim), then the next question is: what is the *general* rotation, by any
angle $\theta$, about any axis $\hat n$? Ordinary 3D rotations have such a
formula (Rodrigues' rotation formula, if you've seen it); single-qubit
gates have a close cousin of it, built from the same three matrices $X$,
$Y$, $Z$ you already know.

This family is older than quantum computing. Nuclear magnetic resonance
spectroscopists have been rotating nuclear spins by arbitrary angles with
radiofrequency pulses since the 1940s, and $R_x$, $R_y$, $R_z$ are the
names for what those pulses do.

## Mathematical Development

### The general rotation formula

For a unit vector $\hat n = (n_x,n_y,n_z)$ and angle $\theta$, define:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "Rn", symbol: "R_n̂(θ)", gloss: "The general single-qubit rotation, by angle θ, about axis n̂." },
    { id: "n", symbol: "n̂ = (n_x,n_y,n_z)", gloss: "A unit vector picking out the rotation axis on the Bloch sphere." },
    { id: "I", symbol: "I", gloss: "The identity matrix." },
    { id: "XYZ", symbol: "X, Y, Z", gloss: "The Pauli matrices, combined here according to the chosen axis." },
  ]}
>
$$
R_{\hat n}(\theta) = \cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)I - i\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)(n_xX+n_yY+n_zZ)
$$
</EquationReveal>

This looks unmotivated until you notice its resemblance to Euler's
formula, $e^{i\alpha}=\cos\alpha+i\sin\alpha$. The resemblance is not an
analogy: $R_{\hat n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta(n_xX+n_yY+n_zZ)/2}$. The reason
the expansion terminates in one cosine and one sine term rather than
running on as an infinite matrix series is that $(n_xX+n_yY+n_zZ)^2=I$ for
any unit vector $\hat n$, which you can check by matrix multiplication for
any particular $\hat n$. That identity plays the role $i^2=-1$ plays in
deriving Euler's formula itself.

Once again the half-angle $\theta/2$ appears, the same phenomenon as in the
Bloch sphere's canonical form and for the same reason: rotating the
physical Bloch vector by $\theta$ corresponds to a $\theta/2$ rotation in
amplitude space.

That claim has a consequence you can plot. Below, $R_x(\theta)$ is applied
to $|0\rangle$ for angles from $0$ to $4\pi$, with the amplitude and the
geometry read out by the platform's own `rotationX` and
`stateToBlochVector`. The two curves run at different rates. The Bloch
$z$-coordinate traces $\cos\theta$ and closes its cycle at $\theta=2\pi$,
as any rotation of a sphere must. The amplitude on $|1\rangle$ traces
$-\sin(\theta/2)$ and is only halfway through its own cycle at that point:
it needs $4\pi$ to come home.

That factor of two between the two curves is the half-angle, drawn. It also
explains the sign in $R_z(2\pi)=-I$: at $\theta = 2\pi$ the sphere is back
where it started while the amplitudes are only halfway around, sitting at
minus their original values.

Read off $\theta=\pi$ while you are here: $z=-1$, so the vector has swung
from north pole to south pole, and the state is $|1\rangle$ up to a phase.
That is the $X$ gate, recovered as one point on a continuous curve.

### The coordinate-axis special cases

Setting $\hat n$ to each coordinate axis in turn:

$$
R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta/2) & -i\sin(\theta/2)\\-i\sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix}, \quad R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta/2) & -\sin(\theta/2)\\ \sin(\theta/2)&\cos(\theta/2)\end{pmatrix}
$$

$$
R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2} & 0\\0&e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}
$$

$R_z$ is the easiest to read directly: it multiplies $\alpha$ by
$e^{-i\theta/2}$ and $\beta$ by $e^{i\theta/2}$, an overall phase
difference of $\theta$ between them. Since only the <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative phase</Term>
$\varphi$ is physical (next lesson makes this precise), $R_z(\theta)$'s
entire effect is $\varphi \to \varphi+\theta$, leaving $\theta$-the-latitude
fixed. $R_x$ and $R_y$ mix $\alpha$ and $\beta$ together, so in general they
change both the Bloch $\theta$ and $\varphi$ at once. The exception is a
state already sitting on the rotation's own axis, where (as with any
rotation) nothing moves at all.

### Reconciling with the named gates

Every gate from the last lesson is one of these, up to global phase.
Check $R_z(\pi/2)$ against $S$:

$$
R_z\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\pi/4}&0\\0&e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix} = e^{-i\pi/4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\pi/2}\end{pmatrix} = e^{-i\pi/4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix} = e^{-i\pi/4}S
$$

$R_z(\pi/2)$ and $S$ differ by the overall factor $e^{-i\pi/4}$, the kind
of global phase the Bloch sphere derivation showed doesn't matter. The
same pattern holds for every other named gate: $X=iR_x(\pi)$,
$Z=iR_z(\pi)$, and so on. "Six special gates" and "one continuous family
of rotations" are the same physics, described at two different levels of
generality.

## Physical Interpretation

This is the payoff of the whole geometric picture: **every possible
single-qubit operation is a rotation of the Bloch sphere**, full stop.
There is no single-qubit gate that does something the rotation formula
can't describe. $R_{\hat n}(\theta)$, ranging over all axes and angles,
covers every physically distinct single-qubit <Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term> (up to the global
phase that was never physical anyway). Six gates were a convenient,
standard vocabulary; the rotation formula is the whole language.

## Worked Example

**Apply $R_z(\pi/2)$ to $|+\rangle$ and identify the result.**

$|+\rangle$ has $\theta=\pi/2$, $\varphi=0$. $R_z(\pi/2)$ adds $\pi/2$ to
$\varphi$, giving $\theta=\pi/2$, $\varphi=\pi/2$, exactly $|{+i}\rangle$.
This matches the $S$ gate's known action ($S$ and $R_z(\pi/2)$ differ only
by global phase, so they must agree on where the state lands): $S|+\rangle=|{+i}\rangle$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming any rotation changes both $\theta$ and $\varphi$.** Only true
in general. $R_z$ specifically *never* changes $\theta$: it's a rotation
about the very axis $\theta$ is measured from.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Mixing up rotation direction conventions.** Different textbooks and
tools can define "positive angle" as clockwise or counterclockwise around
a given axis. This course's convention matches the matrices given above.
If your hand-computed answer's *sign* looks flipped from the simulator,
check the convention before assuming an arithmetic error.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The general rotation is
  $R_{\hat n}(\theta)=\cos(\theta/2)I - i\sin(\theta/2)(n_xX+n_yY+n_zZ)$,
  structurally an "Euler's formula for the Bloch sphere."
- $R_x,R_y,R_z$ are the coordinate-axis special cases; $X,Y,Z,H,S,T$ are
  all specific $(\hat n,\theta)$ choices, up to global phase.
- $R_z(\theta)$ changes only $\varphi$, never $\theta$, a directly
  testable, falsifiable claim.
- Every single-qubit gate is, physically, some rotation of the Bloch
  sphere. There's nothing else a single-qubit gate could be.
