## A symbol used since lesson one, unexamined

We've been writing $|0\rangle$, $|1\rangle$, and
$|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ since the first lesson
without asking what the $|\cdot\rangle$ symbol means or what rules it
follows. The notation is not deep, and this is a short lesson. But every
calculation from here on is written in it, and the half-hour spent now is
repaid in every derivation that follows.

## Why physicists write vectors this way

$|\psi\rangle$ is called a **ket**, and it's just a name for "the vector
$\psi$." Physicists write vectors this way instead of with an arrow
($\vec{\psi}$) or bold type ($\mathbf{\psi}$) mostly by convention, but the
notation earns its keep the moment you introduce its partner, the **bra**
$\langle\psi|$. A bra and a ket combine to form
$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$: read left-to-right, it looks like a
"bra-c-ket," which is where the names come from. That combined
object is a single number (in general, complex), and it plays the role
ordinary dot products play for real vectors.

## Kets, bras, and the number between them

### From column vectors to kets

So far the two <Term id="computational-basis">computational basis</Term>
states have been names, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, with amplitudes
attached to them. A **column vector** is nothing more than a list of
numbers stacked vertically, one slot per basis state. Written out that
way, the two basis states are:

$$
|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
$$

A general ket is a linear combination, $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix}$.
Nothing new yet: $|\psi\rangle$ is just notation for this column vector.

### The bra as a conjugated row vector

The bra $\langle\psi|$ is defined as the **conjugate transpose** of the
ket: take the column vector, lay it on its side, and conjugate every
entry.

$$
|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\\beta\end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\psi| = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha^* & \beta^*\end{pmatrix}
$$

The conjugation is what makes the next step work, and it is not optional
decoration: without it, $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ could come out complex,
and there would be no way to read it as a length.

### The inner product

A bra times a ket is ordinary matrix multiplication of a row vector by a
column vector, which produces a single number:

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}\phi_0^* & \phi_1^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_0\\\psi_1\end{pmatrix} = \phi_0^*\psi_0 + \phi_1^*\psi_1
$$

Apply this to the basis states themselves. Since $|0\rangle=(1,0)$ and
$|1\rangle=(0,1)$:

$$
\langle0|0\rangle = 1, \quad \langle1|1\rangle = 1, \quad \langle0|1\rangle = 0, \quad \langle1|0\rangle = 0
$$

This is **orthonormality**: each basis state has unit overlap with itself
and zero overlap with the other. Plotting a few real inner products side
by side makes the pattern easy to spot: full overlap with yourself, zero
overlap with your orthogonal partner, and something in between for a
state that blends both.

The highlighted bar is $\langle+|0\rangle$, worked out explicitly below:
neither 0 nor 1, because $|+\rangle$ blends both basis states, not
aligned with or orthogonal to either one. It's the reason
$|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are a sensible basis in the first place, and
it lets you compute any inner product without matrix multiplication at
all, just distribute and cancel:

$$
\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = (\phi_0^*\langle0| + \phi_1^*\langle1|)(\psi_0|0\rangle+\psi_1|1\rangle) = \phi_0^*\psi_0\langle0|0\rangle + \phi_0^*\psi_1\langle0|1\rangle + \phi_1^*\psi_0\langle1|0\rangle + \phi_1^*\psi_1\langle1|1\rangle
$$

The two cross terms vanish by orthonormality, leaving
$\phi_0^*\psi_0+\phi_1^*\psi_1$: the same formula as before, this time
derived rather than stated.

### Matrices between a bra and a ket

Two lessons from now, and in nearly every lesson after that, a *matrix*
appears wedged between a bra and a ket: $\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle$.
Nothing new is needed to read it, only one more piece of the same
arithmetic, so it is worth putting on the table here.

A $2\times2$ matrix acting on a ket produces another ket. Each row of the
matrix is paired off against the column vector, entry by entry, and the
products are added:

$$
A|\psi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}A_{00}&A_{01}\\A_{10}&A_{11}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_0\\\psi_1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}A_{00}\psi_0 + A_{01}\psi_1\\A_{10}\psi_0 + A_{11}\psi_1\end{pmatrix}
$$

Row 0 of the matrix meets the whole column to make entry 0 of the answer;
row 1 meets the whole column to make entry 1. That is the entire rule.
A concrete instance, with the matrix that swaps the two entries:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\cdot1 + 1\cdot0\\1\cdot1 + 0\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}
$$

so that matrix sends $|0\rangle$ to $|1\rangle$.

Now $\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle$ is read right to left and needs no new
rule at all: first $A|\psi\rangle$, which is a ket, then the ordinary
bra-times-ket inner product of $\langle\phi|$ with that ket. The result
is a single number, exactly as $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ was:

$$
\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle = \langle\phi|\,\bigl(A|\psi\rangle\bigr) = \phi_0^*(A_{00}\psi_0 + A_{01}\psi_1) + \phi_1^*(A_{10}\psi_0 + A_{11}\psi_1)
$$

In practice the fastest route is usually neither of those: expand
$|\psi\rangle$ in the basis, apply $A$ to each basis ket separately, and
let orthonormality kill the cross terms, exactly as in the distributed
inner product above. That is the method the next few lessons use.

### Three matrices worth naming now

The swap matrix above has a name, and so do two close relatives. These three
appear constantly from here on, and the very next lessons need nothing about
them beyond what each does to $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

They are the **Pauli matrices**. Their action on the basis states is read
straight off the columns by the rule just given, because $|0\rangle$ selects
the first column and $|1\rangle$ selects the second:

$$
X|0\rangle = |1\rangle, \quad X|1\rangle = |0\rangle, \qquad Y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle, \quad Y|1\rangle = -i|0\rangle, \qquad Z|0\rangle = |0\rangle, \quad Z|1\rangle = -|1\rangle
$$

So $X$ swaps the two basis states, $Z$ leaves $|0\rangle$ alone and flips the
sign of $|1\rangle$, and $Y$ does both at once with a factor of $i$. That is
the whole of what the next few lessons ask of them. *Why* these three in
particular, and what makes a matrix a legal quantum gate at all, is the
Quantum Gates lesson's subject; nothing between here and there depends on
the answer.

### Multiplying two matrices

One last case of the same arithmetic, needed once gates start arriving in
sequences. Applying $A$ and then $B$ to a ket is $B\bigl(A|\psi\rangle\bigr)$,
and the two matrices can be combined into a single matrix $BA$ first. The
rule is the matrix-times-ket rule run once per column: entry $(i,j)$ of $BA$
is row $i$ of $B$ paired against column $j$ of $A$.

$$
BA = \begin{pmatrix}B_{00}&B_{01}\\B_{10}&B_{11}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{00}&A_{01}\\A_{10}&A_{11}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}B_{00}A_{00}+B_{01}A_{10} & B_{00}A_{01}+B_{01}A_{11}\\B_{10}A_{00}+B_{11}A_{10} & B_{10}A_{01}+B_{11}A_{11}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Order matters here exactly as it did for bras and kets. Run it on $X$ and
$Z$ from just above:

$$
XZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad ZX = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

Same two matrices, opposite order, different answer. The rightmost matrix in
a product is the one that acts first, which is why gate sequences get read
right to left in the lessons that build circuits out of them.

### Norm, revisited

Set $\langle\phi|=\langle\psi|$ (i.e. take the inner product of
$|\psi\rangle$ with itself):

$$
\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\alpha + \beta^*\beta = |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2
$$

The normalization condition from Lesson 1, $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$, is now
just $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$: one clean statement, in one notation,
that will hold for quantum states of any size, not just single qubits.

One important asymmetry: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ and
$\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ are **not** generally equal. They're complex
conjugates of each other, $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle^*$.
Order matters.

## Overlap, as a complex-valued cosine

$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ measures how much $|\psi\rangle$ "overlaps" with
$|\phi\rangle$, geometrically something like a (complex-valued) cosine of
the angle between them. When we get to measurement in a few lessons, this
overlap will turn out to be the probability <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> for finding
a system prepared in state $|\psi\rangle$ to instead look like $|\phi\rangle$
once measured. For now, treat orthonormality as the algebraic fact that
makes every other calculation in this course tractable: whenever a term
like $\langle0|1\rangle$ appears, it's zero, and whenever $\langle0|0\rangle$
appears, it's one.

## One overlap, computed term by term

**Compute $\langle+|0\rangle$, where $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$.**

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Conjugate-transpose the ket to get its bra. Coefficients here are real, so conjugation doesn't change them.">
$$
\langle+| = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0|+\langle1|)
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Distribute against |0⟩ and apply orthonormality: ⟨0|0⟩=1, ⟨1|0⟩=0.">
$$
\langle+|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0|0\rangle + \langle1|0\rangle) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1 + 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

This number, $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, will resurface directly in the
Measurement lesson: it is the probability *amplitude* for measuring
$|+\rangle$ and finding "0," and squaring it gives the probability,
$\tfrac12$.

## The conjugation the bra requires

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to conjugate the bra's coefficients.** If $|\phi\rangle$ has a
complex coefficient like $i$, the bra's corresponding entry is $-i$, not
$i$. Skipping this silently flips signs in the imaginary part of your
answer.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$.** They're
complex conjugates of each other. They're only equal when the inner
product happens to be real.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $\langle-|1\rangle$, where $|-\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \langle-|1\rangle = -\frac{1}{\sqrt2} \approx -0.707.
   $$

   Turning the ket into a bra conjugates every coefficient, so
   $\langle-| = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0| - \langle1|)$ (the coefficients here
   are real, so conjugation leaves them alone, but form the habit anyway).
   Then

   $$
   \langle-|1\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\langle0|1\rangle - \langle1|1\rangle\right) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(0 - 1) = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}.
   $$

   Two things worth noticing. The answer is negative, which is fine: an inner
   product is an amplitude, not a probability. The probability of getting
   outcome $-$ when measuring $|1\rangle$ in the X basis is
   $|{-1}/\sqrt2|^2 = \tfrac12$, and *that* is what has to be non-negative.

   And the answer is not zero, which is the reflex if you are pattern-matching
   on "different labels must be orthogonal". Orthogonality holds within one
   basis: $\langle0|1\rangle = 0$ because $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ belong to
   the same orthonormal set. $|-\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ come from different
   bases and have no reason to be orthogonal.

   </details>

2. Let $|\psi\rangle = \frac{3}{5}|0\rangle + \frac{4i}{5}|1\rangle$. Verify
   $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$ directly from the inner-product formula.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The bra conjugates both coefficients:
   $\langle\psi| = \tfrac35\langle0| - \tfrac{4i}{5}\langle1|$. Now use
   orthonormality to kill the cross terms:

   $$
   \langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \left(\tfrac35\right)\left(\tfrac35\right) + \left(-\tfrac{4i}{5}\right)\left(\tfrac{4i}{5}\right) = \tfrac{9}{25} - \tfrac{16i^2}{25} = \tfrac{9}{25} + \tfrac{16}{25} = 1 \checkmark
   $$

   The conjugation in the bra is the entire reason this works. Skip it and you
   get $\left(\tfrac35\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{4i}{5}\right)^2 = \tfrac{9}{25} - \tfrac{16}{25} = -\tfrac{7}{25}$,
   a negative "length squared", which should be an immediate alarm: no
   sensible notion of length can come out negative. The conjugate is what turns
   $\alpha^*\alpha$ into $|\alpha|^2 \ge 0$ for every complex $\alpha$, and it
   is why the inner product on $\mathbb{C}^2$ has to be defined with a
   conjugate on the left slot rather than as a plain dot product.

   </details>

3. Show that $\langle+i|-i\rangle = 0$, where
   $|{+i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$ and
   $|{-i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)$. (These two states
   are orthogonal, just like $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$: there's more than
   one orthonormal basis for a qubit.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Conjugating $|{+i}\rangle$ turns $+i$ into $-i$, giving
   $\langle+i| = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0| - i\langle1|)$. Pair it against
   $|{-i}\rangle$ term by term:

   $$
   \langle+i|-i\rangle = \tfrac12\left[(1)(1) + (-i)(-i)\right] = \tfrac12\left[1 + i^2\right] = \tfrac12[1-1] = 0 \checkmark
   $$

   The $\langle0|1\rangle$ and $\langle1|0\rangle$ cross terms vanish by
   orthonormality, so only the two "diagonal" products survive.

   Forget to conjugate and the $|1\rangle$ terms give $(i)(-i) = -i^2 = +1$
   instead, so the total comes to $\tfrac12[1+1] = 1$: the answer says these
   are the *same* state rather than orthogonal ones, off by as much as it is
   possible to be. Two conjugations, both wrong in the same direction, is the
   easiest way to lose a whole basis.

   Worth stepping back: $|{+i}\rangle$ and $|{-i}\rangle$ are the Y-basis, the
   third of the three natural qubit bases alongside
   $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ and $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$. Nothing privileges
   the computational basis mathematically; it is a choice of which question to
   ask the qubit.

   </details>

4. If $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = 3-4i$, what is $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \langle\psi|\phi\rangle = \langle\phi|\psi\rangle^* = 3+4i.
   $$

   This is conjugate symmetry, and it is forced rather than conventional.
   Setting $|\phi\rangle = |\psi\rangle$ gives
   $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\psi\rangle^*$, so a state's own
   inner product with itself must be real, which is what lets
   $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$ be a sensible normalization condition at all.
   Without the conjugate, "length squared" could come out complex.

   Two wrong answers to name. Swapping the arguments does *not* just flip the
   sign of the whole thing, so $-3+4i$ is wrong: only the imaginary part
   changes sign, the real part is untouched. And it is not simply $3-4i$
   unchanged either; the inner product is symmetric only when it happens to be
   real, which is the special case that makes real vector spaces feel simpler
   than they are.

   The physically important consequence: the two orderings always have the same
   modulus, $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle| = |\langle\psi|\phi\rangle| = 5$ here, so
   the *probability* built from an overlap does not care which state you call
   the measurement outcome and which you call the prepared state.

   </details>

## Summary

- $|\psi\rangle$ is a ket (column vector); $\langle\psi|$ is its bra
  (conjugate-transpose row vector).
- The inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\phi_0^*\psi_0+\phi_1^*\psi_1$
  is a single complex number measuring overlap.
- $\langle0|0\rangle=\langle1|1\rangle=1$ and $\langle0|1\rangle=\langle1|0\rangle=0$:
  the computational basis is orthonormal.
- $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$ is the normalization condition, restated.
- $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\langle\psi|\phi\rangle^*$: order matters.
- A matrix on a ket pairs each row against the whole column;
  $\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle$ is that ket, then an ordinary inner product.
- The Pauli matrices $X$, $Y$, $Z$, with $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$,
  $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$, and $Y$ doing both with a factor of $i$.
- Two matrices multiply by the same rule, one column at a time, and $BA$ is
  not $AB$: the rightmost factor acts first.
