## Motivation

The last lesson ended with a claim: a qubit's physical state has exactly
two real degrees of freedom. Two real numbers are exactly what you need to
locate a point on a sphere. This lesson makes that connection precise, not
by analogy, but by direct derivation from $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$.

## Intuition

Picture a globe. Latitude tells you how far from the equator you are;
longitude tells you which way you're facing around the vertical axis. The
Bloch sphere reuses exactly this idea: the "latitude" angle $\theta$ will
turn out to control *how much* $|0\rangle$ versus $|1\rangle$, i.e. the
measurement probabilities, while the "longitude" angle $\varphi$ carries
the *relative phase*, information invisible to a measurement in the
computational basis but very much physically real, as you'll see in a
later lesson.

## Mathematical Development

### Removing the redundant parameters

Start from the general state, $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$,
$|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$.

**Step 1: kill the global phase.** Write $\alpha$ in polar form,
$\alpha = |\alpha|e^{i\gamma}$. Multiply the *entire state* by $e^{-i\gamma}$:

$$
e^{-i\gamma}|\psi\rangle = |\alpha|\,|0\rangle + \beta e^{-i\gamma}|1\rangle
$$

The new coefficient of $|0\rangle$, $|\alpha|$, is now real and
non-negative. (We're allowed to do this because, as the next-lesson
preview noted and as we'll prove properly soon, multiplying a state by an
overall phase changes nothing physical.) Relabel the new coefficients
$\alpha' = |\alpha| \geq 0$ and $\beta' = \beta e^{-i\gamma}$, so
$|\psi\rangle$'s *physics* is fully captured by $\alpha'|0\rangle+\beta'|1\rangle$
with $\alpha'$ real.

**Step 2: parametrize the remaining freedom.** Since $\alpha'$ is real
and $0 \le \alpha' \le 1$ (from normalization), we can always write
$\alpha' = \cos(\theta/2)$ for a unique $\theta \in [0,\pi]$: cosine is a
bijection from $[0,\pi/2]$ onto $[0,1]$ for the half-angle $\theta/2$.
Normalization $(\alpha')^2+|\beta'|^2=1$ then forces
$|\beta'| = \sin(\theta/2)$ automatically, since
$\cos^2(\theta/2)+\sin^2(\theta/2)=1$ is guaranteed regardless of $\theta$.
Writing $\beta'$'s remaining phase as $\varphi$:

$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle = \cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle}
$$

This is the **canonical Bloch form**. Every normalized single-qubit state
(up to the global phase we removed in Step 1) corresponds to exactly one
pair $(\theta,\varphi)$ with $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$, and
every such pair corresponds to exactly one physical state. That's the
"two real parameters" from the previous lesson, made concrete.

<Callout type="mistake">
The **halved** angle $\theta/2$ inside the amplitudes is the single most
common source of factor-of-two errors in this entire subject. The state
rotates through angle $\theta$ on the *Bloch sphere*, but the amplitude
formula uses $\theta/2$. Keep the two angles, the sphere's $\theta$ and
the amplitude's $\theta/2$, mentally separate.
</Callout>

### From amplitudes to a point in space

The pair $(\theta,\varphi)$ are spherical coordinates for a point
$(x,y,z)$ on a unit sphere, via the standard conversion
$x=\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y=\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z=\cos\theta$. It's
worth confirming this is exactly what falls out of the amplitudes
directly, using the inner-product machinery from two lessons ago. Define:

$$
x = \langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle, \quad y = \langle\psi|Y|\psi\rangle, \quad z = \langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle
$$

using the Pauli matrices you'll meet properly in the Quantum Gates lesson.
Working out $z$ for $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$: since
$Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$,

$$
\langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\alpha\langle0|0\rangle - \beta^*\beta\langle1|1\rangle = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2
$$

Substituting the canonical form, $|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2 = \cos^2(\theta/2) - \sin^2(\theta/2) = \cos\theta$,
exactly $z=\cos\theta$, as promised, via the double-angle identity.

The $x$ computation follows the same pattern, using $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$
and $X|1\rangle=|0\rangle$ (again, matrices you'll meet properly in the
Quantum Gates lesson), which swap $\alpha$ and $\beta$'s roles instead of
leaving them alone:

$$
\langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\beta\langle0|0\rangle + \beta^*\alpha\langle1|1\rangle = \alpha^*\beta + \beta^*\alpha = 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)
$$

Substituting the canonical form, $\alpha=\cos(\theta/2)$ real and
$\beta=e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)$, gives
$2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta) = 2\cos(\theta/2)\sin(\theta/2)\cos\varphi = \sin\theta\cos\varphi$
by the same double-angle identity, matching $x$ below. $y$ follows
identically using $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$, $Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle$, giving
$2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta) = \sin\theta\sin\varphi$. All three:

$$
x = 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta), \qquad y = 2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta), \qquad z = |\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2
$$

work directly from *any* $\alpha,\beta$. You never actually need to remove
the global phase by hand first, because (as you can check) these three
formulas don't change if you multiply $\alpha,\beta$ by a common phase.
That cancellation is not an accident; it's the content of the next lesson.

### Reading the sphere

- **North pole** ($\theta=0$): $|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle$, regardless of $\varphi$
  ($\varphi$ is meaningless when $\sin(\theta/2)=0$, since there's no $|1\rangle$
  component to have a phase relative to).
- **South pole** ($\theta=\pi$): $|\psi\rangle=|1\rangle$ (up to an
  unobservable overall phase $e^{i\varphi}$).
- **Equator** ($\theta=\pi/2$): equal-probability superpositions,
  $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle)$. $|+\rangle$,
  $|-\rangle$, $|{+i}\rangle$, $|{-i}\rangle$ all live here, at
  $\varphi = 0, \pi, \pi/2, 3\pi/2$ respectively.

## Physical Interpretation

$\theta$ alone determines the measurement statistics
($P(0)=\cos^2(\theta/2)$, $P(1)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$, you'll derive this
properly next lesson). $\varphi$ determines *nothing* about a
computational-basis measurement by itself, yet it is not discardable
information: it's what makes $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, same $\theta$,
different $\varphi$, behave completely differently once gates get
involved. Geometrically, latitude is "how much," longitude is "in what
phase."

## Worked Example

**Find the state and Bloch point for $\theta=\pi/3$, $\varphi=\pi/2$.**

$$
\alpha = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \beta = e^{i\pi/2}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = i\cdot\frac12 = \frac{i}{2}
$$

$$
x = \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot0 = 0
$$

$$
y = \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot1 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad z = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac12
$$

Set $\theta$ and $\varphi$ to these values in the simulator above (in
degrees: $\theta=60°$, $\varphi=90°$) and confirm $\alpha \approx 0.87$,
$\beta \approx 0.50i$, matching $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx0.87$ and
$\frac12=0.50$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing the Bloch angle $\theta$ with a phase.** $\theta$ is a
*probability* parameter (latitude), not a phase. Only $\varphi$ is a
phase (longitude). Mixing these up is easy since both are angles measured
in radians or degrees.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Expecting $\varphi$ to mean something at the poles.** At $\theta=0$ or
$\theta=\pi$, the state is $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ exactly, and
$\varphi$ has no effect: there's no second component for it to be a
phase *relative to*.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Removing global phase and reparametrizing gives the canonical form
  $|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$.
- $(\theta,\varphi)$ map to Bloch coordinates
  $x=\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y=\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z=\cos\theta$,
  equivalently $x=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)$,
  $y=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)$, $z=|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2$.
- $\theta$ (latitude) controls measurement probability; $\varphi$
  (longitude) is the relative phase.
- The poles are $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$; the equator is the set of
  equal-probability superpositions, distinguished only by phase.
