A qubit's physical state has exactly two real degrees of freedom, and two real numbers are what it takes to locate a point on a sphere.
</LessonHook>

## Counting to two, then building the sphere

The last lesson counted the parameters and stopped there. Counting to two
is not the same as producing the sphere, though, and the derivation below
does the second thing: it starts from $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ and
arrives at a latitude and a longitude, with nothing left over. The payoff
is that from here on you can often answer a question by looking at a
picture instead of multiplying matrices.

## Latitude and longitude, reused

Picture a globe. Latitude tells you how far from the equator you are;
longitude tells you which way you're facing around the vertical axis. The
Bloch sphere reuses this idea: the "latitude" angle $\theta$ will
turn out to control *how much* $|0\rangle$ versus $|1\rangle$, i.e. the
measurement probabilities, while the "longitude" angle $\varphi$ carries
the <Term id="global-relative-phase">relative phase</Term>, information invisible to a measurement in the
computational basis but very much physically real, as you'll see in a
later lesson.

## From two amplitudes to two angles

### Removing the redundant parameters

Start from the general state, $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$,
$|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$.

**Step 1: kill the global phase.** Write $\alpha$ in polar form,
$\alpha = |\alpha|e^{i\gamma}$. Multiply the *entire state* by $e^{-i\gamma}$:

$$
e^{-i\gamma}|\psi\rangle = |\alpha|\,|0\rangle + \beta e^{-i\gamma}|1\rangle
$$

The new coefficient of $|0\rangle$, $|\alpha|$, is now real and
non-negative. (We're allowed to do this because multiplying a state by an
overall phase changes nothing physical: a fact borrowed here, flagged as
borrowed in the last lesson's parameter count, and proved in Global Phase
vs. Relative Phase later in this course.) Relabel the new coefficients
$\alpha' = |\alpha| \geq 0$ and $\beta' = \beta e^{-i\gamma}$, so
$|\psi\rangle$'s *physics* is fully captured by $\alpha'|0\rangle+\beta'|1\rangle$
with $\alpha'$ real.

**Step 2: parametrize the remaining freedom.** Since $\alpha'$ is real
and $0 \le \alpha' \le 1$ (from normalization), we can always write
$\alpha' = \cos(\theta/2)$ for a unique $\theta \in [0,\pi]$: cosine is a
bijection from $[0,\pi/2]$ onto $[0,1]$ for the half-angle $\theta/2$.
Normalization $(\alpha')^2+|\beta'|^2=1$ then forces
$|\beta'| = \sin(\theta/2)$ automatically, since
$\cos^2(\theta/2)+\sin^2(\theta/2)=1$ is guaranteed regardless of $\theta$.
Writing $\beta'$'s remaining phase as $\varphi$:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "theta", symbol: "θ", gloss: "The latitude angle. Controls how much |0⟩ versus |1⟩, i.e. the measurement probabilities." },
    { id: "phi", symbol: "φ", gloss: "The longitude angle. Carries the relative phase: invisible to a computational-basis measurement, but physically real." },
    { id: "zero", symbol: "|0⟩", gloss: "The north pole of the sphere." },
    { id: "one", symbol: "|1⟩", gloss: "The south pole of the sphere." },
  ]}
>
$$
\boxed{|\psi\rangle = \cos\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle}
$$
</EquationReveal>

This is the **canonical Bloch form**. Every normalized single-qubit state
(up to the global phase we removed in Step 1) corresponds to exactly one
pair $(\theta,\varphi)$ with $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, $\varphi\in[0,2\pi)$, and
every such pair corresponds to exactly one physical state. That's the
"two real parameters" from the previous lesson, made concrete.

<Callout type="warning">
The **halved** angle $\theta/2$ inside the amplitudes is the single most
common source of factor-of-two errors in this entire subject. The state
rotates through angle $\theta$ on the *Bloch sphere*, but the amplitude
formula uses $\theta/2$. Keep the two angles, the sphere's $\theta$ and
the amplitude's $\theta/2$, mentally separate.
</Callout>

### From amplitudes to a point in space

The pair $(\theta,\varphi)$ are spherical coordinates for a point
$(x,y,z)$ on a unit sphere, via the standard conversion
$x=\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y=\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z=\cos\theta$. The
inner-product machinery from two lessons ago confirms this falls
directly out of the amplitudes themselves. Define:

$$
x = \langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle, \quad y = \langle\psi|Y|\psi\rangle, \quad z = \langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle
$$

Both halves of that are already on the table. A matrix wedged between a
bra and a ket is the $\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle$ form from Dirac Notation:
apply the matrix to the ket first, then take the ordinary inner product of
the bra with the result, and a single number comes out. The three matrices
are the <Term id="pauli-matrices">Pauli matrices</Term> $X$, $Y$ and $Z$,
written out and read off their columns in that same lesson:

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$

Nothing here needs more of them than their action on the two basis states,
which Dirac Notation lists in full; the Quantum Gates lesson takes up the
separate question of what makes these three legal gates.

Working out $z$ for $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$: from
$Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle$ and $Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle$,

$$
\langle\psi|Z|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\alpha\langle0|0\rangle - \beta^*\beta\langle1|1\rangle = |\alpha|^2 - |\beta|^2
$$

Two standard trigonometric identities finish each of the three
calculations, so they are worth having in front of you:
$\cos^2 A - \sin^2 A = \cos 2A$ and $2\sin A\cos A = \sin 2A$. Both are
read here with $A = \theta/2$, which is where the sphere's full angle
$\theta$ comes back out of the half-angles in the amplitudes.

Substituting the canonical form and using the first of those,
$|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2 = \cos^2(\theta/2) - \sin^2(\theta/2) = \cos\theta$,
which is the $z=\cos\theta$ promised above.

The $x$ computation follows the same pattern, using $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$
and $X|1\rangle=|0\rangle$ (read off $X$'s columns above the same way),
which swap $\alpha$ and $\beta$'s roles instead of leaving them alone:

$$
\langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle = \alpha^*\beta\langle0|0\rangle + \beta^*\alpha\langle1|1\rangle = \alpha^*\beta + \beta^*\alpha = 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)
$$

Substituting the canonical form, $\alpha=\cos(\theta/2)$ real and
$\beta=e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)$, gives
$2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta) = 2\cos(\theta/2)\sin(\theta/2)\cos\varphi = \sin\theta\cos\varphi$
by the second identity, matching $x$ below. $y$ follows
identically using $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$, $Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle$, giving
$2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta) = \sin\theta\sin\varphi$. All three:

$$
x = 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta), \qquad y = 2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta), \qquad z = |\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2
$$

work directly from *any* $\alpha,\beta$. You never need to remove the
global phase by hand first, because (as you can check) these three
formulas don't change if you multiply $\alpha,\beta$ by a common phase.
That cancellation is the content of Global Phase vs. Relative Phase,
later in this course, not a coincidence.

### Reading the sphere

- **North pole** ($\theta=0$): $|\psi\rangle=|0\rangle$, regardless of $\varphi$
  ($\varphi$ is meaningless when $\sin(\theta/2)=0$, since there's no $|1\rangle$
  component to have a phase relative to).
- **South pole** ($\theta=\pi$): $|\psi\rangle=|1\rangle$ (up to an
  unobservable overall phase $e^{i\varphi}$).
- **Equator** ($\theta=\pi/2$): equal-probability superpositions,
  $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle)$. $|+\rangle$,
  $|-\rangle$, $|{+i}\rangle$, $|{-i}\rangle$ all live here, at
  $\varphi = 0, \pi, \pi/2, 3\pi/2$ respectively.

## What each angle controls

$\theta$ alone determines the measurement statistics
($P(0)=\cos^2(\theta/2)$, $P(1)=\sin^2(\theta/2)$, you'll derive this
properly next lesson). $\varphi$ determines *nothing* about a
computational-basis measurement by itself, yet it is not discardable
information: it is what makes $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, same $\theta$
and different $\varphi$, behave differently once gates get involved.
Geometrically, latitude is "how much," longitude is "in what phase."

## The state at one specific latitude and longitude

**Find the state and Bloch point for $\theta=\pi/3$, $\varphi=\pi/2$.**

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Plug θ=π/3, φ=π/2 directly into the canonical Bloch form.">
$$
\alpha = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad \beta = e^{i\pi/2}\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{6}\right) = i\cdot\frac12 = \frac{i}{2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Use x = sinθ cosφ from the amplitude-to-coordinate conversion above.">
$$
x = \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot0 = 0
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Use y = sinθ sinφ and z = cosθ from the same conversion.">
$$
y = \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot1 = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}, \qquad z = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{3}\right) = \frac12
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Set $\theta$ and $\varphi$ to these values in the simulator above (in
degrees: $\theta=60°$, $\varphi=90°$) and confirm $\alpha \approx 0.87$,
$\beta \approx 0.50i$, matching $\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx0.87$ and
$\frac12=0.50$.

## Theta is a probability, not a phase

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing the Bloch angle $\theta$ with a phase.** $\theta$ is a
*probability* parameter (latitude), not a phase. Only $\varphi$ is a
phase (longitude). Mixing these up is easy since both are angles measured
in radians or degrees.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Expecting $\varphi$ to mean something at the poles.** At $\theta=0$ or
$\theta=\pi$, the state is $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ exactly, and
$\varphi$ has no effect: there's no second component for it to be a
phase *relative to*.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Find $(\theta,\varphi)$ for the state $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}|1\rangle$
   (assume both coefficients are real and positive).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \theta = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 120°, \qquad \varphi = 0.
   $$

   Match the state against the canonical form
   $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$.
   The first coefficient gives $\cos(\theta/2) = \tfrac12$, so
   $\theta/2 = \pi/3$ and $\theta = 2\pi/3$. Check it against the second:
   $\sin(\pi/3) = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \checkmark$. Both coefficients are real and
   positive, so $e^{i\varphi} = 1$ and $\varphi = 0$: the state sits in the
   $x$-$z$ plane, on the front of the sphere.

   The half-angle is where this goes wrong. Reading $\cos\theta = \tfrac12$
   instead of $\cos(\theta/2) = \tfrac12$ gives $\theta = \pi/3 = 60°$, exactly
   half the right latitude, and it will then disagree with every probability
   you compute from it. The factor of two is not decoration: amplitudes live on
   a sphere of half-angles precisely so that two *orthogonal* states, which are
   $90°$ apart in amplitude space, land at antipodal points $180°$ apart on the
   Bloch sphere. Without it, $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ would not be opposite
   poles.

   Sanity check with the Born rule: $P(0) = \cos^2(\theta/2) = \tfrac14$ and
   $P(1) = \sin^2(\theta/2) = \tfrac34$, which is what the amplitudes say
   directly.

   </details>

2. Two states have the same $\theta$ but $\varphi$ differing by $\pi$.
   Where do they sit relative to each other on the sphere?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Same latitude, opposite longitudes: directly across the sphere from each
   other *horizontally*, on the same horizontal circle. If one has Bloch
   vector $(x, y, z)$, the other has $(-x, -y, z)$. The $z$-coordinate is set
   by $\theta$ alone and does not move.

   The tempting wrong answer is "antipodal", and it is worth being precise
   about why not. Antipodal means $(-x,-y,-z)$, which needs $\theta \to \pi-\theta$
   as well; here the latitude is held fixed. So these two are antipodal only in
   the one case where $z=0$ already, the equator, $\theta = \pi/2$. That case is
   familiar: $\varphi = 0$ gives $|+\rangle$ and $\varphi = \pi$ gives
   $|-\rangle$, genuinely opposite points, and genuinely orthogonal.

   Off the equator they are *not* orthogonal. Take $\theta = \pi/3$: the two
   states are $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|0\rangle \pm \tfrac12|1\rangle$, and their
   overlap is $\tfrac34 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$, not 0. This is the payoff of
   the half-angle again. Orthogonality on the Bloch sphere means antipodal, and
   a longitude flip alone is not antipodal.

   </details>

3. A state sits at Bloch point $(x,y,z) = (0, 0, -1)$. Write down
   $|\psi\rangle$ in terms of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $|\psi\rangle = |1\rangle$.

   The point is the south pole, so $z = \cos\theta = -1$ gives $\theta = \pi$.
   Substituting into the canonical form:

   $$
   |\psi\rangle = \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)|1\rangle = 0\cdot|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}|1\rangle.
   $$

   The $|0\rangle$ amplitude vanishes, so the only surviving coefficient is
   $e^{i\varphi}$ on $|1\rangle$, and a phase multiplying the *whole* state is a
   global phase with no physical content. Drop it and the state is $|1\rangle$.

   That is also the answer to "which $\varphi$?": none of them, and all of
   them. At the pole, longitude is undefined, exactly as it is at the Earth's
   North Pole, where every meridian meets. Question 4 is this same fact stated
   as a general principle.

   The mistake to avoid is reading $z=-1$ as "probability $-1$" or as needing a
   negative amplitude. $z$ is $\langle Z\rangle$, the *expectation value* of the
   $Z$ measurement, and $z = P(0)-P(1) = 0-1 = -1$ means outcome 1 with
   certainty. Nothing about the amplitude is negative.

   </details>

4. Explain, using the canonical form, why the north and south poles are
   the *only* points on the sphere where $\varphi$ has no physical effect.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Look at where $\varphi$ appears:

   $$
   |\psi\rangle = \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)|0\rangle + e^{i\varphi}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)|1\rangle.
   $$

   $e^{i\varphi}$ sits on exactly one of the two terms. That makes it a
   *relative* phase, and a relative phase is physical, so long as there really
   are two terms to be relative between. It stops being physical in precisely
   the two cases where one of the coefficients is zero.

   At the north pole, $\theta = 0$, so $\sin(\theta/2) = 0$. The $e^{i\varphi}$
   multiplies zero, and the state is $|0\rangle$ whatever $\varphi$ is.

   At the south pole, $\theta = \pi$, so $\cos(\theta/2) = 0$. Now
   $e^{i\varphi}$ is the only coefficient left, so it multiplies the entire
   state: it has been promoted from a relative phase to a global one, and
   global phases are unobservable.

   Anywhere else, $0 < \theta < \pi$, both $\cos(\theta/2)$ and $\sin(\theta/2)$
   are strictly nonzero, so $e^{i\varphi}$ is genuinely relative and moving
   $\varphi$ moves the state to a different point. That is the "only": the two
   escape routes are "the phase multiplies nothing" and "the phase multiplies
   everything", and each needs one coefficient to vanish, which happens at one
   pole each.

   The count agrees with the geometry. A sphere needs two coordinates, and the
   poles are the two points where the second one degenerates, the same
   coordinate singularity that makes longitude meaningless at the Earth's poles.

   </details>

## Summary

- Removing global phase and reparametrizing gives the canonical form
  $|\psi\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$.
- $(\theta,\varphi)$ map to Bloch coordinates
  $x=\sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y=\sin\theta\sin\varphi$, $z=\cos\theta$,
  equivalently $x=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)$,
  $y=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)$, $z=|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2$.
- $\theta$ (latitude) controls measurement probability; $\varphi$
  (longitude) is the relative phase.
- The poles are $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$; the equator is the set of
  equal-probability superpositions, distinguished only by phase.
