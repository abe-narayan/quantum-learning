## Twice asked to take something on faith

You have twice been asked to accept a result on faith. $Y|0\rangle$ and
$X|0\rangle$ differ by a factor of $i$, yet sit at the same point on the
Bloch sphere. $R_z(2\pi) = -I$ flips the sign of every amplitude, yet no
experiment can detect that it ran. In both cases the state's numbers changed
and the physics did not.

That should bother you. The amplitudes are supposed to be the complete
description of the state, so how can two different sets of numbers describe
the same thing? The answer is that a phase can enter a state in two
structurally different ways, and only one of them is a change of state at
all. This lesson draws that line and proves where it falls.

## Rotating the photograph, or one figure inside it

**Global phase** multiplies an entire state by the same factor:
$|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$. Every amplitude gets rotated by
the same angle in the complex plane, together, all at once, like
rotating a photograph; the picture doesn't change, only your description
of "which way is up" does.

**Relative phase** changes the phase of one amplitude *relative to* the
other: $\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle \to \alpha|0\rangle+e^{i\delta}\beta|1\rangle$.
This is not a re-labeling. It rotates the state to a different point on the
Bloch sphere (a different $\varphi$), and that rotation is measurable.

## One phase proven invisible, the other proven not

### Global phase is unobservable

**Claim:** for any orthonormal basis $\{|e_0\rangle,|e_1\rangle\}$ and any
real $\gamma$, measuring $e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$ gives the same
probabilities as measuring $|\psi\rangle$.

**Proof.** By the <Term id="born-rule">measurement postulate</Term>,

$$
P(e_i) = |\langle e_i|(e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle)|^2 = |e^{i\gamma}|^2|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2
$$

using $|e^{i\gamma}|=1$ for any real $\gamma$, since a phase factor never
changes a modulus. The right-hand side is the probability computed from
$|\psi\rangle$ alone, with no $\gamma$ in it anywhere. Since this holds for
*every* basis, not just the computational one, global phase is unobservable
by any measurement whatsoever. Not "hard to detect": provably, permanently
invisible.

The same fact shows up geometrically: the Bloch-vector formulas
$x=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)$, $y=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)$,
$z=|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2$ depend on $\alpha,\beta$ only through
$\alpha^*\beta$, $|\alpha|^2$, $|\beta|^2$, and substituting
$\alpha\to e^{i\gamma}\alpha$, $\beta\to e^{i\gamma}\beta$ leaves every one
of those combinations unchanged, since $(e^{i\gamma}\alpha)^*(e^{i\gamma}\beta)=e^{-i\gamma}e^{i\gamma}\alpha^*\beta=\alpha^*\beta$.
Global phase moves nothing on the sphere. This is *why* the simulator you've
been using has no "global phase" control: there is nothing for such a
control to do.

The Bloch sphere shows the *consequence* of that proof: nothing moves. The
amplitude picture below shows the *mechanism*. Drag γ and both arrows sweep
around the plane together, in lockstep, while P(0), P(1), and the $\alpha^*\beta$
cross-term underneath stay frozen. Drag δ instead and only β's arrow moves.
That is the difference between rotating the whole picture and rotating one
part of it against the other, made visible amplitude by amplitude rather
than point by point on the sphere.

### Relative phase is observable

Multiply only $\beta$ this time: $\beta \to e^{i\delta}\beta$. In the
computational basis this changes nothing, since
$|e^{i\delta}\beta|^2=|\beta|^2$, which is why relative phase stayed
invisible for the first several lessons of this course, where every
measurement was implicitly in the computational basis. But recall the
X-basis result from the Measurement lesson:

$$
P(+) = \frac{1+2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)}{2}
$$

Replace $\beta$ with $e^{i\delta}\beta$:

$$
P(+)_{\text{new}} = \frac{1+2\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\delta}\alpha^*\beta)}{2}
$$

This depends on $\delta$. Take $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$: as
$\operatorname{Re}(e^{i\delta})$ sweeps from $1$ to $-1$, $P(+)$ runs from
$1$ at $\delta=0$ down to $0$ at $\delta=\pi$. Relative phase is measurable,
just not in the computational basis. It is the $\varphi$ coordinate on the
Bloch sphere, and it is what $S$, $T$, and $R_z$ manipulate.

Plotting both bases against $\delta$ at once puts the two claims of this
lesson side by side. Every point below is computed by the same
`crossBasisProbability` routine the platform's amplitude tooling uses,
evaluated on the state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|0\rangle + e^{i\delta}|1\rangle\right)$:

The flat line is why relative phase can hide: measure in the computational
basis and you learn nothing about $\delta$, no matter what value it takes.
The curve is why it is nonetheless real. One change of basis and the same
$\delta$ swings a probability across its entire range. A global phase would
leave *both* curves flat.

### Resolving the two open threads

**$Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$ vs. $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$:** here $\alpha=0$ for
both, so there is no second amplitude for a phase to be "relative" to.
The factor of $i$ is necessarily a *global* phase on the single nonzero
term, hence physically invisible. That's why both land at the same Bloch
point.

**$R_z(2\pi)=-I$:** multiplies both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ by $-1$ equally,
a global phase ($\gamma=\pi$) applied to the whole state, not a relative
one. Unobservable, as the simulator showed.

## A redundancy on one side, real physics on the other

Global phase is a redundancy in the mathematical description: the same
physical state can be written infinitely many ways, differing by
$e^{i\gamma}$, and no experiment can prefer one writing over another.
Relative phase is the opposite: it is the information <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term>
depends on, and it is what every phase gate in this course was built to
manipulate. Conflating the two by dismissing *any* phase as "just a global
phase, ignore it" is a common and expensive mistake; algorithms like
Deutsch-Jozsa and Grover do all their real work in the phases that get
dismissed. The point of this lesson is that you never have to guess which
kind you are looking at. Check whether the factor multiplies every
amplitude or only some of them.

## Telling the two kinds apart on a concrete pair

**Do $|\psi_1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$ and
$|\psi_2\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)$ differ by a global
phase, a relative phase, or neither?**

If they differed by global phase, $|\psi_2\rangle = e^{i\gamma}|\psi_1\rangle$
for some $\gamma$ would require *both* coefficients to pick up the same
factor $e^{i\gamma}$. But $|\psi_1\rangle$'s coefficients are $(1,1)/\sqrt2$
and $|\psi_2\rangle$'s are $(1,-1)/\sqrt2$: going from the first pair to
the second multiplies only the *second* entry by $-1$, not both entries
by a common factor. That's a relative phase ($\delta=\pi$, since
$e^{i\pi}=-1$), not a global one, consistent with $|\psi_1\rangle=|+\rangle$
and $|\psi_2\rangle=|-\rangle$ sitting at opposite, physically distinct
points on the equator.

## Decide which kind before dismissing it

<Callout type="mistake">
**"It's just a phase, so it doesn't matter."** Half-true, dangerously so.
*Global* phase never matters. *Relative* phase can matter enormously.
Always identify which kind you're looking at before dismissing it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Judging global vs. relative by whether a $-1$ or $i$ "looks small."**
The size or simplicity of a phase factor has nothing to do with whether
it's global or relative. Only whether it multiplies *every* amplitude
equally (global) or only *some* of them (relative) determines that.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. $|\psi_1\rangle=\frac{3}{5}|0\rangle+\frac{4}{5}|1\rangle$ and
   $|\psi_2\rangle=-\frac{3}{5}|0\rangle-\frac{4}{5}|1\rangle$. Global
   phase, relative phase, or neither? Justify with a calculation.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Global phase. The two states are physically identical.

   The test is whether one common factor takes every coefficient of
   $|\psi_1\rangle$ to the matching coefficient of $|\psi_2\rangle$. Divide
   them one at a time:

   $$
   \frac{-3/5}{3/5} = -1, \qquad \frac{-4/5}{4/5} = -1.
   $$

   The same factor both times, so $|\psi_2\rangle = -|\psi_1\rangle = e^{i\pi}|\psi_1\rangle$.
   That is a global phase with $\gamma = \pi$, and a global phase is
   unobservable, so no experiment of any kind separates these two.

   Confirm it on the sphere: both states have Bloch vector
   $(0.96,\ 0,\ -0.28)$, the same point, because every Bloch coordinate is an
   expectation value $\langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle$ and the two factors of $e^{i\pi}$
   meet as $e^{-i\pi}e^{i\pi} = 1$.

   The mistake worth naming is treating "there are minus signs here" as
   evidence of a relative phase. Two minus signs on two amplitudes is one
   common factor; one minus sign on one amplitude, question 2, is not. What
   matters is not whether a phase is present but whether it multiplies
   everything.

   </details>

2. $|\psi_1\rangle=\frac{3}{5}|0\rangle+\frac{4}{5}|1\rangle$ and
   $|\psi_3\rangle=\frac{3}{5}|0\rangle-\frac{4}{5}|1\rangle$. Same
   question.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Relative phase. These are genuinely different states.

   The same ratio test now gives two different answers:

   $$
   \frac{3/5}{3/5} = +1, \qquad \frac{-4/5}{4/5} = -1.
   $$

   No single factor works, so this cannot be a global phase. It is a relative
   phase of $\pi$ sitting on the $|1\rangle$ amplitude alone.

   The Bloch vectors show the consequence: $|\psi_1\rangle$ is at
   $(0.96,\ 0,\ -0.28)$ and $|\psi_3\rangle$ is at $(-0.96,\ 0,\ -0.28)$. Same
   latitude, mirrored longitude, two distinct points.

   Here is what makes this the important half of the pair. In the
   computational basis the two are *indistinguishable*: both give
   $P(0) = 0.36$ and $P(1) = 0.64$, because squaring throws the sign away. Ask
   in the X basis instead and they could hardly be further apart:

   $$
   P(+) = \frac{1+2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)}{2}, \qquad
   P(+)_{\psi_1} = \frac{1+0.96}{2} = 0.98, \qquad
   P(+)_{\psi_3} = \frac{1-0.96}{2} = 0.02.
   $$

   A 98/2 split against a 2/98 split, from a sign that one measurement could
   not see at all. That is why "it's just a phase" has to be resisted until you
   have checked which kind it is, and why choosing a basis is choosing what you
   are able to find out.

   </details>

3. Explain why a state with $\alpha=0$ (i.e. proportional to $|1\rangle$)
   can never exhibit a *relative*-phase effect from a phase gate
   $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\delta})$, no matter what $\delta$ that gate
   introduces.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a relative phase is a phase *between* two amplitudes, and this state
   only has one.

   Apply the gate:

   $$
   \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\delta}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0\\\beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\e^{i\delta}\beta\end{pmatrix} = e^{i\delta}\begin{pmatrix}0\\\beta\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   The factor $e^{i\delta}$ was meant to touch only the $|1\rangle$ component,
   but the $|0\rangle$ component is zero, so multiplying it by 1 and
   multiplying it by $e^{i\delta}$ give the same thing: nothing. The phase can
   therefore be pulled out in front of the whole state, which is the definition
   of global. The Bloch point does not move; it was at the south pole before
   and it is at the south pole after.

   Said without algebra: "relative" is a two-place word. It needs a second
   amplitude to be relative *to*. States with one nonzero amplitude, the two
   poles, are precisely the states that cannot supply one, which is the same
   fact the Bloch sphere lesson recorded as "$\varphi$ has no meaning at the
   poles".

   Note the scope of the claim, because it is narrower than it first looks.
   Nothing here says $|1\rangle$ is immune to interesting gates. $H|1\rangle = |-\rangle$
   creates two nonzero amplitudes out of one, and *then* a phase gate has
   something to act on. The claim is only that a diagonal phase gate, acting on
   a state with a single nonzero amplitude, can do nothing observable. Sequence
   a gate that spreads the state first and the same phase becomes physical
   immediately.

   </details>

4. Two experimentalists prepare what they both call "$|+\rangle$," but one
   of them has an extra $\pi$ global phase in their equipment's
   convention they don't know about. Would any experiment they run be
   able to tell their two preparations apart? Why or why not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No experiment on those states alone can tell them apart.

   Every prediction quantum mechanics makes about a single state is a
   probability of the form $P(e) = |\langle e|\psi\rangle|^2$, for whatever
   basis $\{|e\rangle\}$ the measurement uses. Substituting the second lab's
   state:

   $$
   \left|\langle e|\left(e^{i\pi}|\psi\rangle\right)\right|^2 = \left|e^{i\pi}\right|^2\left|\langle e|\psi\rangle\right|^2 = \left|\langle e|\psi\rangle\right|^2,
   $$

   since $|e^{i\pi}| = 1$. The phase is squared away for *every* basis at once,
   not just the computational one, so there is no clever choice of measurement
   that exposes it. The same argument runs through expectation values, so the
   two Bloch vectors coincide as well.

   The qualification that matters in practice: this holds because the phase is
   genuinely global, applied to the entire state the labs are working with.
   It stops holding the moment the two preparations are brought into contact
   with each other. Send both qubits into one interferometer, or use one lab's
   qubit as the control for a gate acting on the other's, and what was a global
   phase on one isolated state becomes a *relative* phase between two branches
   of a single larger state. That is observable, and it is how such convention
   mismatches are actually discovered.

   So the precise answer is not "phases never matter" but "a phase common to
   everything in the experiment never matters". As long as the two labs never
   compare qubits, the extra $\pi$ is bookkeeping. The instant they do, it is
   physics.

   </details>

## Summary

- Global phase, $|\psi\rangle \to e^{i\gamma}|\psi\rangle$, is
  unobservable in *any* measurement basis, proven directly from the Born
  rule, not just asserted.
- Relative phase, changing one amplitude's phase relative to the other,
  moves the state's Bloch point and is measurable (just not always in the
  computational basis).
- This resolves both $Y|0\rangle$ vs. $X|0\rangle$ and $R_z(2\pi)=-I$: both
  are global-phase effects, hence invisible.
