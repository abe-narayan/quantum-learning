## Motivation

The <Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term> ($P(0)=|\alpha|^2$, $P(1)=|\beta|^2$) has been used
informally since Lesson 1. Here is the question it leaves open: what if you
wanted to ask a qubit something other than "are you 0 or 1"? A qubit lives
on a whole sphere of states, and the computational basis is two points on
it. Nothing about the sphere singles those two out.

This lesson generalizes the measurement postulate to any pair of points you
like. The payoff arrives in the X-basis calculation, where the relative
phase $\varphi$, invisible to every measurement you have made so far,
becomes something you can read off a detector.

## Intuition

Measuring is like asking a question with a fixed menu of possible answers.
"Is this 0 or 1?" is one question: the menu is $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$.
But "is this $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$?" is an equally legitimate
question, with a different menu. Which question you ask is a choice you
make (by choosing what to measure); which answer you get, for a fixed
question, is irreducibly random, weighted by how much your prepared state
overlaps with each possible answer.

## Mathematical Development

### The measurement postulate

Choose any **orthonormal basis** $\{|e_0\rangle, |e_1\rangle\}$: any pair
of states with $\langle e_0|e_0\rangle=\langle e_1|e_1\rangle=1$ and
$\langle e_0|e_1\rangle=0$, which is the orthonormality property Dirac
notation gave us. Measuring $|\psi\rangle$ "in this basis" means:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "Pei", symbol: "P(e_i)", gloss: "The probability of getting outcome e_i when you measure." },
    { id: "ei", symbol: "|e_i⟩", gloss: "One of the two basis states of the chosen measurement: an item on the menu of possible answers." },
    { id: "psi", symbol: "|ψ⟩", gloss: "The state being measured, before collapse." },
  ]}
>
$$
P(e_i) = |\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2
$$
</EquationReveal>

and afterward, the state has <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapsed</Term> to exactly $|e_i\rangle$: every
trace of the original superposition is gone. This is the general Born
rule; the computational-basis version from Lesson 1 is the special case
$\{|e_0\rangle,|e_1\rangle\}=\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$:

$$
P(0) = |\langle0|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\alpha|^2, \qquad P(1) = |\langle1|\psi\rangle|^2 = |\beta|^2
$$

using $\langle0|\psi\rangle=\langle0|(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle)=\alpha$
by orthonormality: the amplitude $\alpha$ *is* the inner product with
$|0\rangle$, not just notation that happens to look like one.

### Measuring in the X-basis

Take $\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}$ instead, with
$|\pm\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)$. For a general
$|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$:

$$
\langle+|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0|+\langle1|)(\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle) = \frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}
$$

$$
P(+) = \left|\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\sqrt2}\right|^2 = \frac{|\alpha+\beta|^2}{2}
$$

This behaves differently from the computational-basis case. Expand
$|\alpha+\beta|^2 = |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2+2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta) = 1 + 2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)$,
and $P(+)$ turns out to depend on the *relative phase* between $\alpha$ and
$\beta$ (through $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)$), not only on their
individual sizes. So two states with identical $P(0)$ and $P(1)$ can have
entirely different $P(+)$ and $P(-)$: $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ are both
50/50 in the Z basis, but one gives $P(+)=1$ and the other $P(+)=0$. This
is the first place in the course where interference appears as a number a
detector could report.

### Why one measurement isn't enough

A single measurement returns one classical bit of information: 0 or 1,
$+$ or $-$, whatever the chosen basis's outcomes are called. It does
**not** return $\theta$, $\varphi$, or any amplitude. Learning
$P(0)\approx0.73$, say, to any useful precision requires repeating the
*entire experiment* (prepare the same state, measure, record the
outcome, discard the now-collapsed state, repeat) many times, and
computing the fraction of 0s. This is a hard physical constraint, not a
technological limitation: once you measure, the superposition is gone, so
you cannot "measure again more carefully."

## Physical Interpretation

Choosing a measurement basis is choosing which physical property to
observe (for a real qubit, e.g. photon polarization, this might mean
literally orienting a polarizing filter one way vs. another). The
outcome's randomness is not error or noise to be engineered away. The
postulate puts it in the theory at the ground floor, and nothing in
quantum mechanics predicts which outcome a given run will give.

## Interactive Experiment

The experiment below needs one gate, the **Hadamard gate** $H$, a lesson
ahead of where it is properly introduced. Two facts about it are enough
here, and they are the two the next lesson starts from: $H$ is
linear, so it acts on a superposition term by term, and on the two basis
states it does

$$
H|0\rangle = |+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle), \qquad H|1\rangle = |-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-|1\rangle)
$$

That is everything the prediction needs: apply those two rules to
$|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$, collect terms, and
read off what the Born rule then says about a $Z$-basis measurement of
the result. It is a two-line calculation, not a guess.

## Worked Example

**Compute $P(+)$ and $P(-)$ for $|\psi\rangle = |{+i}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$.**

Here $\alpha=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, $\beta=\frac{i}{\sqrt2}$, so
$\alpha^*\beta = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{i}{\sqrt2} = \frac{i}{2}$, which
is purely imaginary: $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)=0$.

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Substitute Re(α*β)=0 into the P(+) formula derived above.">
$$
P(+) = \frac{1+2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)}{2} = \frac{1+0}{2} = \frac12
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

and by the same logic $P(-)=\frac12$. Even though $|{+i}\rangle$ sits on
the equator like $|+\rangle$, measuring it in the X basis is a coin flip.
The geometry says the same thing: $|{+i}\rangle$ is $90°$ of longitude from
$|+\rangle$, so its Bloch vector is perpendicular to the X axis and has no
preference for either X-basis outcome.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Believing a measurement "reveals" $\theta$ or $\varphi$.** A single
measurement gives one bit (which outcome occurred), full stop. Only
statistics over *many* identical preparations approximate a probability,
and even then you only recover $\theta$-like information from one basis
and $\varphi$-like information from a different basis, never both from
one series of measurements in one basis.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying the computational-basis Born rule to a different basis by
habit.** $P(0)=|\alpha|^2$ is specific to measuring in
$\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$. For any other basis, go back to
$P(e_i)=|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$ and compute the inner product
directly.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Measuring in an orthonormal basis $\{|e_0\rangle,|e_1\rangle\}$ gives
  outcome $e_i$ with probability $|\langle e_i|\psi\rangle|^2$, collapsing
  the state to $|e_i\rangle$.
- The familiar $P(0)=|\alpha|^2$ is the special case of the computational
  basis.
- X-basis probabilities depend on the *relative phase* between $\alpha$
  and $\beta$, a direct, measurable consequence of interference.
- A single measurement yields one classical bit; probabilities require
  many repeated preparations and measurements.
