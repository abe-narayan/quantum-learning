## Motivation

In the last lesson, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ showed up as "complex numbers" and
we mostly left it at that: every example used real values like
$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$. That was deliberately gentle. It's about to stop being
gentle. The Bloch sphere's phase factor $e^{i\varphi}$, the difference
between a $Z$ gate and an $S$ gate, and the reason quantum interference
exists at all all come down to genuinely complex amplitudes, not just real
ones with a sign.

So before going further, we need the actual toolkit: how to add, multiply,
and geometrically picture complex numbers. If you've seen this before,
treat this lesson as a fast, quantum-flavored refresher. The polar form
and Euler's formula sections are the parts that matter most going forward.

## Intuition

A real number lives on a line. A complex number lives on a **plane**: one
axis for its real part, one for its imaginary part. That's really it. A
complex number is just an ordered pair $(a, b)$, written $a + bi$, plotted
as a point (or an arrow from the origin) in this plane.

The "imaginary" unit $i$ is defined by one property: $i^2 = -1$. That's the
entire rulebook. Everything else, every identity in this lesson, follows
from treating $i$ like any other algebraic symbol, except that whenever
$i^2$ shows up, you replace it with $-1$.

## Mathematical Development

### Rectangular form: addition and multiplication

A complex number $z = a + bi$ has **real part** $\operatorname{Re}(z) = a$
and **imaginary part** $\operatorname{Im}(z) = b$ (both $a$ and $b$ are
ordinary real numbers).

Addition is component-wise:

$$
(a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i
$$

Multiplication follows from distributing and applying $i^2 = -1$:

$$
(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bdi^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i
$$

### Conjugate and modulus

The **complex conjugate** of $z = a+bi$ flips the sign of the imaginary
part: $z^* = a - bi$. Multiplying a number by its own conjugate always
produces a non-negative real number:

$$
z z^* = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - abi + abi - b^2i^2 = a^2 + b^2
$$

That quantity, always real, always $\geq 0$, defines the **modulus**
(length) of $z$:

$$
|z| = \sqrt{z z^*} = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}
$$

This is exactly why $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ from the last lesson
are guaranteed to be real, non-negative numbers suitable for probabilities,
no matter how complex $\alpha$ and $\beta$ get: $|\alpha|^2 = \alpha\alpha^*$
by construction.

### Polar form and Euler's formula

Rectangular form $(a, b)$ is one way to locate a point in the plane; polar
coordinates, a distance $r$ and an angle $\theta$ from the positive real
axis, are another. They're related by $a = r\cos\theta$,
$b = r\sin\theta$, so:

$$
z = a + bi = r(\cos\theta + i \sin\theta)
$$

Remarkably, $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ can be written as a single
exponential, $e^{i\theta}$. This is **Euler's formula**, and it's worth
seeing where it comes from rather than just accepting it. The Taylor series
for $e^x$ works for complex inputs too, so substitute $x = i\theta$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = 1 + i\theta + \frac{(i\theta)^2}{2!} + \frac{(i\theta)^3}{3!} + \frac{(i\theta)^4}{4!} + \cdots
$$

Using $i^2=-1, i^3=-i, i^4=1$ and regrouping into real and imaginary terms:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^4}{4!} - \cdots\right)}_{\cos\theta} \; + \; i\underbrace{\left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)}_{\sin\theta}
$$

which is exactly $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. So:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad z = re^{i\theta}
$$

A number written this way has modulus $r$ and **phase** (or **argument**)
$\theta$. Every complex number has both a rectangular form and a polar
form: they're the same information, viewed two ways. It's easier to feel
that equivalence by moving a number between the two forms yourself than
by reading it in equations alone:

### Why polar form matters: multiplication adds phases

Multiply two numbers in polar form, $z_1 = r_1 e^{i\theta_1}$ and
$z_2 = r_2e^{i\theta_2}$, using ordinary exponent rules:

$$
z_1 z_2 = r_1 e^{i\theta_1} \cdot r_2 e^{i\theta_2} = r_1 r_2\, e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}
$$

**Magnitudes multiply; phases add.** This one fact is the entire reason
quantum computing cares about phase at all: applying a phase gate to a
qubit multiplies an amplitude by $e^{i\theta}$ for some $\theta$, and this
identity is exactly what tells you the *new* phase is the old phase plus
$\theta$, with nothing about the probability (which depends on $r$)
changing.

Conjugation is simple in polar form too: $z^* = re^{-i\theta}$, same
modulus, negated phase. You can check this matches
$\cos(-\theta)+i\sin(-\theta) = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$.

## Physical Interpretation

In quantum mechanics, an amplitude's **modulus** will always turn out to
control a probability (through $|\alpha|^2$), while its **phase** carries
information that never shows up in a probability by itself, only in how
one amplitude compares to another. That split, "how much" versus "in what
phase," is precisely $r$ and $\theta$ in $z = re^{i\theta}$. You'll see
this made completely explicit when we reach the Bloch sphere: the
$e^{i\varphi}$ you'll write there is not a decoration, it's literally this
lesson's polar form.

## Worked Example

**Multiply $(3+4i)(1-2i)$ directly, and find its modulus.**

$$
(3+4i)(1-2i) = 3(1) + 3(-2i) + 4i(1) + 4i(-2i) = 3 - 6i + 4i - 8i^2
$$

Since $i^2 = -1$, the last term is $+8$:

$$
= (3+8) + (-6+4)i = 11 - 2i
$$

Modulus: $|11-2i| = \sqrt{11^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt5$.

**Check it against polar form.** $|3+4i| = \sqrt{9+16}=5$ and
$|1-2i|=\sqrt{1+4}=\sqrt5$. Since magnitudes multiply, we should get
$5 \cdot \sqrt5 = 5\sqrt5$, which matches, without needing to expand anything.

**Verify Euler's formula at $\theta = \pi/2$:**

$$
e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) + i\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0 + i(1) = i
$$

So $e^{i\pi/2} = i$ exactly, a fact you'll meet again the moment we define
the $S$ gate.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Writing $i^2 = +1$.** This is the single most common slip, and it
silently breaks every calculation downstream. $i^2 = -1$, always.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing $|z|$ with $z$ itself.** $|z|$ is always a non-negative real
number, a distance. It has thrown away the phase entirely. Two very
different complex numbers can have the same modulus (e.g. $1$ and $i$ both
have modulus $1$), so knowing $|z|$ alone never tells you $z$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming $(a+bi)^* = a + bi$.** Conjugation *only* flips the sign of the
imaginary part, not the real part: $(a+bi)^* = a - bi$, not $-a - bi$ or
$-a+bi$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A complex number $a+bi$ is a point in the plane; $i^2=-1$ is the only new
  rule needed to do arithmetic with it.
- The conjugate $z^*=a-bi$ makes $zz^*=a^2+b^2$ real and non-negative,
  defining the modulus $|z|=\sqrt{zz^*}$.
- Polar form $z=re^{i\theta}=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$ (Euler's formula)
  separates a complex number into a magnitude $r$ and a phase $\theta$.
- Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their magnitudes and **adds**
  their phases, the reason phase gates compose the way they do.
