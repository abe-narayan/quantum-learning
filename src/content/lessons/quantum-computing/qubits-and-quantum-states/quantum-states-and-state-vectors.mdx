## Motivation

Everything so far has treated $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$
almost like a definition handed down from nowhere. It's time to ask a
sharper question: *why* is a qubit's state a vector at all, and what
physical fact is encoded in that choice? The answer is also the reason
superposition is possible in the first place, and it has no classical
counterpart.

## Intuition

A classical bit's state space is the *set* $\{0, 1\}$. Sets don't come
with addition: "0 + 1" isn't a classical bit state, it's meaningless. A
qubit's state space, by contrast, is a **vector space**: $\mathbb{C}^2$,
the set of pairs of complex numbers, which *does* come with addition and
scalar multiplication built in. That structural difference, set versus
vector space, is the entire reason a qubit can be "part $|0\rangle$, part
$|1\rangle$" while a classical bit cannot be "part 0, part 1." Superposition
isn't a strange add-on; it's a direct consequence of which kind of
mathematical object we chose to represent the state.

## Mathematical Development

### The state-space postulate

The first postulate of quantum mechanics, restricted to a single qubit,
says: the physical state of a qubit is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in the
2-dimensional complex vector space $\mathbb{C}^2$, normalized so that
$\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$.

Because $\mathbb{C}^2$ is a vector space, if $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$
are valid states, then *any* linear combination
$\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ is a candidate state too. The vector
space doesn't restrict you to the basis vectors themselves. Normalization
is the only extra constraint: not every vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$
corresponds to a physical state, only the ones on the "unit sphere"
defined by $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$.

### Counting the real degrees of freedom

How much genuine information does a normalized qubit state contain? Start
counting parameters:

- $\alpha = a_1 + ia_2$ and $\beta = b_1+ib_2$ together have **4** real
  numbers ($a_1,a_2,b_1,b_2$).
- Normalization, $a_1^2+a_2^2+b_1^2+b_2^2=1$, is **one** real equation,
  removing one degree of freedom: **3** remain.
- In the next lesson we'll show that multiplying the *entire* state by
  $e^{i\gamma}$ for any real $\gamma$ changes none of its physical
  predictions. That's **one more** real parameter ($\gamma$) that carries
  no physical information: **2** remain.

Two real numbers. That's not a coincidence: it's exactly the count you
need to specify a point on the surface of a sphere (two angles, like
latitude and longitude on Earth). That sphere is the subject of the next
lesson.

### Superposition and interference

Because addition is a real operation in this vector space, you can combine
states and get genuine cancellation or reinforcement, something
classical probability cannot do. Compare two ways of "not knowing" a bit:

**Classical mixture.** A bit that's 0 with probability $p$ and 1 with
probability $1-p$: the probabilities themselves add,
$P(\text{0 or 1}) = p + (1-p) = 1$, and that's the whole story.

**Quantum superposition.** A qubit $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$:
the *amplitudes* add (when combined with other amplitudes later, e.g.
through a gate), and only afterward do you square to get a probability.
Because squaring a sum is not the same as summing squares,
$|\alpha+\beta|^2 \neq |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2$ in general, quantum
combinations can produce probabilities that are *larger* than either part
alone (constructive interference) or *exactly zero* even when both parts
are individually nonzero (destructive interference). This gap between
"add probabilities" and "add amplitudes" is where every advantage quantum
algorithms will eventually exploit comes from.

A concrete case makes the gap impossible to miss: two amplitudes,
$\alpha_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\alpha_2=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, each on
their own would give probability $\frac12$.

Classical probability-addition would predict a combined probability of 1.
The actual quantum answer, squaring the *summed amplitude*
$\alpha_1+\alpha_2=0$ first, is exactly 0: complete destructive
interference. Nothing like this has a classical-probability analogue.

## Physical Interpretation

Treating $\mathbb{C}^2$ as the state space is a physical claim, not just a
notational convenience: it says nature genuinely allows combinations that
a classical description cannot represent, and it predicts real,
measurable interference effects. Every one of those predictions has been
confirmed experimentally. This is not a mathematical formalism chosen
for elegance, it's the formalism that happens to match reality.

## Worked Example

**Is $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\,|1\rangle$ a valid quantum state?**

Check normalization:

$$
\left|\frac12\right|^2 + \left|\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right|^2 = \frac14 + \frac34 = 1 \checkmark
$$

Yes. The coefficients being complex doesn't prevent normalization; only
the *moduli squared* need to sum to 1.

**A partially specified state.** Suppose $|\psi\rangle = \frac{3}{5}|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$
and $\beta$ is known to be a positive real number. Find $\beta$.

$$
\left(\frac35\right)^2 + \beta^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta^2 = 1-\frac{9}{25}=\frac{16}{25} \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta = \frac45
$$

(We took the positive root because we were told $\beta>0$; in general,
normalization alone only pins down $|\beta|$, not $\beta$'s phase.)

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking superposition means "randomly 0 or 1, we just don't know
which."** That describes a classical mixture, which behaves completely
differently: it can never produce interference. A qubit in superposition
has no definite pre-existing value at all, which is a physical claim, not
an admission of ignorance (see Lesson 1's callout on exactly this point).
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting normalization after combining states.** If you add two
already-normalized states, $|\psi\rangle+|\phi\rangle$ is almost never
itself normalized. You generally need to renormalize (divide by the norm
of the sum) before treating it as a physical state.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A qubit's state is a normalized vector in the complex vector space
  $\mathbb{C}^2$, a physical postulate, not just notation.
- Because $\mathbb{C}^2$ has addition built in, superposition is a direct
  consequence of the vector-space structure.
- Counting real parameters: 4 (from $\alpha,\beta$) $-$ 1 (normalization)
  $-$ 1 (global phase, next lesson) $=$ **2** real degrees of freedom.
- Amplitudes add before squaring, which is what makes quantum interference
  possible. Probabilities alone could never do this.
