## Why a vector, and not something else

Everything so far has treated $|\psi\rangle=\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$
almost like a definition handed down from nowhere. It's time to ask a
sharper question: *why* is a qubit's state a vector at all, and what
physical fact is encoded in that choice? The answer is also the reason
superposition is possible in the first place, and it has no classical
counterpart.

## The state space a classical bit does not have

A classical bit's state space is the *set* $\{0, 1\}$. Sets don't come
with addition: "0 + 1" isn't a classical bit state, it's meaningless. A
qubit's state space, by contrast, is a <Term id="vector-space">vector space</Term>: $\mathbb{C}^2$,
the set of pairs of complex numbers, which *does* come with addition and
scalar multiplication built in. That structural difference, set versus
vector space, is the entire reason a qubit can be "part $|0\rangle$, part
$|1\rangle$" while a classical bit cannot be "part 0, part 1." Superposition
isn't a strange add-on; it's a direct consequence of which kind of
mathematical object we chose to represent the state.

## The postulate, and counting what is left

### The state-space postulate

The first postulate of quantum mechanics, restricted to a single qubit,
says: the physical state of a qubit is a vector $|\psi\rangle$ in the
2-dimensional complex vector space $\mathbb{C}^2$, normalized so that
$\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$.

Because $\mathbb{C}^2$ is a vector space, if $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$
are valid states, then *any* linear combination
$\alpha|0\rangle+\beta|1\rangle$ is a candidate state too. The vector
space doesn't restrict you to the basis vectors themselves. Normalization
is the only extra constraint: not every vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$
corresponds to a physical state, only the ones on the "unit sphere"
defined by $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$.

### Counting the real degrees of freedom

How much information does a normalized qubit state contain? Start counting
parameters:

- $\alpha = a_1 + ia_2$ and $\beta = b_1+ib_2$ together have **4** real
  numbers ($a_1,a_2,b_1,b_2$).
- Normalization, $a_1^2+a_2^2+b_1^2+b_2^2=1$, is **one** real equation,
  removing one degree of freedom: **3** remain.
- Multiplying the *entire* state by $e^{i\gamma}$ for any real $\gamma$
  changes none of its physical predictions. The next lesson uses that fact
  to build the sphere, and Global Phase vs. Relative Phase later in this
  course proves it. That's **one more** real parameter ($\gamma$) that
  carries no physical information: **2** remain.

Two real numbers. That's not a coincidence: it's exactly the count you
need to specify a point on the surface of a sphere (two angles, like
latitude and longitude on Earth). That sphere is the subject of the next
lesson.

### Superposition and interference

Because addition is a defined operation in this vector space, you can
combine states and get cancellation or reinforcement, which classical
probability cannot do. Compare two ways of "not knowing" a bit:

**Classical mixture.** A bit that's 0 with probability $p$ and 1 with
probability $1-p$: the probabilities themselves add,
$P(\text{0 or 1}) = p + (1-p) = 1$, and that's the whole story.

**Quantum superposition.** A qubit $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$:
the *amplitudes* add (when combined with other amplitudes later, e.g.
through a gate), and only afterward do you square to get a probability.
Because squaring a sum is not the same as summing squares,
$|\alpha+\beta|^2 \neq |\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2$ in general, quantum
combinations can produce probabilities that are *larger* than either part
alone (constructive interference) or *exactly zero* even when both parts
are individually nonzero (destructive interference). This gap between
"add probabilities" and "add amplitudes" is where every advantage quantum
algorithms will eventually exploit comes from.

A concrete case makes the gap impossible to miss: two amplitudes,
$\alpha_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and $\alpha_2=-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$, each on
their own would give probability $\frac12$.

## What choosing this state space commits you to

Treating $\mathbb{C}^2$ as the state space is a physical claim, not just a
notational convenience. It says nature allows combinations a classical
description cannot represent, and it predicts measurable interference
effects. Every one of those predictions has survived experimental test,
which is the only reason to keep the formalism.

## Testing one candidate state

**Is $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\,|1\rangle$ a valid quantum state?**

Check normalization:

$$
\left|\frac12\right|^2 + \left|\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right|^2 = \frac14 + \frac34 = 1 \checkmark
$$

Yes. The coefficients being complex doesn't prevent normalization; only
the *moduli squared* need to sum to 1.

**A partially specified state.** Suppose $|\psi\rangle = \frac{3}{5}|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$
and $\beta$ is known to be a positive real number. Find $\beta$.

$$
\left(\frac35\right)^2 + \beta^2 = 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta^2 = 1-\frac{9}{25}=\frac{16}{25} \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta = \frac45
$$

(We took the positive root because we were told $\beta>0$; in general,
normalization alone only pins down $|\beta|$, not $\beta$'s phase.)

## Superposition against a classical mixture

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking superposition means "randomly 0 or 1, we just don't know
which."** That describes a classical mixture, which behaves differently in
a way you can test: it can never produce interference. A qubit in
superposition has no definite pre-existing value, which is a physical
claim rather than an admission of ignorance (Lesson 1's callout makes the
same distinction).
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting normalization after combining states.** If you add two
already-normalized states, $|\psi\rangle+|\phi\rangle$ is almost never
itself normalized. You generally need to renormalize (divide by the norm
of the sum) before treating it as a physical state.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Is $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle$ a
   valid quantum state? If not, what would fix it?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No. The squared moduli come to

   $$
   \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 + \left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\right|^2 = \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac23,
   $$

   not 1. The vector is a perfectly good element of $\mathbb{C}^2$, it just
   does not sit on the unit sphere the state-space postulate restricts you to.

   The fix is to divide by its length. The vector's norm is
   $\sqrt{2/3}$, so

   $$
   |\psi'\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2/3}}\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle\right) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}|1\rangle = |+\rangle.
   $$

   Rescaling is always available, and it never changes which *direction* the
   vector points, which is why normalization costs you nothing physical: it
   is the one degree of freedom the counting argument above already discarded.

   The reason $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ looks plausible is that $\tfrac13 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = 1$.
   That is the right constant for three equally likely outcomes. A qubit has
   two, so the right constant is $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$.

   </details>

2. A state is known to satisfy $|\alpha| = |\beta|$. What does this tell you
   about $\alpha$ and $\beta$ individually? Give two different valid states
   satisfying this.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It pins their *sizes* completely and their *phases* not at all. Combine
   $|\alpha| = |\beta|$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$ and you get
   $2|\alpha|^2 = 1$, so

   $$
   |\alpha| = |\beta| = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}.
   $$

   Two different valid states:
   $|+\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + |1\rangle)$ and
   $|-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle - |1\rangle)$. Both satisfy the
   condition; $|{+i}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle + i|1\rangle)$ is a
   third, and there is a whole circle of them.

   The wrong move is reading $|\alpha| = |\beta|$ as $\alpha = \beta$. The
   bars are moduli, and a modulus throws away the sign and the phase, so the
   condition says only "the two arrows are the same length", never "the two
   arrows point the same way". That gap is exactly what separates $|+\rangle$
   from $|-\rangle$: identical measurement statistics in the computational
   basis, genuinely different states, and the next lesson is about how to tell
   them apart.

   </details>

3. Suppose two amplitudes $\alpha_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ and
   $\alpha_2 = -\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ would each individually give probability
   $\frac12$. What is $|\alpha_1+\alpha_2|^2$? What does this illustrate?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Zero.

   $$
   \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} = 0, \qquad |\alpha_1+\alpha_2|^2 = 0.
   $$

   This is total destructive interference, and the point is the contrast with
   what classical probability would have said. Adding the two *probabilities*
   gives $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$: certainty. Adding the two *amplitudes*
   first and squaring afterwards gives 0: impossibility. The same two
   contributions, and the two rules disagree as violently as it is possible to
   disagree.

   The order of operations is the whole content. Amplitudes add, then you
   square. $|\alpha_1+\alpha_2|^2 \neq |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2$ in general,
   because squaring a sum produces a cross term
   $2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha_1^*\alpha_2)$ that has no classical counterpart.
   Here that cross term is $-1$, which cancels the $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12$
   exactly.

   Notice what did *not* matter: both amplitudes have modulus
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$, so on their own they are indistinguishable. Only the
   relative minus sign, invisible to either one alone, produced the
   cancellation.

   </details>

4. Explain in your own words why "a classical bit's state space is a set,
   not a vector space" is the reason classical bits can't be in
   superposition.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because superposition is a linear combination, and a set has no addition
   to build one with.

   The state space of a classical bit is $\{0, 1\}$: two elements, and nothing
   else. To write "part 0, part 1" you would need to form something like
   $\alpha \cdot 0 + \beta \cdot 1$, which requires two operations the set does
   not have, adding two states and scaling a state by a number. The expression
   is not false for a classical bit; it is not even well formed. A qubit's
   state space is $\mathbb{C}^2$, which comes with both operations built in, so
   $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$ is automatically another element of the
   same space. Superposition is not an extra postulate bolted on afterwards.
   It is what you get for free the moment you choose a vector space.

   The objection worth answering, because it is the one most readers reach
   for: a classical bit *can* be described by a probability distribution, "70%
   chance of 0, 30% chance of 1", and that looks like a mixture of the two.
   The difference is which numbers are allowed to combine. A distribution
   combines probabilities, which are non-negative and sum to 1, so nothing can
   ever cancel; the most two contributions can do is share the total. A
   superposition combines amplitudes, which are complex, so two nonzero
   contributions can add to exactly zero, as question 3 just did. That
   cancellation is measurable, it has no representation as a distribution over
   $\{0,1\}$, and it is what the vector-space structure buys.

   </details>

## Summary

- A qubit's state is a normalized vector in the complex vector space
  $\mathbb{C}^2$: a physical postulate, with notation following from it.
- Because $\mathbb{C}^2$ has addition built in, superposition is a direct
  consequence of the vector-space structure.
- Counting real parameters: 4 (from $\alpha,\beta$) $-$ 1 (normalization)
  $-$ 1 (global phase, proved later in this course) $=$ **2** real degrees
  of freedom.
- Amplitudes add before squaring, which is what makes quantum interference
  possible. Probabilities alone could never do this.
