## Motivation

The operations that move a qubit from one Bloch-sphere point to another
are <Term id="quantum-gates">quantum gates</Term>, and every quantum circuit
you'll ever see is a sequence of them. There are only six you need for now,
and one structural rule they all obey. The rule comes first, because it is
what rules out most matrices you could write down.

## Intuition

A classical logic gate (AND, NOT, OR) takes a bit and produces a bit, and
most of them are irreversible: given the output of an AND gate, you can't
recover its two inputs. Quantum gates are different by requirement: they
must be **reversible**, which turns out to mean every quantum gate is a
*rotation* of the <Term id="bloch-sphere-term">Bloch sphere</Term>. Rotations never destroy information.
Rotate one way, then rotate back, and you are where you started.
That single constraint shapes everything about how quantum gates behave.

## Mathematical Development

### Why gates must be unitary

A gate is a matrix $U$ acting on the state vector: $|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle$.
Since $|\psi'\rangle$ must itself be a valid, normalized state, we need
$\langle\psi'|\psi'\rangle=1$ whenever $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$. Expand
the left side using $\langle\psi'| = \langle\psi|U^\dagger$ (the bra
transforms with the conjugate-transpose, written $U^\dagger$):

$$
\langle\psi'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|U^\dagger U|\psi\rangle
$$

For this to equal $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ for *every* state
$|\psi\rangle$, we need $U^\dagger U = I$. A matrix satisfying this is
called **<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>**. Unitary matrices are automatically invertible (with
$U^{-1}=U^\dagger$), which is the reversibility property from the intuition
above: undoing a gate means applying its conjugate transpose.

That test is worth seeing performed rather than asserted. Below, each of
this lesson's six gates is shown next to its conjugate transpose and next
to the product $U^\dagger U$, all three computed from the platform's own
gate definitions. The third panel comes out as the identity every time,
including for $T$, whose entries are not even real:

### The gates

Each gate below acts on a single qubit and has a geometric meaning as a
rotation of the Bloch vector by some angle about some axis.

**$X$: bit flip.**

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X|0\rangle=|1\rangle, \quad X|1\rangle=|0\rangle
$$

Swaps the two basis states, which is what a classical NOT gate does. Rotates the
Bloch vector by $\pi$ about the $x$-axis.

**$Y$.**

$$
Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle, \quad Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle
$$

Rotates by $\pi$ about the $y$-axis. Notice $Y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle$, not
plain $|1\rangle$: an extra phase factor $i$ appears that $X|0\rangle$
didn't have. Geometrically, though, $X|0\rangle$ and $Y|0\rangle$ land on
the same point of the sphere. Hold onto that discrepancy: two states with
different amplitudes and identical physics is the subject of the
global-phase lesson two steps from here.

**$Z$: phase flip.**

$$
Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle, \quad Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle
$$

Leaves $|0\rangle$ untouched and flips the sign of $|1\rangle$'s
coefficient. Rotates by $\pi$ about the $z$-axis, which is why it fixes
the poles (they sit *on* the $z$-axis) while still doing something to
every other point.

**$H$: Hadamard.**

$$
H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad H|0\rangle=|+\rangle, \quad H|1\rangle=|-\rangle
$$

Let's verify $H|0\rangle$ directly, writing out the matrix-vector product
from Dirac Notation in full — row 0 of $H$ against the whole column to
make entry 0, row 1 against the whole column to make entry 1:

$$
H|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1 + 1\cdot0\\1\cdot1 + (-1)\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) = |+\rangle
$$

Every other gate application in this lesson is the same three lines of
arithmetic with different entries, which is why the results below are
stated rather than each worked out.
$H$ is the gate that *creates* <Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> from a definite state, and
since it's its own inverse ($H^2=I$, worth checking), it also *removes*
superposition: $H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$. Geometrically, $H$ rotates by $\pi$
about the diagonal axis halfway between the $x$- and $z$-axes, the only
one of these six gates whose axis isn't a coordinate axis.

**$S$ and $T$: phase gates.**

$$
S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Both leave $|0\rangle$ alone and multiply $|1\rangle$'s coefficient by a
phase: $i=e^{i\pi/2}$ for $S$, $e^{i\pi/4}$ for $T$. In the canonical
Bloch form, multiplying $\beta$ by $e^{i\delta}$ adds
$\delta$ to $\varphi$: $S$ rotates by $\pi/2$ about the $z$-axis (a
quarter turn), $T$ by $\pi/4$ (an eighth turn). Unlike $X,Y,Z,H$, neither
is its own inverse: $S^2=Z$ (two quarter-turns make a half-turn) and
$S^4=I$.

## Physical Interpretation

Every gate here rotates the Bloch vector by a fixed angle about a fixed
axis. That's not a coincidence of these six examples. It's a general
fact about single-qubit gates that the next lesson proves directly. Once
you see it that way, you don't need to memorize six unrelated matrices;
you need one geometric picture ("rotate the sphere") and six axis/angle
pairs.

## Worked Example

**Compute $Y|0\rangle$ by matrix multiplication, and confirm it lands on
the same Bloch point as $X|0\rangle$.**

$$
Y|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\i\end{pmatrix} = i|1\rangle
$$

Bloch coordinates use $\alpha=0,\beta=i$:
$x=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)=0$, $y=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)=0$,
$z=|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2 = 0-1=-1$. That's $(0,0,-1)$, the south pole,
where $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$ also lands. Same point, different
amplitudes: $i|1\rangle$ vs. plain $|1\rangle$.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every gate is its own inverse.** $X,Y,Z,H$ are (applying any
of them twice gives back the identity), but $S$ and $T$ are not: $S$
needs to be applied four times, $T$ needs eight, to return to identity.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Reading matrix-vector multiplication in the wrong order.** $U|\psi\rangle$
means "apply $U$'s rows to $|\psi\rangle$'s column," the standard
matrix-vector product. If your arithmetic keeps producing the wrong
answer, write out $|\psi\rangle$ as an explicit column vector first.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Gates must be unitary ($U^\dagger U = I$) so that valid states map to
  valid states. This is what makes them reversible.
- $X,Y,Z$ each rotate by $\pi$ about their respective axis; $H$ rotates by
  $\pi$ about the diagonal between $x$ and $z$; $S$ and $T$ rotate by
  $\pi/2$ and $\pi/4$ about $z$.
- $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$ lands on the same Bloch point as $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$,
  same rotation, different overall phase.
