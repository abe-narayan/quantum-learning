## Six matrices, and one requirement

The operations that move a qubit from one Bloch-sphere point to another
are <Term id="quantum-gates">quantum gates</Term>, and every quantum circuit
you'll ever see is a sequence of them. There are only six you need for now,
and one structural rule they all obey. The rule comes first, because it is
what rules out most matrices you could write down.

## Classical gates throw information away

A classical logic gate (AND, NOT, OR) takes a bit and produces a bit, and
most of them are irreversible: given the output of an AND gate, you can't
recover its two inputs. Quantum gates are different by requirement: they
must be **reversible**, which turns out to mean every quantum gate is a
*rotation* of the <Term id="bloch-sphere-term">Bloch sphere</Term>. Rotations never destroy information.
Rotate one way, then rotate back, and you are where you started.
That single constraint shapes everything about how quantum gates behave.

## Why unitary, then the six of them

### Why gates must be unitary

A gate is a matrix $U$ acting on the state vector: $|\psi'\rangle=U|\psi\rangle$.
Since $|\psi'\rangle$ must itself be a valid, normalized state, we need
$\langle\psi'|\psi'\rangle=1$ whenever $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$. Getting
the bra of $|\psi'\rangle$ needs one rule about conjugate transposes
beyond the vector case from Dirac Notation: taking the conjugate
transpose of a product *reverses the order* of its factors, the same way
undoing "socks then shoes" means removing shoes first. Writing $U^\dagger$
for the conjugate transpose of the matrix $U$, that rule turns
$|\psi'\rangle = U|\psi\rangle$ into $\langle\psi'| = \langle\psi|U^\dagger$,
with the bra now sitting to the *left* of the dagger. Expand the left
side with it:

$$
\langle\psi'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|U^\dagger U|\psi\rangle
$$

For this to equal $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ for *every* state
$|\psi\rangle$, we need $U^\dagger U = I$. A matrix satisfying this is
called **<Term id="unitary-operator">unitary</Term>**. Unitary matrices are automatically invertible (with
$U^{-1}=U^\dagger$), which is the reversibility property from the intuition
above: undoing a gate means applying its conjugate transpose.

That test is worth seeing performed rather than asserted. Below, each of
this lesson's six gates is shown next to its conjugate transpose and next
to the product $U^\dagger U$, all three computed from the platform's own
gate definitions. The third panel comes out as the identity every time,
including for $T$, whose entries are not even real:

### The gates

Each gate below acts on a single qubit and has a geometric meaning as a
rotation of the Bloch vector by some angle about some axis.

**$X$: bit flip.**

$$
X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad X|0\rangle=|1\rangle, \quad X|1\rangle=|0\rangle
$$

Swaps the two basis states, which is what a classical NOT gate does. Rotates the
Bloch vector by $\pi$ about the $x$-axis.

**$Y$.**

$$
Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle, \quad Y|1\rangle=-i|0\rangle
$$

Rotates by $\pi$ about the $y$-axis. Notice $Y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle$, not
plain $|1\rangle$: an extra phase factor $i$ appears that $X|0\rangle$
didn't have. Geometrically, though, $X|0\rangle$ and $Y|0\rangle$ land on
the same point of the sphere. Hold onto that discrepancy: two states with
different amplitudes and identical physics is the subject of the
global-phase lesson two steps from here.

**$Z$: phase flip.**

$$
Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad Z|0\rangle=|0\rangle, \quad Z|1\rangle=-|1\rangle
$$

Leaves $|0\rangle$ untouched and flips the sign of $|1\rangle$'s
coefficient. Rotates by $\pi$ about the $z$-axis, which is why it fixes
the poles (they sit *on* the $z$-axis) while still doing something to
every other point.

**$H$: Hadamard.**

$$
H = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad H|0\rangle=|+\rangle, \quad H|1\rangle=|-\rangle
$$

Let's verify $H|0\rangle$ directly, writing out the matrix-vector product
from Dirac Notation in full: row 0 of $H$ against the whole column to
make entry 0, row 1 against the whole column to make entry 1.

$$
H|0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1 + 1\cdot0\\1\cdot1 + (-1)\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle) = |+\rangle
$$

Every other gate application in this lesson is the same three lines of
arithmetic with different entries, which is why the results below are
stated rather than each worked out.
$H$ is the gate that *creates* <Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> from a definite state, and
since it's its own inverse ($H^2=I$, worth checking), it also *removes*
superposition: $H|+\rangle=|0\rangle$. Geometrically, $H$ rotates by $\pi$
about the diagonal axis halfway between the $x$- and $z$-axes, the only
one of these six gates whose axis isn't a coordinate axis.

**$S$ and $T$: phase gates.**

$$
S = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}, \qquad T = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\pi/4}\end{pmatrix}
$$

Both leave $|0\rangle$ alone and multiply $|1\rangle$'s coefficient by a
phase: $i=e^{i\pi/2}$ for $S$, $e^{i\pi/4}$ for $T$. In the canonical
Bloch form, multiplying $\beta$ by $e^{i\delta}$ adds
$\delta$ to $\varphi$: $S$ rotates by $\pi/2$ about the $z$-axis (a
quarter turn), $T$ by $\pi/4$ (an eighth turn). Unlike $X,Y,Z,H$, neither
is its own inverse: $S^2=Z$ (two quarter-turns make a half-turn) and
$S^4=I$.

## Every one of them is a rotation

Every gate here rotates the Bloch vector by a fixed angle about a fixed
axis. That's not a coincidence of these six examples. It's a general
fact about single-qubit gates that the next lesson proves directly. Once
you see it that way, you don't need to memorize six unrelated matrices;
you need one geometric picture ("rotate the sphere") and six axis/angle
pairs.

## Y on the north pole, and where it lands

**Compute $Y|0\rangle$ by matrix multiplication, and confirm it lands on
the same Bloch point as $X|0\rangle$.**

$$
Y|0\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0\\i\end{pmatrix} = i|1\rangle
$$

Bloch coordinates use $\alpha=0,\beta=i$:
$x=2\operatorname{Re}(\alpha^*\beta)=0$, $y=2\operatorname{Im}(\alpha^*\beta)=0$,
$z=|\alpha|^2-|\beta|^2 = 0-1=-1$. That's $(0,0,-1)$, the south pole,
where $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$ also lands. Same point, different
amplitudes: $i|1\rangle$ vs. plain $|1\rangle$.

## Not every gate is its own inverse

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming every gate is its own inverse.** $X,Y,Z,H$ are (applying any
of them twice gives back the identity), but $S$ and $T$ are not: $S$
needs to be applied four times, $T$ needs eight, to return to identity.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Reading matrix-vector multiplication in the wrong order.** $U|\psi\rangle$
means "apply $U$'s rows to $|\psi\rangle$'s column," the standard
matrix-vector product. If your arithmetic keeps producing the wrong
answer, write out $|\psi\rangle$ as an explicit column vector first.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute $X|+\rangle$ by matrix multiplication. Does the result match
   what you'd expect geometrically (recall $|+\rangle$ sits on the
   $x$-axis)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   X|+\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = |+\rangle.
   $$

   $X$ swaps the two amplitudes, and $|+\rangle$'s two amplitudes are already
   equal, so the swap changes nothing at all. $|+\rangle$ is a fixed point
   of $X$.

   That is exactly what the geometry predicts. $X$ is a $180°$ rotation *about
   the $x$-axis*, and every rotation leaves the points on its own axis alone,
   the way spinning a globe moves every city except the two poles.
   $|+\rangle$ sits at the $+x$ pole, so $X$ cannot move it. ($|-\rangle$, at
   the $-x$ pole, is fixed too: $X|-\rangle = |-\rangle$ is worth checking as
   a second case.)

   The reflex that misfires here is "$X$ is the quantum NOT gate, so it must
   flip something". It flips $|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$, and that
   description is complete only on the computational basis. On a general state
   $X$ is a rotation, and a rotation's effect depends entirely on where the
   state already is.

   </details>

2. Compute $Z|+\rangle$. What state is this in terms of
   $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$, and where on the Bloch sphere did it move to?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   Z|+\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} = |-\rangle.
   $$

   On the sphere the state moved from $(1,0,0)$ to $(-1,0,0)$: straight across
   the equator to the antipodal point, half a turn about the $z$-axis, which is
   what $Z$ is.

   The pairing with question 1 is the point of this pair. $X$ left $|+\rangle$
   alone because $|+\rangle$ lies on $X$'s rotation axis. $Z$ moves it as far
   as anything on the sphere can move, because $|+\rangle$ lies as far from
   $Z$'s axis as possible, out on the equator. Same state, two gates, opposite
   extremes, and the deciding factor is geometric rather than algebraic.

   Notice also what a measurement would and would not see. $P(0) = P(1) = \tfrac12$
   both before and after, so a computational-basis measurement is completely
   blind to what just happened. The change is entirely in the relative phase,
   and the way to see it is to measure in the X basis, where $P(+)$ goes from 1
   to 0.

   </details>

3. Verify $S^2=Z$ by multiplying $S$'s matrix by itself.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   S^2 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i^2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = Z \checkmark
   $$

   Diagonal matrices multiply entrywise, so the whole computation is
   $i \times i = -1$.

   The rotation picture says the same thing with no arithmetic. $S$ is a
   quarter turn ($\pi/2$) about the $z$-axis and $Z$ is a half turn ($\pi$)
   about the same axis, so doing $S$ twice must be $Z$: rotations about a
   shared axis simply add their angles. By the same argument $T^2 = S$
   ($\pi/4$ twice is $\pi/2$) and $Z^2 = I$, and you can build the whole
   $\{T, S, Z\}$ family as powers of $T$ without multiplying a single matrix.

   The slip to watch for is writing $i^2$ as $+1$ by treating the square as
   "size squared". Squaring the matrix entry means multiplying the complex
   number by itself, not by its conjugate. $i \cdot i = -1$; it is
   $i \cdot i^* = i \cdot (-i) = +1$ that gives the positive answer, and that
   product appears when you compute probabilities, not when you compose gates.

   </details>

4. Which gate(s) from this lesson leave $|0\rangle$ unchanged?
   Explain geometrically why that has to be true for those gates
   specifically.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $Z$, $S$ and $T$.

   All three are diagonal with a 1 in the top-left corner:
   $Z = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$, $S = \mathrm{diag}(1,i)$,
   $T = \mathrm{diag}(1,e^{i\pi/4})$. Applied to $|0\rangle = (1,0)^\mathsf{T}$,
   the lower entry is multiplied by zero, so only the 1 survives and the state
   comes back exactly, not merely up to a phase.

   Geometrically all three are rotations about the $z$-axis, and $|0\rangle$ is
   the north pole, sitting *on* that axis. A rotation fixes its own axis, so it
   cannot move a state at either pole. The same argument immediately gives the
   companion fact: these three also leave $|1\rangle$ where it is, at the south
   pole, though there they multiply it by a phase (for instance
   $Z|1\rangle = -|1\rangle$), which is invisible because with only one nonzero
   amplitude that phase is global.

   The other three fail, each for a visible reason. $X$ and $Y$ are half turns
   about the $x$- and $y$-axes, both perpendicular to $z$, so they carry the
   north pole all the way to the south pole: $X|0\rangle = |1\rangle$ and
   $Y|0\rangle = i|1\rangle$. $H$ rotates about the diagonal axis halfway
   between $x$ and $z$ and sends $|0\rangle$ to $|+\rangle$, out on the equator.

   </details>

5. Predict, without a calculator, what $T^8|1\rangle$ equals. Justify it
   from the rotation-angle picture, not matrix multiplication.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $T^8|1\rangle = |1\rangle$, exactly, with no leftover phase.

   $T$ is a $\pi/4$ rotation about $z$, so eight of them make
   $8 \times \pi/4 = 2\pi$: one complete turn. A full turn of the Bloch sphere
   returns every point to itself, so the state must come back.

   The reason "exactly" is worth saying is that a full turn does *not*
   guarantee this in general. $R_z(2\pi) = -I$, a full turn that returns every
   Bloch point but leaves a global phase of $-1$ behind, and that sign is real
   enough to matter inside a controlled operation. What makes $T$ different is
   the convention its matrix is written in: $T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})$
   puts the entire phase on $|1\rangle$ and none on $|0\rangle$, so

   $$
   T^8 = \mathrm{diag}\!\left(1, \left(e^{i\pi/4}\right)^8\right) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{2\pi i}) = I,
   $$

   the identity on the nose. $R_z$ splits the same rotation symmetrically as
   $\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta/2}, e^{i\theta/2})$, and it is that split, not the
   rotation, that produces the sign. Same physical operation, different choice
   of global phase.

   The trap is stopping at "eight eighth-turns is a full turn, so it is the
   identity up to phase" and calling that the answer. Up to phase is the easy
   half; the question asks what $T^8|1\rangle$ *equals*.

   </details>

## Summary

- Gates must be unitary ($U^\dagger U = I$) so that valid states map to
  valid states. This is what makes them reversible.
- $X,Y,Z$ each rotate by $\pi$ about their respective axis; $H$ rotates by
  $\pi$ about the diagonal between $x$ and $z$; $S$ and $T$ rotate by
  $\pi/2$ and $\pi/4$ about $z$.
- $Y|0\rangle=i|1\rangle$ lands on the same Bloch point as $X|0\rangle=|1\rangle$,
  same rotation, different overall phase.
