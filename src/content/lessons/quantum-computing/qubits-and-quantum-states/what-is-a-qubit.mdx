## Motivation

A classical bit is always either 0 or 1. Every computer you've ever used — your
laptop, your phone — is built entirely out of bits in one of these two states.
It's a simple, robust idea, and it's wrong for describing how nature actually
behaves at the scale of electrons and photons.

When physicists tried to describe systems like a single electron's spin or a
photon's polarization, they found something a classical bit can't capture:
the system can be in a combination of "0" and "1" at once, in a precise
mathematical sense — not just "we don't know which one it is yet." A qubit is
the unit of information built to describe this.

## Intuition

A useful (if imperfect) way to start: imagine a coin that hasn't landed yet.
While it's spinning in the air, it isn't heads, and it isn't tails — it's
genuinely in a mixture of both possibilities. A qubit's superposition is
similar in spirit, but stronger: it's not a coin whose outcome is merely
*unknown*, it's a system whose state is a real, mathematical combination of
$|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, and that combination has consequences you can
measure — like interference between the two possibilities — that a "maybe
heads, maybe tails" coin could never produce.

<Callout type="note">
Superposition is not classical uncertainty. A classical bit you haven't
looked at yet is still secretly 0 or 1. A qubit in superposition has no
definite value until it's measured — that's a physical fact, not a gap in
your knowledge.
</Callout>

## Mathematical Development

We write a qubit's state using **Dirac notation**. The two basis states are
written $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, corresponding to the classical bit
values 0 and 1. A general qubit state $|\psi\rangle$ is a linear combination
of these two:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle
$$

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers called **amplitudes**. For
this to describe a physically valid state, the amplitudes must satisfy the
normalization condition:

$$
|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
$$

This condition exists because $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ will turn out to
be *probabilities* — and probabilities have to add up to 1.

## Physical Interpretation

The connection between the math and what you can actually observe is the
**Born rule**: if you measure a qubit in state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$,
you get outcome 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and outcome 1 with
probability $|\beta|^2$. Measurement is the one place where the smooth,
continuous evolution of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ gives way to a single, random,
classical outcome — the superposition **collapses**.

This is why normalization matters physically, not just mathematically: it's
exactly the statement that "probability of 0" plus "probability of 1" must
equal 1.

## Worked Example

Consider the state:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle
$$

**Check normalization:**

$$
\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 + \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1 \checkmark
$$

**Measurement probabilities:**

$$
P(0) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(1) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}
$$

So this qubit, when measured, gives 0 or 1 with equal 50% probability — an
**equal superposition**. This is exactly the state a Hadamard gate produces
from $|0\rangle$, which you'll meet in the next course.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting to square the amplitude.** The probability of an outcome is
$|\alpha|^2$, not $\alpha$ itself. For a real amplitude like $-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
the probability is still a positive $\tfrac12$ — the sign disappears once you
square it, but it isn't irrelevant: it affects how this state interferes with
others later on.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating superposition as "50/50 either way."** The coefficients don't have
to be equal. $|\psi\rangle = 0.6|0\rangle + 0.8i|1\rangle$ is a valid,
normalized qubit state ($0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 1$) with a 36% chance of measuring 0
and a 64% chance of measuring 1.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A classical bit is always 0 or 1; a qubit's state is a complex linear
  combination $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$.
- Physical qubit states must be normalized: $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.
- The Born rule connects amplitudes to measurement probabilities:
  $P(0) = |\alpha|^2$, $P(1) = |\beta|^2$.
- Measurement collapses the superposition to a single classical outcome.
- Every qubit state corresponds to a point on the Bloch sphere.
