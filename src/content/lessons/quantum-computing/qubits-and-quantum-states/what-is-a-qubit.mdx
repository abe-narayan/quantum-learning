<LessonHook eyebrow="Not a gap in what we know">
A qubit does not keep a definite value in reserve, waiting for you to look. It can be in a combination of "0" and "1" at once. That is a claim about the system itself, not a polite way of saying we don't yet know which one it is.
</LessonHook>

## Two spots, and nothing between them

Right now, every bit in your phone or laptop has a definite value. Nobody
needs to check it for that to be true: it's 0, or it's 1, sitting there
waiting, whether or not anything ever reads it. That assumption is so basic
to how we think about information that it's easy to forget it's an
assumption at all.

Shrink the question down to a single atom and the assumption stops working.
In 1922, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach fired a beam of silver atoms through
a magnet whose field was stronger at the top than at the bottom. Each atom
carries a tiny magnetic compass needle, and classical physics is unambiguous
about what should happen: the needles point in all directions, so the beam
should be deflected by every amount between "fully up" and "fully down" and
smear into a continuous band on the far screen.

<InteractiveSection title="What the beam does on the screen" description="Toggle between the smear classical physics predicts and the two spots Stern and Gerlach actually recorded. Nothing lands in between.">
  <SternGerlachScreen ariaLabel="Schematic of the Stern-Gerlach apparatus: an atom beam through an inhomogeneous magnet onto a screen, toggling between a continuous classical smear across the whole screen and the two discrete spots that were observed." />
</InteractiveSection>

They got two spots. Not a band, not a bright middle with faint edges: two,
with nothing between them. Measure that atom's compass needle along any axis
you like and you get one of two answers, and only two.

Two spots on their own still admit an easy explanation: perhaps each atom
was simply born carrying a hidden "up" or "down" label, and the magnet
only reads it off. Ruling that out takes one more magnet.

Take the atoms from the upper spot, so every one of them has measured
"up" along the vertical axis. Send that filtered beam through a second
magnet turned on its side, measuring left-versus-right instead. It splits
evenly, half left and half right, which is unsurprising: a vertical label
says nothing about horizontal. Now keep only the "left" half and send it
through a *third* magnet, vertical again. Every atom in this beam has been
confirmed "up" once already, and nothing since has touched the vertical
axis.

Half up, half down. The vertical answer you carefully sorted for is gone.
No fixed label the atom was carrying all along can survive that, because a
label wouldn't change when you asked an unrelated question.

So a two-outcome system with no fixed answer in storage needs a unit of
information a bit cannot supply. That unit is the **qubit**.

Qubits are not only silver atoms. Today they are superconducting loops
etched onto chips, single ions held in electromagnetic traps, and
polarized photons in optical fibre, among others (the
[Physical Qubit Platforms](/courses/physical-qubit-platforms) course covers
each in turn). The mathematics below describes all of them at once, which
is why it is worth learning before any particular hardware.

## The coin analogy, and where it breaks

A useful (if imperfect) way to start: imagine a coin that hasn't landed yet.
While it's spinning in the air, it isn't heads and it isn't tails; it's in a
mixture of both possibilities. A qubit's <Term id="superposition">superposition</Term> is similar in spirit,
but stronger, and the difference is the one the sideways magnet exposed. The
spinning coin's outcome is only *unknown*. Once it lands, it will turn out to
have been heads or tails all along, and a slow-motion camera could have told
you which. The silver atom has no such answer to be photographed. Its state
is a mathematical combination of the two outcomes, written $|0\rangle$ and
$|1\rangle$ (the notation is set up properly in the next section; for now
read them as labels for "up" and "down"), and that combination has
measurable consequences of its own, like <Term id="phase-interference">interference</Term>
between the two possibilities, which a "maybe heads, maybe tails" coin
could never produce.

<Callout type="note">
Superposition is not classical uncertainty. A classical bit you haven't
looked at yet is still secretly 0 or 1. A qubit in superposition has no
definite value until it's measured. That's a physical fact, not a gap in
your knowledge.
</Callout>

## Two amplitudes, and one condition on them

We write a qubit's state using <Term id="dirac-notation">Dirac notation</Term>. The two basis states are
written $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$, corresponding to the classical bit
values 0 and 1. A general qubit state $|\psi\rangle$ is a linear combination
of these two:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "psi", symbol: "|ψ⟩", gloss: "The qubit's state: everything there is to know about it." },
    { id: "alpha", symbol: "α", gloss: "The amplitude on |0⟩. A complex number, and not a probability itself." },
    { id: "zero", symbol: "|0⟩", gloss: "The basis state corresponding to the classical bit value 0." },
    { id: "beta", symbol: "β", gloss: "The amplitude on |1⟩. A complex number, and not a probability itself." },
    { id: "one", symbol: "|1⟩", gloss: "The basis state corresponding to the classical bit value 1." },
  ]}
>
$$
|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle
$$
</EquationReveal>

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are complex numbers called <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term>. For
this to describe a physically valid state, the amplitudes must satisfy the
<Term id="normalization">normalization</Term> condition:

$$
|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1
$$

This condition exists because $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ will turn out to
be *probabilities*, and probabilities have to add up to 1.

<Callout type="note">
If complex numbers are new to you, keep going: every calculation in this
lesson works if you treat $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as ordinary numbers, and the
bars in $|\alpha|^2$ as "square the size, ignore the sign." The next lesson
builds the complex case from scratch; nothing here depends on it yet.
</Callout>

## The Born rule, and the one place randomness enters

The connection between the math and what you can observe is the
<Term id="born-rule">Born rule</Term>: if you measure a qubit in state $|\psi\rangle = \alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$,
you get outcome 0 with probability $|\alpha|^2$ and outcome 1 with
probability $|\beta|^2$. Measurement is the one place where the smooth,
continuous evolution of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ gives way to a single, random,
classical outcome: the superposition <Term id="wavefunction-collapse">collapses</Term>.

That is why normalization is a physical requirement rather than a
bookkeeping convention. It is the statement that "probability of 0" plus
"probability of 1" must come to 1. The Stern-Gerlach screen makes the same
point: every atom lands somewhere, so the two spot intensities have to
account for the whole beam.

## Every state as a point on a sphere

Every qubit state can be pictured as a point on the surface of a sphere, the
<Term id="bloch-sphere-term">Bloch sphere</Term>, where the north pole is $|0\rangle$, the south pole is
$|1\rangle$, and every other point is some superposition of the two. The
closer a point sits to a pole, the larger that outcome's probability; the
equator, exactly halfway between the poles, is where the two are equally
likely.

## The equal superposition, checked twice

Consider the state:

$$
|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|0\rangle + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}|1\rangle
$$

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Both amplitudes are 1/√2, so squaring each and adding must give 1 for this to be a valid state.">
$$
\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 + \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1 \checkmark
$$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The Born rule turns each amplitude into a measurement probability by squaring its size.">
$$
P(0) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad P(1) = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{2}
$$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

So this qubit, when measured, gives 0 or 1 with equal 50% probability: an
**equal superposition**. It is the state a <Term id="hadamard-gate">Hadamard gate</Term>
produces from $|0\rangle$, and it is, up to the sign on one amplitude, the state the
Stern-Gerlach atoms were in after the sideways magnet, when the vertical
measurement came back half up and half down.

## Squaring, and the split that need not be even

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting to square the amplitude.** The probability of an outcome is
$|\alpha|^2$, not $\alpha$ itself. For a real amplitude like $-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$,
the probability is still a positive $\tfrac12$. The sign disappears once you
square it, but it isn't irrelevant: it affects how this state interferes with
others later on.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating superposition as "50/50 either way."** The coefficients don't have
to be equal. $|\psi\rangle = 0.6|0\rangle + 0.8i|1\rangle$ is a valid,
normalized qubit state ($0.6^2 + 0.8^2 = 1$) with a 36% chance of measuring 0
and a 64% chance of measuring 1. (The $i$ in $0.8i$ is the imaginary unit,
$i = \sqrt{-1}$, coming in the next lesson. For the probabilities, only the
amplitude's size matters, and $|0.8i| = 0.8$.)
</Callout>

The two cases side by side, with every bar computed by squaring the
amplitudes exactly as the Born rule says:

<BarChart
  bars={lopsidedBars}
  maxValue={1}
  ariaLabel="Four bars. The equal superposition gives 50 percent for outcome 0 and 50 percent for outcome 1. The state 0.6|0⟩ + 0.8i|1⟩ gives 36 percent for outcome 0 and 64 percent for outcome 1, a lopsided split rather than an even one."
/>

Both states are superpositions. Only one of them is a fair coin. A qubit
in superposition is described by two numbers you get to choose, and the
even split is one point in that range rather than the meaning of the word.

## Practice Questions

1. Is $|\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{2}|0\rangle + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}|1\rangle$ a
   valid qubit state? Show your work.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes. Normalization asks for $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$, so square each
   amplitude's size and add:

   $$
   \left|\tfrac{1}{2}\right|^2 + \left|\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right|^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{3}{4} = 1 \checkmark
   $$

   It is valid, and the Born rule then reads off $P(0) = \tfrac14 = 25\%$ and
   $P(1) = \tfrac34 = 75\%$. A lopsided superposition, not a fair coin.

   The usual way to get "no" here is to add the amplitudes instead of their
   squares: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 1.37$, which is not 1, so the
   state looks broken. Nothing in quantum mechanics ever asks the amplitudes
   themselves to sum to 1. They are not probabilities. Squaring is the step
   that turns them into probabilities, and it is the squares that must sum
   to 1.

   </details>

2. A qubit has a 25% probability of measuring 0. Give one possible pair of
   amplitudes $(\alpha, \beta)$ consistent with this.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $(\alpha, \beta) = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$ works, which
   is exactly the state from question 1.

   Run it backwards from the Born rule. $P(0) = |\alpha|^2 = 0.25$ forces
   $|\alpha| = 0.5$. Normalization then fixes the other one:
   $|\beta|^2 = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75$, so $|\beta| = \sqrt{0.75} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} \approx 0.866$.

   Note "one possible pair". The Born rule pins down the *sizes* of the two
   amplitudes and says nothing about their signs or their complex phases, so
   $\left(-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$ and
   $\left(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right)$ are equally correct answers to
   this question. They are genuinely different states, they just happen to be
   indistinguishable by this one measurement. Two lessons from now that
   leftover freedom turns out to be the whole subject.

   The common wrong answer is $\alpha = 0.25$: reading "25%" straight into the
   amplitude slot without squaring. That state has $P(0) = 0.25^2 = 6.25\%$
   instead.

   </details>

3. Why can't a qubit's amplitudes both be negative real numbers close to $-1$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because normalization would fail badly. With $\alpha \approx -1$ and
   $\beta \approx -1$,

   $$
   |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 \approx 1 + 1 = 2,
   $$

   which would say the two outcomes together happen 200% of the time. There is
   no such state.

   The trap in this question is the word "negative", and it is worth being
   clear that the sign is not what breaks it.
   $\alpha = \beta = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$ is a perfectly good qubit state: both
   amplitudes are negative, both squares are $\tfrac12$, and they sum to 1. It
   is the *size* that is illegal, not the sign. Squaring throws the sign away,
   so an amplitude near $-1$ is exactly as oversized as one near $+1$.

   Geometrically: normalization says the vector $(\alpha, \beta)$ has length 1.
   Both components close to $-1$ puts it at length $\approx\sqrt2$, off the unit
   circle. Which quadrant it points into was never the constraint.

   </details>

## Summary

- A classical bit is always 0 or 1; a qubit's state is a complex linear
  combination $\alpha|0\rangle + \beta|1\rangle$.
- Physical qubit states must be normalized: $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.
- The Born rule connects amplitudes to measurement probabilities:
  $P(0) = |\alpha|^2$, $P(1) = |\beta|^2$.
- Measurement collapses the superposition to a single classical outcome.
- Every qubit state corresponds to a point on the Bloch sphere.
