## Routing When the Couplers Differ

Quantum Compilation & Transpilation established the mechanics of
SWAP-network routing on an idealized linear chain where every coupler
was equally reliable, and the previous lesson, Clifford+T Synthesis and
Resource Counting, established T-count as the currency a fault-tolerant
compiler actually spends. Real devices force two more honest ingredients
into the picture at once: a circuit with several two-qubit interactions
rarely maps cleanly onto a small device's connectivity graph, so SWAP
overhead compounds across many gates, not just one; and error rates
vary qubit to qubit and coupler to coupler in the first place.
This lesson works both pieces through a single concrete example, then
combines them into one estimated circuit success probability.

## Counting the SWAP Overhead

### A concrete routing problem

Take a small circuit needing two-qubit interactions between four logical
qubits $q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3$, specifically these five pairs:

$$
(q_0,q_1),\ (q_1,q_2),\ (q_2,q_3),\ (q_0,q_2),\ (q_1,q_3).
$$

Compile it for a 4-qubit linear-chain device, physical qubits
$P_0\!-\!P_1\!-\!P_2\!-\!P_3$, where only adjacent physical qubits share
a coupler (Physical Qubit Platforms' nearest-neighbor connectivity
graph, the same constraint Quantum Compilation & Transpilation
introduced for a single pair). Start with the simplest valid mapping,
the **identity mapping** $q_i \mapsto P_i$.

Under this mapping, three of the five required interactions are already
between physically adjacent qubits and need no SWAPs at all:
$(q_0,q_1)\to(P_0,P_1)$, $(q_1,q_2)\to(P_1,P_2)$, and
$(q_2,q_3)\to(P_2,P_3)$. The remaining two are not adjacent:
$(q_0,q_2)$ sits at chain distance $d=2$ (one qubit, $P_1$, in between),
and so does $(q_1,q_3)$ (with $P_2$ in between).

### Adding up the inserted SWAPs

Reusing Quantum Compilation & Transpilation's own result for a linear
chain, bridging a gap of distance $d$ costs $\text{swapOverheadForDistance}(d) = 2(d-1)$
SWAP gates: walk one qubit's data into adjacency, apply the two-qubit
gate, walk it back. For $(q_0,q_2)$: SWAP $P_0\leftrightarrow P_1$
(carrying $q_0$'s data to sit next to $q_2$ at $P_2$), apply the gate on
$(P_1,P_2)$, then SWAP $P_0\leftrightarrow P_1$ back, restoring the
mapping: $2(2-1)=2$ SWAPs. For $(q_1,q_3)$: SWAP $P_1\leftrightarrow
P_2$ (carrying $q_1$ to sit next to $q_3$ at $P_3$), apply the gate on
$(P_2,P_3)$, then SWAP $P_1\leftrightarrow P_2$ back, another $2$ SWAPs.

$$
\text{totalSwapsInserted} = \underbrace{0+0+0}_{\text{already adjacent}} + \underbrace{2+2}_{(q_0,q_2),\,(q_1,q_3)} = 4.
$$

`totalSwapsInserted` above evaluates to **{totalSwapsInserted}**. Each
SWAP itself costs 3 CNOTs on real hardware (or is implemented as a
native but still non-trivial-cost operation on some platforms; either
way, it is never free), so the total two-qubit-gate-equivalent cost of
this circuit is the 5 logical gates plus $4\times3=12$ CNOTs' worth of
SWAP machinery:

$$
\text{totalCnotEquivalentGates} = 5 + 4\times 3 = 17,
$$

exactly `totalCnotEquivalentGates` = **{totalCnotEquivalentGates}** above.
A circuit whose "textbook" gate count is 5 two-qubit interactions
actually asks the device to execute 17 CNOT-equivalent operations once
this specific, restricted connectivity is honestly accounted for.

## Couplers Are Not Interchangeable

### Real couplers are not interchangeable

Noise, Decoherence & Scaling's sources-of-noise catalog and Control &
Readout's Calibration lesson both established that real devices are
characterized qubit by qubit and coupler by coupler, not by one shared
number: some couplers carry a stray two-level-system defect or sit
further from resonance and measurably fail more often than their
neighbors, and this is exactly what a device's published calibration
data reports. A compiler that is blind to this, one that only counts
gates and SWAPs, treats every coupler in the routing problem above as
interchangeable. A **noise-aware compiler** instead consults the
device's actual calibration data and chooses, among the mappings that
achieve the *same* SWAP count, the one that routes the most heavily used
operations through the *better* couplers.

In the routing example above, tally how many of the 17 CNOT-equivalent
operations use each of the three physical couplers under the identity
mapping: coupler $P_0\!-\!P_1$ carries the $(q_0,q_1)$ gate plus both
SWAPs for $(q_0,q_2)$, for $1+2\times3=7$ operations; coupler
$P_1\!-\!P_2$ carries $(q_1,q_2)$, the routed $(q_0,q_2)$ gate itself,
and both SWAPs for $(q_1,q_3)$, for $1+1+2\times3=8$ operations;
coupler $P_2\!-\!P_3$ carries only $(q_2,q_3)$ and the routed
$(q_1,q_3)$ gate, for $1+1=2$ operations. These are exactly
`naiveOperationsByEdge` above: **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**,
**{naiveOperationsByEdge.p1p2}**, and **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p2p3}**
respectively (summing to the same 17 total).

Suppose this device's calibration data flags $P_0\!-\!P_1$ as its worst
coupler, a per-operation error rate of `badCouplerErrorRate` =
**{(badCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%**, against a typical
`midCouplerErrorRate` = **{(midCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%**
for $P_1\!-\!P_2$ and a well-calibrated `goodCouplerErrorRate` =
**{(goodCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%** for $P_2\!-\!P_3$. The
identity mapping, chosen with no regard for calibration, happens to run
7 of its 17 operations across the *worst* coupler on the device.

A noise-aware compiler can do better here without inserting a single
extra SWAP: mirror the mapping, $q_0\mapsto P_3,\ q_1\mapsto P_2,\
q_2\mapsto P_1,\ q_3\mapsto P_0$. Every distance in the routing problem
is unchanged by this reflection (the chain looks the same read
backwards), so the SWAP count is still exactly 4 and the total gate
count is still exactly 17: the same compiled circuit size.
What changes is *which* coupler now carries the heavy $(q_0,q_1)$-plus-two-SWAPs
load: under the mirror mapping that load sits between $q_3$ and $q_2$ at
physical edge $P_2\!-\!P_3$ (the *good* coupler), while the bad coupler
$P_0\!-\!P_1$ now carries only the light $(q_2,q_3)$-and-one-routed-gate
load. This is `noiseAwareOperationsByEdge` above: **{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**
operations on the bad coupler (down from **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**),
**{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p1p2}** unchanged on the middle coupler
(it sits on the chain's own mirror axis, so no mapping choice can move
its load elsewhere), and **{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p2p3}** on the
now-good coupler.

Following the same union-bound-style reasoning Fault-Tolerance
Frontiers' resource-estimation capstone used (the circuit succeeds only
if *every* gate succeeds, so the overall success probability is the
product of each gate's own success probability), and modeling each
coupler's operations as independent:

$$
P_{\text{success}} = (1-p_{\text{bad}})^{n_{\text{bad}}}\,(1-p_{\text{mid}})^{n_{\text{mid}}}\,(1-p_{\text{good}})^{n_{\text{good}}}.
$$

**Naive (identity) mapping**, using `naiveOperationsByEdge`'s
$(7,8,2)$ split:

$$
P_{\text{naive}} = (0.97)^7\,(0.99)^8\,(0.995)^2
$$

which comes out at {naiveSuccessProbability.toFixed(4)}, exactly
`naiveSuccessProbability` above: **{(naiveSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(2)}%**.

**Noise-aware (mirrored) mapping**, using `noiseAwareOperationsByEdge`'s
$(2,8,7)$ split, the same three exponents redistributed:

$$
P_{\text{noiseAware}} = (0.97)^2\,(0.99)^8\,(0.995)^7
$$

which comes out at {noiseAwareSuccessProbability.toFixed(4)}, exactly
`noiseAwareSuccessProbability` above: **{(noiseAwareSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(2)}%**.

Both compilations run the exact same 17 CNOT-equivalent gates on the
exact same device. The only difference is which physical coupler
absorbs which share of that load. Yet the noise-aware choice reaches
`successProbabilityGainPoints` = **{successProbabilityGainPoints.toFixed(1)}**
percentage points higher success probability
(**{successProbabilityRelativeGainPercent.toFixed(1)}%** relatively
higher) than the naive one, purely from remapping. That is the whole
point of noise-aware compilation as one comparable number: gate count
held fixed, success probability moved ten percentage points by
respecting calibration data.

## Where the Gate Count Actually Goes

SWAP overhead and noise-aware routing are not academic footnotes. They
are why a circuit's "ideal," textbook gate count (the 5 two-qubit
interactions this lesson started with) can badly understate what
actually runs on near-term hardware, which turned out to be 17
CNOT-equivalent operations before a single error rate was even
considered. Programming Quantum Computers' Simulators vs. Real Hardware
lesson already flagged connectivity constraints as one of the practical
gaps between a simulator and a real device; this lesson has now made
that gap fully quantitative, and added the further fact that the *same*
compiled circuit can perform meaningfully differently on two different
real devices, or even on the same device at two different calibration
times, purely because of which physical qubits a compiler happened to
choose. Neither effect shows up if all anyone reports is a logical gate
count. This course's own upcoming capstone, From Algorithm to Qubit
Count, is where all three ingredients this course has now built
(T-count from Clifford+T Synthesis and Resource Counting, routing
overhead, and noise-aware success probability) get chained together into
one end-to-end resource estimate for a real algorithm, the same kind of
synthesis Fault-Tolerance Frontiers' own capstone already modeled for
the fault-tolerant side of the pipeline.

<ResearchConnection
  title="Noise-aware mapping as a compiler pass"
  source="Murali, Baker, Javadi-Abhari, Chong & Martonosi, ASPLOS 2019 (arXiv:1901.11054)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1901.11054"
>
  The paper that built this lesson's idea into a real compiler and
  measured it on IBM hardware, reporting success-probability improvements
  of the same order as the toy calculation above. Two things worth
  extracting. First, the mapping problem it solves is intractable to
  solve exactly at scale, so every production compiler uses heuristics
  or a bounded-time solver, and two compilers can hand you different
  circuits for the same program. Second, the calibration data the pass
  depends on is stale by construction: it was measured at the last
  calibration, and the device has drifted since.
</ResearchConnection>

<Callout type="warning">
**The success-probability model above assumes errors multiply, and
sometimes they do not.** $P=\prod(1-p_i)$ treats each gate as an
independent coin flip, which is the right model for *stochastic*
(depolarizing-like) error. Coherent error, a systematic over-rotation or
a miscalibrated pulse, accumulates in *amplitude*
instead, so $n$ repetitions of a small coherent error can degrade
fidelity like $n^2 p$ rather than $np$, and the product formula
understates the damage. It can also overstate it: coherent errors from
successive gates sometimes cancel. Randomized compiling exists
specifically to convert coherent error into stochastic error, at a small
gate-count cost, so that estimates like the one above become
trustworthy. Quoting a product-model success probability for a circuit
that has not been randomized is quoting a number whose error bars are
unknown in both directions.
</Callout>

## Assuming the Best Mapping Is Obvious

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming noise-aware compilation works by reducing gate count.** It
doesn't, and this lesson's worked example is deliberately built to show
that directly: both the naive and noise-aware mappings execute exactly
17 CNOT-equivalent gates. The entire {successProbabilityGainPoints.toFixed(1)}-point
success-probability gain comes from *which* physical coupler each
operation lands on, not from doing less work.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating a device's per-qubit or per-coupler error rate as roughly
uniform.** Sources of Noise and Calibration both established that real
devices are characterized component by component because they
are *not* uniform; some qubits or couplers can be several times worse
than the device's median. A resource estimate that plugs in one average
error rate for every gate, as Fault-Tolerance Frontiers' capstone
explicitly did as a stated simplification, will systematically miss
the effect this lesson just computed.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Under the identity mapping, why does coupler $P_1\!-\!P_2$ carry 8
   operations regardless of whether the compiler is noise-aware or not?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the middle coupler sits on the chain's mirror axis, and
   mirroring is the only relabeling the noise-aware compiler used.

   First, where the 8 comes from. Under the identity mapping,
   $P_1\!-\!P_2$ carries the $(q_1,q_2)$ gate, the routed $(q_0,q_2)$
   gate itself once its qubits have been brought adjacent, and both
   SWAPs inserted for $(q_1,q_3)$, at 3 CNOTs each:

   $$
   1 + 1 + 2\times3 = 8 .
   $$

   Now the symmetry. The noise-aware alternative is the mirror
   $q_i\mapsto P_{3-i}$, which sends edge $P_0\!-\!P_1$ to
   $P_2\!-\!P_3$ and back, and sends $P_1\!-\!P_2$ to itself. And the
   circuit's own interaction list is invariant under $i\mapsto3-i$:
   $(0,1)\leftrightarrow(2,3)$, $(0,2)\leftrightarrow(1,3)$, and
   $(1,2)$ maps to itself. So mirroring permutes the three couplers'
   loads exactly as it permutes the couplers themselves. The 7 and the 2
   trade places; the 8 is a fixed point.

   That is why the lesson's table reads 7/8/2 before and 2/8/7 after.
   The middle number was never going to move, and it is worth
   recognizing that as a structural fact about the pair of layouts rather
   than a coincidence of the arithmetic. Question 2 asks what happens
   when that immovable 8 is sitting on the coupler you most want to
   avoid.

   </details>

2. If a device's *middle* coupler ($P_1\!-\!P_2$ here) were the flagged
   worst one instead of an end coupler, would mirroring the mapping help
   at all? Explain why or why not using the chain's own symmetry.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Barely. Mirroring cannot touch the middle coupler at all, for exactly
   the reason question 1 gives: the mirror fixes that edge, so its 8
   operations stay on it whatever the calibration data says.

   What mirroring can still do is decide which of the two *end* couplers
   carries the heavy 7 and which carries the light 2, so it helps only to
   the extent that those two differ from each other. Price it. Put the
   worst rate $0.03$ on the middle coupler and leave $0.01$ and $0.005$
   on the two ends. The identity layout $(7,8,2)$ succeeds with
   probability $0.7232$; the mirror $(2,8,7)$ gives $0.7417$. A gain of
   about $1.8$ percentage points, against the $10$ points mirroring
   bought when the bad coupler was on an end. The lever is still there
   and it is pointed at the wrong problem.

   **What does work is a mapping that is not a mirror.** Send
   $q_0\mapsto P_0$, $q_2\mapsto P_1$, $q_1\mapsto P_2$,
   $q_3\mapsto P_3$. Every required interaction's distance profile
   becomes $(2,1,2,1,1)$ instead of $(1,1,1,2,2)$, which is still 4
   SWAPs and still 17 total operations, so this costs nothing in gate
   count. But the load redistributes to $(7,3,7)$: routing
   $(q_0,q_1)$ and $(q_2,q_3)$ now spends its SWAPs on the two end
   couplers and only lands one gate each on the middle, and the middle
   coupler's total drops from 8 to 3.

   With the middle coupler at $0.03$ that layout succeeds with
   probability $0.8213$, about 8 points better than the mirror and 10
   better than the identity. The immovable 8 was immovable only under
   mirroring.

   **The general lesson,** which is the reason this question is worth
   more than its answer: the mirror is a *symmetry* of the layout, and
   symmetries can only permute what is already there. Real noise-aware
   compilation searches over mappings that are not symmetries, and the
   useful ones change which interactions are near each other, not merely
   which end of the chain they sit on. A search restricted to symmetries
   will find the 7-and-2 swap and will never find the 8-to-3 one.

   **The wrong turn** would be concluding that the middle load can always
   be pushed down. It cannot go to zero. Removing $P_1\!-\!P_2$
   disconnects the chain, so every interaction between the two halves has
   to cross it, and the best any 2-2 split of the logical qubits can do
   for this circuit is 3 crossing interactions out of 5. The middle
   coupler has an irreducible share fixed by graph connectivity, and a
   compiler's job is to reach that floor rather than to escape it.

   </details>

3. The noise-aware mapping above still runs 8 of its 17 operations
   across the mid-error coupler. Explain why a compiler cannot always
   route every heavy load onto the single best coupler on a device with
   only three couplers to choose from.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a coupler is not a destination the compiler can send work to.
   It is a pair of adjacent physical sites, and which gates can run there
   is decided by where the qubits are, which is decided by the mapping
   and the routing, both of which are constrained by the same graph.

   Three constraints stack, and each one alone is enough to rule the idea
   out.

   **A coupler touches only two qubits.** $P_2\!-\!P_3$ can execute a
   gate only between whatever currently sits at $P_2$ and $P_3$. This
   circuit needs interactions among all four logical qubits, so the work
   cannot all be assigned to one edge no matter how the labels are
   arranged. Load has to spread simply because the circuit is not a
   two-qubit circuit.

   **The middle coupler is a bridge.** Delete it and the chain falls into
   $\{P_0,P_1\}$ and $\{P_2,P_3\}$. Every interaction whose two qubits
   end up in different halves must cross it, and so must every SWAP that
   carries a qubit across. The compiler's only freedom is which 2-2 split
   of the logical qubits to induce, and for this circuit's interaction
   graph the best available split still leaves 3 of the 5 interactions
   crossing. That is a floor set by connectivity, and no amount of
   compiler effort goes below it.

   **Moving load costs gates.** Getting more work onto a favoured coupler
   means putting the relevant qubits next to it, which lengthens the
   distances for everything else, which inserts SWAPs, and each SWAP is
   three more two-qubit operations that themselves have to run somewhere.
   Past a certain point the extra operations cost more expected error
   than the better coupler saves. The optimum is a real trade rather than
   a free relabeling. The lesson's remapping escaped the trade because it
   was a symmetry of the chain; question 2's $(7,3,7)$ layout escaped it
   for a different reason, by rearranging the distance profile without
   changing its total. Free remappings exist, then, but they are the
   exception a particular circuit happens to admit, not something a
   compiler can count on.

   Set against those, "route everything through the best coupler" is not
   a hard optimization problem that current compilers fail at. It is a
   description of something the hardware graph forbids.

   **What a compiler is actually optimizing,** stated correctly, is the
   *product* over couplers of $(1-\epsilon_e)^{n_e}$, subject to the
   $n_e$ being a feasible routing of the circuit on the device graph. It
   pushes exponent onto small $\epsilon_e$ where the graph permits, and
   accepts the floor where it does not. That is an optimization with
   genuine structure and no clean closed form, which is why real
   compilers search rather than compute, and why the search is worth
   running: 10 percentage points of success probability, for free, on a
   17-operation circuit is a large return for changing nothing but a
   labeling.

   </details>

## Summary

- Routing a circuit's logical two-qubit gates onto a device's limited
  connectivity graph can multiply its real gate count substantially:
  this lesson's 5-interaction, 4-logical-qubit circuit needed 4 inserted
  SWAP gates (`totalSwapsInserted`), for 17 total CNOT-equivalent
  operations (`totalCnotEquivalentGates`) on a 4-qubit linear chain.
- Real devices have non-uniform, individually-calibrated error rates
  across their qubits and couplers (Sources of Noise, Calibration). A
  noise-aware compiler chooses among mappings with identical gate count
  to favor the device's better-calibrated components for its
  heaviest-used operations.
- Computed directly for this lesson's example: the same 17-gate circuit
  reaches {(naiveSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(1)}% estimated success
  probability under a naive, calibration-blind mapping, versus
  {(noiseAwareSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(1)}% under a noise-aware
  one. That gap is driven purely by which physical qubits were chosen,
  not by any change in what the circuit computes or how many gates it
  uses.
