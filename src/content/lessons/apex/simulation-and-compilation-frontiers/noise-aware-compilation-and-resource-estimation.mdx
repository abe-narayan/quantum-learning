## Motivation

Quantum Compilation & Transpilation established the mechanics of
SWAP-network routing on an idealized linear chain where every coupler
was equally reliable, and the previous lesson, Clifford+T Synthesis and
Resource Counting, established T-count as the currency a fault-tolerant
compiler actually spends. Real devices force two more honest ingredients
into the picture at once: a circuit with several two-qubit interactions
rarely maps cleanly onto a small device's connectivity graph, so SWAP
overhead compounds across many gates, not just one; and a real device's
physical error rates are never uniform across its qubits and couplers.
This lesson works both pieces through a single concrete example, then
combines them into one number: an estimated circuit success probability,
computed two different ways for the *same* gate count.

## Mathematical Development

### A concrete routing problem

Take a small circuit needing two-qubit interactions between four logical
qubits $q_0, q_1, q_2, q_3$, specifically these five pairs:

$$
(q_0,q_1),\ (q_1,q_2),\ (q_2,q_3),\ (q_0,q_2),\ (q_1,q_3).
$$

Compile it for a 4-qubit linear-chain device, physical qubits
$P_0\!-\!P_1\!-\!P_2\!-\!P_3$, where only adjacent physical qubits share
a coupler (Physical Qubit Platforms' nearest-neighbor connectivity
graph, the same constraint Quantum Compilation & Transpilation
introduced for a single pair). Start with the simplest valid mapping,
the **identity mapping** $q_i \mapsto P_i$.

Under this mapping, three of the five required interactions are already
between physically adjacent qubits and need no SWAPs at all:
$(q_0,q_1)\to(P_0,P_1)$, $(q_1,q_2)\to(P_1,P_2)$, and
$(q_2,q_3)\to(P_2,P_3)$. The remaining two are not adjacent:
$(q_0,q_2)$ sits at chain distance $d=2$ (one qubit, $P_1$, in between),
and so does $(q_1,q_3)$ (with $P_2$ in between).

### Counting the SWAP overhead

Reusing Quantum Compilation & Transpilation's own result for a linear
chain, bridging a gap of distance $d$ costs $\text{swapOverheadForDistance}(d) = 2(d-1)$
SWAP gates: walk one qubit's data into adjacency, apply the two-qubit
gate, walk it back. For $(q_0,q_2)$: SWAP $P_0\leftrightarrow P_1$
(carrying $q_0$'s data to sit next to $q_2$ at $P_2$), apply the gate on
$(P_1,P_2)$, then SWAP $P_0\leftrightarrow P_1$ back, restoring the
mapping — $2(2-1)=2$ SWAPs. For $(q_1,q_3)$: SWAP $P_1\leftrightarrow
P_2$ (carrying $q_1$ to sit next to $q_3$ at $P_3$), apply the gate on
$(P_2,P_3)$, then SWAP $P_1\leftrightarrow P_2$ back — another $2$ SWAPs.

$$
\text{totalSwapsInserted} = \underbrace{0+0+0}_{\text{already adjacent}} + \underbrace{2+2}_{(q_0,q_2),\,(q_1,q_3)} = 4.
$$

`totalSwapsInserted` above evaluates to **{totalSwapsInserted}**. Each
SWAP itself costs 3 CNOTs on real hardware (or is implemented as a
native but still non-trivial-cost operation on some platforms — either
way, it is never free), so the total two-qubit-gate-equivalent cost of
this circuit is the 5 logical gates plus $4\times3=12$ CNOTs' worth of
SWAP machinery:

$$
\text{totalCnotEquivalentGates} = 5 + 4\times 3 = 17,
$$

exactly `totalCnotEquivalentGates` = **{totalCnotEquivalentGates}** above.
A circuit whose "textbook" gate count is 5 two-qubit interactions
actually asks the device to execute 17 CNOT-equivalent operations once
this specific, restricted connectivity is honestly accounted for.

## Physical Interpretation: Why Noise-Aware Compilation Matters

### Real couplers are not interchangeable

Noise, Decoherence & Scaling's sources-of-noise catalog and Control &
Readout's Calibration lesson both established that real devices are
characterized qubit by qubit and coupler by coupler, not by one shared
number: some couplers carry a stray two-level-system defect or sit
further from resonance and measurably fail more often than their
neighbors, and this is exactly what a device's published calibration
data reports. A compiler that is blind to this, one that only counts
gates and SWAPs, treats every coupler in the routing problem above as
interchangeable. A **noise-aware compiler** instead consults the
device's actual calibration data and chooses, among the mappings that
achieve the *same* SWAP count, the one that routes the most heavily used
operations through the *better* couplers.

In the routing example above, tally how many of the 17 CNOT-equivalent
operations use each of the three physical couplers under the identity
mapping: coupler $P_0\!-\!P_1$ carries the $(q_0,q_1)$ gate plus both
SWAPs for $(q_0,q_2)$, for $1+2\times3=7$ operations; coupler
$P_1\!-\!P_2$ carries $(q_1,q_2)$, the routed $(q_0,q_2)$ gate itself,
and both SWAPs for $(q_1,q_3)$, for $1+1+2\times3=8$ operations;
coupler $P_2\!-\!P_3$ carries only $(q_2,q_3)$ and the routed
$(q_1,q_3)$ gate, for $1+1=2$ operations. These are exactly
`naiveOperationsByEdge` above: **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**,
**{naiveOperationsByEdge.p1p2}**, and **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p2p3}**
respectively (summing to the same 17 total).

Suppose this device's calibration data flags $P_0\!-\!P_1$ as its worst
coupler, a per-operation error rate of `badCouplerErrorRate` =
**{(badCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%**, against a typical
`midCouplerErrorRate` = **{(midCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%**
for $P_1\!-\!P_2$ and a well-calibrated `goodCouplerErrorRate` =
**{(goodCouplerErrorRate * 100).toFixed(1)}%** for $P_2\!-\!P_3$. The
identity mapping, chosen with no regard for calibration, happens to run
7 of its 17 operations across the *worst* coupler on the device.

A noise-aware compiler can do better here without inserting a single
extra SWAP: mirror the mapping, $q_0\mapsto P_3,\ q_1\mapsto P_2,\
q_2\mapsto P_1,\ q_3\mapsto P_0$. Every distance in the routing problem
is unchanged by this reflection (the chain looks the same read
backwards), so the SWAP count is still exactly 4 and the total gate
count is still exactly 17 — genuinely the same compiled circuit size.
What changes is *which* coupler now carries the heavy $(q_0,q_1)$-plus-two-SWAPs
load: under the mirror mapping that load sits between $q_3$ and $q_2$ at
physical edge $P_2\!-\!P_3$ (the *good* coupler), while the bad coupler
$P_0\!-\!P_1$ now carries only the light $(q_2,q_3)$-and-one-routed-gate
load. This is `noiseAwareOperationsByEdge` above: **{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**
operations on the bad coupler (down from **{naiveOperationsByEdge.p0p1}**),
**{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p1p2}** unchanged on the middle coupler
(it sits on the chain's own mirror axis, so no mapping choice can move
its load elsewhere), and **{noiseAwareOperationsByEdge.p2p3}** on the
now-good coupler.

Following the same union-bound-style reasoning Fault-Tolerance
Frontiers' resource-estimation capstone used (the circuit succeeds only
if *every* gate succeeds, so the overall success probability is the
product of each gate's own success probability), and modeling each
coupler's operations as independent:

$$
P_{\text{success}} = (1-p_{\text{bad}})^{n_{\text{bad}}}\,(1-p_{\text{mid}})^{n_{\text{mid}}}\,(1-p_{\text{good}})^{n_{\text{good}}}.
$$

**Naive (identity) mapping**, using `naiveOperationsByEdge`'s
$(7,8,2)$ split:

$$
P_{\text{naive}} = (0.97)^7\,(0.99)^8\,(0.995)^2 \approx {naiveSuccessProbability.toFixed(4)},
$$

exactly `naiveSuccessProbability` above: **{(naiveSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(2)}%**.

**Noise-aware (mirrored) mapping**, using `noiseAwareOperationsByEdge`'s
$(2,8,7)$ split — the same three exponents, only redistributed:

$$
P_{\text{noiseAware}} = (0.97)^2\,(0.99)^8\,(0.995)^7 \approx {noiseAwareSuccessProbability.toFixed(4)},
$$

exactly `noiseAwareSuccessProbability` above: **{(noiseAwareSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(2)}%**.

Both compilations run the exact same 17 CNOT-equivalent gates on the
exact same device. The only difference is which physical coupler
absorbs which share of that load. Yet the noise-aware choice reaches
`successProbabilityGainPoints` = **{successProbabilityGainPoints.toFixed(1)}**
percentage points higher success probability
(**{successProbabilityRelativeGainPercent.toFixed(1)}%** relatively
higher) than the naive one, purely from remapping. This is the entire
point of noise-aware compilation stated as one comparable number: gate
count held fixed, success probability moved by roughly a tenth just by
respecting calibration data.

## Why This Matters

SWAP overhead and noise-aware routing are not academic footnotes. They
are exactly why a circuit's "ideal," textbook gate count — the 5
two-qubit interactions this lesson started with — can dramatically
understate what actually runs on near-term hardware, which turned out
to be 17 CNOT-equivalent operations before a single error rate was even
considered. Programming Quantum Computers' Simulators vs. Real Hardware
lesson already flagged connectivity constraints as one of the practical
gaps between a simulator and a real device; this lesson has now made
that gap fully quantitative, and added the further fact that the *same*
compiled circuit can perform meaningfully differently on two different
real devices, or even on the same device at two different calibration
times, purely because of which physical qubits a compiler happened to
choose. Neither effect shows up if all anyone reports is a logical gate
count. This course's own upcoming capstone, From Algorithm to Qubit
Count, is where all three ingredients this course has now built —
T-count (Clifford+T Synthesis and Resource Counting), routing overhead,
and noise-aware success probability — finally get chained together into
one end-to-end resource estimate for a real algorithm, the same kind of
synthesis Fault-Tolerance Frontiers' own capstone already modeled for
the fault-tolerant side of the pipeline.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming noise-aware compilation works by reducing gate count.** It
doesn't, and this lesson's worked example is deliberately built to show
that directly: both the naive and noise-aware mappings execute exactly
17 CNOT-equivalent gates. The entire {successProbabilityGainPoints.toFixed(1)}-point
success-probability gain comes from *which* physical coupler each
operation lands on, not from doing less work.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating a device's per-qubit or per-coupler error rate as roughly
uniform.** Sources of Noise and Calibration both established that real
devices are characterized component by component precisely because they
are *not* uniform; some qubits or couplers can be several times worse
than the device's median. A resource estimate that plugs in one average
error rate for every gate, as Fault-Tolerance Frontiers' capstone
explicitly did as a stated simplification, will systematically miss
exactly the effect this lesson just computed.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Routing a circuit's logical two-qubit gates onto a device's limited
  connectivity graph can multiply its real gate count substantially:
  this lesson's 5-interaction, 4-logical-qubit circuit needed 4 inserted
  SWAP gates (`totalSwapsInserted`), for 17 total CNOT-equivalent
  operations (`totalCnotEquivalentGates`) on a 4-qubit linear chain.
- Real devices have non-uniform, individually-calibrated error rates
  across their qubits and couplers (Sources of Noise, Calibration). A
  noise-aware compiler chooses among mappings with identical gate count
  to favor the device's better-calibrated components for its
  heaviest-used operations.
- Computed directly for this lesson's example: the same 17-gate circuit
  reaches {(naiveSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(1)}% estimated success
  probability under a naive, calibration-blind mapping, versus
  {(noiseAwareSuccessProbability * 100).toFixed(1)}% under a noise-aware
  one — a real, computed gap driven purely by which physical qubits were
  chosen, not by any change in what the circuit computes or how many
  gates it uses.
