## Making the Qualitative Case Quantitative

Tensor Network Methods made the qualitative case: a state with limited
entanglement can be written as a chain of small tensors instead of one
flat list of $2^n$ amplitudes, and the size of the "bond" connecting
adjacent tensors controls how much entanglement the chain can hold. That
lesson never showed where the chain comes from, and it used "bond
dimension" as an intuitive knob, never defining it precisely.
Going deeper means building the decomposition itself, from an actual
$2^n$-amplitude tensor, using singular value decomposition, the same
tool Schmidt Decomposition and Purification used to prove the two-party
Schmidt theorem. Here it gets applied repeatedly, once per cut, to a
multi-party chain.

## Cut by Cut, With the SVD

### Reshaping: from amplitude tensor to matrix

An $n$-qubit state is a rank-$n$ amplitude tensor $\psi_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_n}$
with $2^n$ complex entries, $i_k\in\{0,1\}$. Picking any cut after qubit
$k$ splits the indices into two groups, $(i_1,\ldots,i_k)$ and
$(i_{k+1},\ldots,i_n)$, and *reshaping* (not changing a single number,
only regrouping which indices are rows and which are columns) turns
$\psi$ into an ordinary $2^k\times 2^{n-k}$ matrix $\Psi^{(k)}$. This is
exactly the amplitude-matrix move Schmidt Decomposition and Purification
built for a single bipartition ($M_{ij}$ for a 2-qubit state); the only
new idea is doing it at *every* cut, one after another.

### The construction: SVD, cut by cut

<TheoremBox title="Matrix product state decomposition" provenance="derived">

Reshape $\psi$ into $\Psi^{(1)}$ (rows $i_1$, columns
$(i_2,\ldots,i_n)$), an ordinary $2\times 2^{n-1}$ matrix, and take its
SVD: $\Psi^{(1)}=U^{(1)}\Sigma^{(1)}V^{(1)\dagger}$. Define
$A^{i_1}_{a_1}\equiv U^{(1)}_{i_1,a_1}$, a $1\times\chi_1$ row for each
$i_1$, where $\chi_1$ is the number of nonzero singular values kept.
Absorb the rest, $\Sigma^{(1)}V^{(1)\dagger}$, into a new tensor indexed
by $(a_1,i_2,\ldots,i_n)$, reshape it into a $(2\chi_1)\times 2^{n-2}$
matrix grouping $(a_1,i_2)$ as rows, and take *its* SVD to peel off
$A^{i_2}_{a_1,a_2}$ (now a full $\chi_1\times\chi_2$ matrix for each
$i_2$). Repeating through site $n-1$ leaves a final
$\chi_{n-1}\times 2$ piece that is itself $A^{i_n}_{a_{n-1}}$ (a
$\chi_{n-1}\times 1$ column for each $i_n$). The result is

$$
\boxed{\psi_{i_1 i_2\cdots i_n} = \sum_{a_1,\ldots,a_{n-1}} A^{i_1}_{1,a_1}\,A^{i_2}_{a_1,a_2}\cdots A^{i_n}_{a_{n-1},1}}
$$

exact for any state, with no approximation yet made.

</TheoremBox>

<DefinitionBox title="Bond dimension">

The **bond dimension** $\chi_k$ at the cut between site $k$ and site
$k+1$ is the number of singular values kept at that step, i.e. the size
of the matrix index $a_k$ that $A^{i_k}$ and $A^{i_{k+1}}$ share. Since
the SVD is exact when every nonzero singular value is kept, $\chi_k$
computed this way is not a free choice; it is exactly the **Schmidt
rank** of $\psi$ across that cut (the rank of $\Psi^{(k)}$): the two
notions are the same number, seen from two directions (a linear-algebra
rank versus a tensor-network index size). $\chi_k=1$ means the state
factors across that cut (no entanglement there); a general $n$-qubit
state can need $\chi_k$ as large as $2^{\min(k,n-k)}$.
`eigenvaluesHermitian2x2`, already used for exactly this purpose in
Schmidt Decomposition and Purification, is enough to certify $\chi_k$
whenever the smaller side of a cut is a single qubit, which is every
cut in the worked example below.

</DefinitionBox>

An *approximate* MPS truncates each SVD to keep only the largest $\chi$
singular values, dropping the smallest ones (and the entanglement they
represent). Nothing below does this: the GHZ construction that follows
keeps every nonzero singular value, so it is an exact rewriting of the
state, not a lossy compression.

## The Area Law and the Simulability Boundary

### The area law

<TheoremBox title="Area law for gapped, local 1D ground states" provenance="cited">

For the ground state of a **gapped** (nonzero energy gap above the
ground state), **local** 1D Hamiltonian, the entanglement entropy across
any spatial cut is bounded by a constant that does **not** grow with
system size $n$. This is a nontrivial theorem, not a heuristic:
proved rigorously for 1D gapped systems building on Hastings' 2007 work
(M. B. Hastings, "An area law for one-dimensional quantum systems,"
*J. Stat. Mech.* (2007) P08024); the higher-dimensional generalization
remains an important, harder, still-actively-researched question, well
beyond this lesson's scope to derive.

</TheoremBox>

Contrast this with a **generic** (e.g. Haar-random) $n$-qubit state,
whose entanglement entropy across a cut through the middle grows
*linearly* with the smaller side's size, a **volume law**. A bond
dimension $\chi$ can carry at most $\log_2\chi$ bits, so
$S\leq\log_2\chi$, and a volume-law entropy of order $n/2$ *forces*
$\chi\geq2^{n/2}$ at the middle cut: exactly the exponential wall Tensor
Network Methods started from.

<Callout type="warning">
Read that inequality in the right direction. $S\leq\log_2\chi$ makes
entropy a **lower** bound on bond dimension, never an upper one, so a
*bounded* entropy does not cap $\chi$. The definition above is explicit
that $\chi_k$ is the Schmidt rank, and rank is a count of nonzero
singular values that weights say nothing about. Give a cut the spectrum
$\{1-\delta\}$ together with $2^{n/2}$ equal tiny weights sharing
$\delta$: at $\delta\approx2/n$ the entropy stays a couple of bits no
matter how large $n$ gets, while the Schmidt rank, and therefore the
*exact* bond dimension, is $1+2^{n/2}$. An area law by itself bounds no
exact bond dimension.
</Callout>

What an area law really buys is *fast decay* of the Schmidt spectrum,
which is a statement about approximation rather than about rank. Keep
the largest singular values and discard the tail, and the discarded
weight controls the fidelity error; for a gapped, local 1D ground state the
number that has to be kept to reach error $\varepsilon$ is
$\chi=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)$, against $2^{n/2}$ for a
volume-law state and no bound at all in the exact, untruncated regime
this lesson works in. That is the real mechanism behind the MPS memory
cost from Tensor Network Methods, $\sim 32n\chi^2$ bytes, staying
*polynomial* in $n$ for an area-law state instead of climbing back
toward $2^n$: not that $\chi$ cannot grow, but that it grows
polynomially rather than exponentially.

### Why this matters: the boundary of classical simulability

This is not a curiosity about GHZ states: it is the reason tensor
networks are the primary classical numerical tool condensed-matter
physicists actually use to simulate large 1D quantum many-body systems
(and, via more sophisticated generalizations such as PEPS and MERA,
higher-dimensional ones too) on ordinary classical computers. A gapped
1D ground state's rapidly decaying Schmidt spectrum, and the polynomial
truncated bond dimension it permits, is what makes an exponentially
large Hilbert space classically tractable in
practice, and what a highly-entangled algorithm output (this
course's own eventual quantum circuits, or Tensor Network Methods' own
Grover's-algorithm counterexample) does not have. Making that boundary
*precise*, not just "large versus small entanglement" but an exact,
checkable criterion for when a classical computer can and cannot keep up
with a quantum one, is the job of this course's next lesson,
When Classical Simulation Works, and it reuses the bond-dimension and
area-law machinery built here directly.

## GHZ, Reduced to Bond Dimension 2

**Construct the exact MPS decomposition of the GHZ state
$|\text{GHZ}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|000\rangle+|111\rangle)$, and
verify its bond dimension is exactly 2 at both cuts.**

$|\text{GHZ}\rangle$ has only two nonzero amplitudes,
$\psi_{000}=\psi_{111}=1/\sqrt2$. Reshaping across the first cut (qubit
1 versus qubits 2,3, rows $i_1$, columns $(i_2,i_3)$ in order
$00,01,10,11$) gives the $2\times4$ amplitude matrix below.

<MatrixGrid matrix={amplitudeMatrixCut1} label="Ψ⁽¹⁾ (qubit 1 | qubits 2,3)" ariaLabel="The 2 by 4 reshaped amplitude matrix for GHZ across the first cut, with entries 1/sqrt2 at positions (0,0) and (1,3), zero elsewhere" digits={3} />

Its two rows are already orthogonal, each with norm $1/\sqrt2$, so its
SVD is immediate: $U^{(1)}=I_2$ and the two nonzero **singular values**
are both exactly $\sigma=1/\sqrt2\approx$ {sigma1Cut1.toFixed(6)}
(computed directly: `sigma1Cut1` = {sigma1Cut1.toFixed(6)},
`sigma2Cut1` = {sigma2Cut1.toFixed(6)}, `singularValuesEqualCut1` =
{String(singularValuesEqualCut1)}). Equivalently, via $\rho_A=MM^\dagger$
(the identity Schmidt Decomposition and Purification proved in general):

<MatrixGrid matrix={rhoQubit1} label="ρ (qubit 1) = Tr₂,₃(|GHZ⟩⟨GHZ|)" ariaLabel="The reduced density matrix of qubit 1 for GHZ, diag(0.5, 0.5)" highlightDiagonal digits={3} />

computed here by this platform's own `partialTrace(rhoGHZ, 3, [1, 2])`,
with `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` returning $\lambda_1=$ {lambda1Cut1.toFixed(6)},
$\lambda_2=$ {lambda2Cut1.toFixed(6)}, matching $\sigma^2$ exactly, and
confirmed by direct computation: `rhoQubit1MatchesMM` =
{String(rhoQubit1MatchesMM)}. Both singular values are nonzero, so the
**bond dimension across this cut is exactly 2**
(`bondDimensionCut1` = {bondDimensionCut1}), matching the Schmidt rank,
and `vonNeumannEntropy(rhoQubit1)` = {entropyCut1.toFixed(6)} bit, exactly
the 1-bit answer Entanglement Entropy for Pure Bipartite States gets for
any Bell-pair-like maximally-entangled cut.

Repeating the procedure at the second cut (qubits 1,2 versus qubit 3,
rows $(i_1,i_2)$ in order $00,01,10,11$, column $i_3$):

<MatrixGrid matrix={amplitudeMatrixCut2} label="Ψ⁽²⁾ (qubits 1,2 | qubit 3)" ariaLabel="The 4 by 2 reshaped amplitude matrix for GHZ across the second cut, with entries 1/sqrt2 at positions (0,0) and (3,1), zero elsewhere" digits={3} />

is the same story transposed: two orthogonal columns of norm $1/\sqrt2$,
so the singular values are again both exactly $1/\sqrt2$. The Schmidt
theorem (proved for general $d_A\times d_B$ in Schmidt Decomposition and
Purification, not just the $2\times2$ case) guarantees the *same* nonzero
eigenvalue set appears on whichever side is checked, so it suffices to
check the 2-dimensional side (qubit 3) directly:

<MatrixGrid matrix={rhoQubit3} label="ρ (qubit 3) = Tr₁,₂(|GHZ⟩⟨GHZ|)" ariaLabel="The reduced density matrix of qubit 3 for GHZ, diag(0.5, 0.5)" highlightDiagonal digits={3} />

giving $\lambda_1=$ {lambda1Cut2.toFixed(6)}, $\lambda_2=$
{lambda2Cut2.toFixed(6)}, entropy {entropyCut2.toFixed(6)} bit, and
**bond dimension {bondDimensionCut2}** again (`bondDimensionCut2` =
{bondDimensionCut2}). Both cuts of $|\text{GHZ}\rangle$ need $\chi=2$,
never more, regardless of which cut is chosen: a checkable fact
about this particular (highly symmetric) state, not a general law about
every state's cuts (see Common Mistakes).

### The explicit tensors, and a direct check

Collecting the per-site matrices ($A^{i_1}$ a $1\times2$ row,
$A^{i_2}$ a full $2\times2$ matrix, $A^{i_3}$ a $2\times1$ column):

$$
A^{0}_{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\end{pmatrix},\ A^{1}_{(1)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A^{0}_{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\ A^{1}_{(2)}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A^{0}_{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}1/\sqrt2\\0\end{pmatrix},\ A^{1}_{(3)}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1/\sqrt2\end{pmatrix}
$$

built here as real `Matrix` instances (`mpsSite1`, `mpsSite2`,
`mpsSite3`) and contracted with nothing but `Matrix.mul` and `.get(0,0)`
(`mpsAmplitude(i1, i2, i3) = mpsSite1[i1].mul(mpsSite2[i2]).mul(mpsSite3[i3]).get(0, 0)`).
Checking all 8 basis states against `psiGHZ` directly gives
`mpsAmplitudesMatch` = {String(mpsAmplitudesMatch)}: the chain of small
tensors reproduces $|\text{GHZ}\rangle$ exactly, not approximately.

Count what that costs. Each site stores one matrix per value of its
physical index, so the three sites hold
$2(1{\times}2) + 2(2{\times}2) + 2(2{\times}1) = 4+8+4 = 16$ numbers
against GHZ's own $8$ amplitudes. At $n=3$ the MPS is a net loss, and
saying so is more useful than implying otherwise. The point is the
scaling: the same $\chi=2$ tensors tile out to any $n$, with $n-2$
interior sites at $8$ numbers each, for $8+8(n-2)$ numbers total against
$2^n$ amplitudes. The two costs cross at $n=5$ and diverge from there.

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming every cut of an MPS shares the same bond dimension.** GHZ's
$\chi=2$ at both of its cuts is a special symmetry of that particular
state; a generic state's Schmidt rank is a
different number at each cut (often larger toward the middle of a chain,
smaller near the ends, since $\chi_k\leq 2^{\min(k,n-k)}$ caps it
differently at every $k$).
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Conflating bond dimension (an integer, the Schmidt rank) with
entanglement entropy (a real number, in bits).** They are related but
not the same quantity: $|\phi\rangle=\sqrt{0.99}|000\rangle+\sqrt{0.01}|111\rangle$
has the identical bond dimension 2 at the first cut as GHZ
(`bondDimensionSkewed` = {bondDimensionSkewed}, both nonzero
eigenvalues {lambda1Skewed.toFixed(4)} and {lambda2Skewed.toFixed(4)}),
yet its entropy is only {entropySkewed.toFixed(4)} bit, nowhere near
GHZ's full 1 bit. The *rank* only counts how many Schmidt terms are
present, not how evenly they're weighted.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does keeping *every* nonzero singular value at each SVD step (as
   this lesson's construction does) make the resulting MPS an *exact*
   rewriting of the state rather than an approximation?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the SVD is an equality, and the only terms being dropped are
   the ones multiplied by zero.

   Write one step out. For the reshaped amplitude matrix $M$,

   $$
   M_{ij} = \sum_{a=1}^{r} U_{ia}\,\sigma_a\,V^\dagger_{aj},
   $$

   where $r$ is the rank, that is, the number of nonzero singular values.
   That sum is not an approximation to $M$; it reconstructs $M$ entry for
   entry. Every index $a>r$ carries $\sigma_a=0$ and contributes nothing,
   so declining to store those columns changes no amplitude anywhere. The
   construction performs $n-1$ such factorizations in sequence, each an
   exact rewriting of the previous step's tensor, and a chain of
   equalities is an equality.

   The contrast with a truncated MPS is quantitative. Cutting the sum at
   some $\chi<r$ leaves an error whose size is exactly the discarded
   weight,

   $$
   \lVert M - M_\chi\rVert_F^2 = \sum_{a>\chi}\sigma_a^2 ,
   $$

   which is zero precisely when every discarded $\sigma_a$ was already
   zero. So "keep all nonzero singular values" and "incur no error" are
   the same instruction, and the error formula is the reason why.

   Worth adding what this buys beyond exactness. Since the number of
   nonzero singular values at a cut *is* the Schmidt rank there, keeping
   all of them sets $\chi_k$ to the smallest value at which an exact MPS
   is possible at all. The construction is not merely exact, it is exact
   at minimum bond dimension, so nothing is being spent on padding.

   **The wrong turn** is assuming that because an MPS looks like a
   compression, from $2^n$ amplitudes to roughly $2n\chi^2$ numbers,
   something must have been thrown away to achieve it. Nothing was. The
   compression is real when $\chi$ is small and it is genuinely lossless,
   because $\chi$ is not a setting chosen in advance; it is whatever the
   state's own Schmidt ranks turn out to be. For a highly entangled state
   with $\chi=2^{n/2}$ at the middle cut, the exact MPS is as large as
   the original amplitude list and saves nothing at all. Exactness is
   never traded away; the *savings* are what vary from state to state.

   </details>

2. For the product state $|000\rangle$, what is the bond dimension at
   both cuts, and what does that say about the corresponding $A^{i_k}$
   matrices' shapes?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\chi_1=\chi_2=1$, and every $A^{i_k}$ is a $1\times1$ matrix, which
   is to say a single number.

   The bond dimension is the Schmidt rank, and $|000\rangle$ is a product
   state, so cutting it anywhere gives a Schmidt decomposition with one
   term. Rank $1$ at both cuts.

   Following the shape rules through: $A^{i_1}$ is $1\times\chi_1 =
   1\times1$, $A^{i_2}$ is $\chi_1\times\chi_2 = 1\times1$, and
   $A^{i_3}$ is $\chi_2\times1 = 1\times1$. Concretely $A^0=1$ and
   $A^1=0$ at each site, giving
   $\psi_{i_1i_2i_3}=A^{i_1}A^{i_2}A^{i_3}$, which is $1$ for
   $i_1i_2i_3=000$ and $0$ otherwise.

   The observation worth keeping is that this is not a special case, it is
   a restatement. Matrix multiplication of $1\times1$ matrices is
   multiplication of numbers, so "$\chi=1$ everywhere" says the
   amplitudes factorize across every cut, which is the definition of a
   product state. The MPS format has not represented the product state,
   it has *become* it.

   **The wrong turn** is reading $\chi=1$ as "only one nonzero
   amplitude." It says nothing of the kind. $|{+}{+}{+}\rangle$ also has
   $\chi=1$ at both cuts, with all eight amplitudes nonzero and equal.
   Bond dimension counts Schmidt rank, which is a statement about
   correlation between the two sides of a cut, not about how many
   amplitudes happen to be zero.

   </details>

3. If a 1D gapped Hamiltonian's ground state has entanglement entropy
   bounded by 3 bits across every cut no matter how large $n$ gets, what
   does the area law say happens to the required bond dimension $\chi$
   as $n\to\infty$, and why is that exactly the property that makes MPS
   simulation of that ground state efficient?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The *truncated* bond dimension needed for a fixed accuracy stays
   polynomial in $n$ and $1/\varepsilon$. The *exact* bond dimension is
   not bounded by the area law at all, and saying it is caps the wrong
   quantity.

   Start with the inequality and read its direction carefully. A bond of
   dimension $\chi$ carries at most $\log_2\chi$ bits, so
   $S\le\log_2\chi$, which rearranges to

   $$
   \chi \;\ge\; 2^{S}.
   $$

   Entropy is a **lower** bound on bond dimension. With $S\le3$ that says
   $\chi\ge8$ is not forced, and it says nothing whatever about how large
   $\chi$ could be. Bond dimension is the Schmidt rank, a count of
   nonzero singular values, and entropy is a weighted quantity that a
   long tail of tiny values barely moves. A cut whose spectrum is one
   large value plus $2^{n/2}$ vanishingly small ones can have entropy
   under $3$ bits while its exact Schmidt rank grows exponentially in
   $n$. Bounded entropy and bounded rank are simply different conditions.

   **So what does the efficiency come from?** Not from the entropy bound
   itself, but from the singular-value decay that stands behind it. The
   area-law results for gapped 1D local Hamiltonians establish that the
   discarded weight $\sum_{a>\chi}\sigma_a^2$ falls off fast enough that
   truncating each bond to

   $$
   \chi = \mathrm{poly}(n,\,1/\varepsilon)
   $$

   leaves a state within $\varepsilon$ of the true ground state. That is
   the actual theorem an MPS algorithm relies on, and it is a statement
   about the tail of the spectrum, not about the entropy.

   Given that, the efficiency follows mechanically. Applying one
   nearest-neighbour two-qubit gate to an MPS costs $O(\chi^3)$, so a
   layer over all $n$ sites costs $O(n\chi^3)$ and a polynomial $\chi$
   makes every step polynomial and the whole simulation polynomial. And because
   the error is exactly the discarded weight, it is a *measured* quantity
   rather than a hoped-for one: the algorithm can report how much it
   threw away and therefore how far from exact its answer is. A certified
   approximation with a computable error bar is a different kind of
   object from a heuristic that happens to work.

   **The wrong turn** is the natural one: $S\le3$, so $\chi\le2^3=8$, so
   MPS simulation is not merely efficient but constant-cost. Both steps
   are wrong. The inequality points the other way, and the conclusion
   would make exact MPS representation of these ground states far easier
   than it is. The correct statement keeps three things distinct: entropy
   is bounded (the area law), the exact Schmidt rank need not be, and the
   truncated bond dimension at fixed accuracy is polynomial (the theorem
   that actually does the work).

   </details>

## Summary

- An $n$-qubit amplitude tensor can be rewritten exactly as a chain of
  small tensors $A^{i_1}A^{i_2}\cdots A^{i_n}$ via repeated reshaping and
  SVD, one cut at a time; keeping every nonzero singular value makes the
  rewriting exact, not approximate.
- **Bond dimension** $\chi_k$ at a cut is the number of
  singular values kept there, which is exactly the Schmidt rank of that
  bipartition, verified directly for GHZ, $\chi=2$ at both cuts of a
  3-qubit chain, using this platform's own `partialTrace` and
  `eigenvaluesHermitian2x2`, and cross-checked by contracting the
  explicit `Matrix` tensors back into the original state.
- Bond dimension and entanglement entropy are related but distinct:
  rank counts terms, entropy weighs them (GHZ vs. the skewed
  $\sqrt{0.99}/\sqrt{0.01}$ state above).
- The **area law** (cited, not derived here, per Hastings 2007) bounds a
  gapped 1D ground state's entanglement entropy by a size-independent
  constant. That bound runs one way only: $S\leq\log_2\chi$ makes
  entropy a *lower* bound on bond dimension, so a bounded entropy caps
  nothing, and the exact Schmidt rank of such a state can still be full.
  What the area law delivers is fast singular-value decay, so an
  $\varepsilon$-accurate truncated MPS needs only
  $\chi=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)$, against $2^{n/2}$ for a
  volume-law state. That is the mechanism
  behind tensor networks being classical physics' primary tool for large
  1D (and, via PEPS/MERA, higher-dimensional) many-body simulation.

## Bond Dimension as a Computed Number

Bond dimension is now a computed number rather than a knob. What it is
not yet is a criterion: knowing $\chi=2$ for GHZ says nothing on its own
about where the line between classically easy and classically hard
actually falls, or about the entirely separate route to easiness that
does not involve entanglement at all.
