## Past the Picture, Into the Lattice

Surface Codes: A Conceptual Introduction earned its title honestly: it drew
a $3\times3$-vertex patch, named the two stabilizer types, and stopped
there, stating outright that a real construction was "out of scope." That
was the right call for an introduction sitting right after syndrome
measurement was first introduced. It is the wrong stopping point for a
course called Fault Tolerance Frontiers.

This lesson goes where that one didn't: an actual distance-3 surface-code
patch, with 13 named data qubits, 12 named stabilizer generators, every
one of their qubit memberships written down explicitly, a real
commutation check carried out on a specific pair sharing 2
qubits (not asserted by analogy), and both logical operators identified
by a direct verification that a picture alone cannot give. Nothing here
contradicts the conceptual introduction's picture (vertex stabilizers
are still $Z$-type, face stabilizers are still $X$-type, stabilizers are
still local). It just stops leaving the picture as a picture.

## An Explicit Distance-3 Patch

### Choosing a convention: the unrotated surface code

Two standard conventions build a distance-$d$ surface code. The
**rotated** code places $d^2$ data qubits on a plain $d\times d$ grid,
with stabilizers nestled in the gaps: more qubit-efficient, and the
layout real hardware experiments (including the Sycamore and Willow
chips) actually use. The **unrotated** code (the one the conceptual
introduction already gestured at, with qubits "on the edges of a 2D
grid" and separate vertex/face stabilizers) uses
$n=2d^2-2d+1$ data qubits for the same distance $d$: more qubits, but a
cleaner, more directly geometric match to that earlier lesson's own
language.

**This lesson uses the unrotated convention**, specifically so that
"vertex stabilizer = $Z$-type" and "face stabilizer = $X$-type" carry
over unchanged from the conceptual introduction, and so every claim below
can be checked against that lesson's own picture rather than a
relabeled one. The usual "roughly double" undersells the trade-off, so
here is the actual number:

below without ever reaching it. That distinction matters at small
distances and stops mattering at the distances a real machine would run:
by $d=11$ the unrotated convention is costing an extra hundred physical
qubits per logical qubit, which is why every hardware demonstration uses
the rotated layout and every pedagogical construction, including this
one, uses the unrotated one.

### An explicit distance-3 patch: coordinates, not just a picture

Lay out a $5\times5$ coordinate grid, $(x,y)$ for $x,y\in\{0,1,2,3,4\}$,
a $(2d-1)\times(2d-1)$ grid at $d=3$. Every site with $x+y$ **even**
holds a **data qubit**; every site with $x+y$ **odd** holds a
**stabilizer**. This is the same "qubits on edges, stabilizers on
vertices and faces" lattice the conceptual introduction drew, just given
real coordinates, and the coarser grid that makes it one is the grid
whose **vertices are the $Z$-stabilizer sites**: $x$ odd, $y$ even,
spacing 2, a $2\times3$ array of vertices at $x\in\{1,3\}$,
$y\in\{0,2,4\}$. Every midpoint of an edge of *that* grid is an even-sum
site, so **the data qubits are the ones on edges**, exactly as the
conceptual introduction said; its six vertices are the six
$Z$-stabilizers; and its two faces have centers $(2,1)$ and $(2,3)$,
which are precisely the two weight-4 bulk $X$-stabilizers. The other
four $X$-stabilizers, at $(0,1)$, $(0,3)$, $(4,1)$ and $(4,3)$, are
*half*-faces hanging off the grid's left and right sides, which is
exactly why they come out weight 3, and the six data qubits at
$x\in\{0,4\}$ are the matching dangling half-edges. Seven interior edge
midpoints plus those six half-edges is 13. Qubits on edges, stabilizers
on vertices and faces, with the boundary truncations appearing as
half-faces and half-edges rather than as exceptions to the rule.
Counting even-sum sites in a $5\times5$ grid
directly: 3 per even row $\times$ 3 even rows, plus 2 per odd row
$\times$ 2 odd rows, $=9+4=13$ data qubits, matching
$2d^2-2d+1=2(9)-6+1=13$. That is not a coincidence: the same counting
argument works for any $d$ (see "General $d$-dependence" below).

Label the 13 data qubits $q_1,\ldots,q_{13}$ in reading order:

$$
\begin{array}{ccccc}
q_1{=}(0,0) & & q_2{=}(2,0) & & q_3{=}(4,0)\\
& q_4{=}(1,1) & & q_5{=}(3,1) & \\
q_6{=}(0,2) & & q_7{=}(2,2) & & q_8{=}(4,2)\\
& q_9{=}(1,3) & & q_{10}{=}(3,3) & \\
q_{11}{=}(0,4) & & q_{12}{=}(2,4) & & q_{13}{=}(4,4)
\end{array}
$$

Each odd-sum site is a stabilizer touching the (up to 4) data qubits at
distance 1 along the grid axes: the "4 edge-qubits around a
vertex or face" rule the conceptual introduction stated, now applied at
real coordinates. **Every stabilizer's qubit membership, computed
directly from adjacency, no exceptions:**

| $X$-stabilizer (face) | site | qubits | weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| $x_A$ | $(0,1)$ | $q_4,q_1,q_6$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $x_B$ | $(2,1)$ | $q_4,q_5,q_2,q_7$ | 4 (bulk) |
| $x_C$ | $(4,1)$ | $q_5,q_3,q_8$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $x_D$ | $(0,3)$ | $q_9,q_6,q_{11}$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $x_E$ | $(2,3)$ | $q_9,q_{10},q_7,q_{12}$ | 4 (bulk) |
| $x_F$ | $(4,3)$ | $q_{10},q_8,q_{13}$ | 3 (boundary) |

| $Z$-stabilizer (vertex) | site | qubits | weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| $z_A$ | $(1,0)$ | $q_1,q_2,q_4$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $z_B$ | $(3,0)$ | $q_2,q_3,q_5$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $z_C$ | $(1,2)$ | $q_6,q_7,q_4,q_9$ | 4 (bulk) |
| $z_D$ | $(3,2)$ | $q_7,q_8,q_5,q_{10}$ | 4 (bulk) |
| $z_E$ | $(1,4)$ | $q_{11},q_{12},q_9$ | 3 (boundary) |
| $z_F$ | $(3,4)$ | $q_{12},q_{13},q_{10}$ | 3 (boundary) |

The $X$-stabilizers sit at columns $x\in\{0,2,4\}$: the two extreme
columns ($x=0,4$) each have only 3 in-bounds neighbors, giving the
weight-3 boundary stabilizers along the **left/right edges**; the middle
column ($x=2$) gives the two weight-4 bulk stabilizers. By the same
logic on rows, the $Z$-stabilizers' weight-3 members sit along the
**top/bottom edges**. Call left/right the **rough** boundary (where
face/$X$-stabilizers are the truncated ones) and top/bottom the
**smooth** boundary (where vertex/$Z$-stabilizers are truncated).
This is standard terminology, now tied to a coordinate fact.

### The commutation check, done on a real pair

Objective 1 asks for this check: pick a representative $X$-stabilizer
and $Z$-stabilizer and verify they commute. Take the two bulk generators
$x_B$ (qubits $q_4,q_5,q_2,q_7$) and $z_C$ (qubits $q_6,q_7,q_4,q_9$).
Their supports overlap at **exactly 2 qubits**, $q_4$ and $q_7$:

$$
x_B = X_4X_5X_2X_7, \qquad z_C = Z_6Z_7Z_4Z_9
$$

At $q_4$: $X$ and $Z$ anticommute (one sign flip). At $q_7$: $X$ and $Z$
anticommute again (a second sign flip). Two sign flips cancel:
$x_Bz_C=(-1)^2z_Cx_B=z_Cx_B$. This is the CSS Codes and the General
Stabilizer Formalism rule ($X^g,Z^h$ commute iff $g\cdot h\equiv0\bmod2$,
i.e. an even overlap) applied concretely: $g\cdot h=2\equiv0$. It is not
a coincidence specific to this one pair. Practice Question 2 below asks
for the geometric reason *every* $X$/$Z$ stabilizer pair in this lattice
shares an even number of qubits (0 or 2, never 1 or 3).

Two same-type generators always commute regardless of overlap (identical
Pauli at every shared qubit), so checking every $X$/$Z$ pair is the only
work needed. Doing so for all $6\times6=36$ pairs (each pair overlapping
in 0 or 2 qubits, by the same boundary-loop argument) confirms **all 12
generators commute**, so they generate a stabilizer group.

### Logical operators, verified by direct commutation

A logical operator must commute with every stabilizer while not itself
being a product of stabilizers. Try the horizontal string across the
**middle row** ($y=2$): $\bar X\equiv X_6X_7X_8$ (qubits $q_6,q_7,q_8$,
weight 3). Checking against every $Z$-stabilizer directly: $z_A,z_B$
(top boundary) share 0 qubits with $\{q_6,q_7,q_8\}$ (commute
trivially); $z_C$ shares $\{q_6,q_7\}$, 2 qubits (commutes); $z_D$
shares $\{q_7,q_8\}$, 2 qubits (commutes); $z_E,z_F$ (bottom boundary)
share 0 qubits (commute trivially). $\bar X$ commutes with every
generator. It is also not a product of the six $X$-generators alone
(the only way a pure-$X$ stabilizer-group element could arise): the
product of *all six* $X$-generators works out to $\{q_1,q_2,q_3,q_{11},q_{12},q_{13}\}$
(the top row and bottom row together, weight 6), confirming $\bar X$
(the middle row alone) is a different, nontrivial operator,
equivalent to top-row-alone or bottom-row-alone (each also weight 3,
differing from the middle row by a stabilizer) but not equivalent to the
identity.

$\bar X$ runs from $q_6$ (on the **left rough boundary**, $x=0$) through
the center $q_7$ to $q_8$ (on the **right rough boundary**, $x=4$):
a string connecting the two rough boundaries, the
"boundary-to-boundary string" the conceptual picture promised, now an
explicit weight-3 operator.

By the same check on the **middle column** ($x=2$): $\bar
Z\equiv Z_2Z_7Z_{12}$ (qubits $q_2,q_7,q_{12}$, weight 3) commutes with
every $X$-stabilizer by an identical argument, and runs from $q_2$ (top
**smooth boundary**, $y=0$) through $q_7$ to $q_{12}$ (bottom smooth
boundary, $y=4$).

$\bar X$ and $\bar Z$ share **a single qubit**, $q_7$: the patch's
center. One shared qubit means $X$ and $Z$ anticommute there, and
nowhere else to cancel it: $\bar X\bar Z=-\bar Z\bar X$, the
symplectic structure a single logical qubit's own Pauli operators must
have (the same check CSS Codes and the General Stabilizer Formalism ran
on the Steane code's $\bar X,\bar Z$).

### Why the distance is 3

**Distance** is the minimum weight of any logical (normalizer,
non-stabilizer) operator. $\bar X$ and $\bar Z$ above both have weight
3, so $d\leq3$. No weight-1 or weight-2 operator can be logical: a
weight-1 $X$ error on any single qubit anticommutes with at least one
$Z$-stabilizer (every data qubit touches at least one $Z$-generator,
including the corners, e.g. $q_1$ touches $z_A$ alone), so it is
*detected*, not an undetectable logical error; a similar check rules out
every weight-2 combination reaching from one rough (or smooth) boundary
to the other, since the $5\times5$ coordinate spacing forces at least 3
qubits to bridge $x=0$ to $x=4$ (or $y=0$ to $y=4$) along the lattice.
So $d=3$: the shortest boundary-to-boundary string *is* the
code distance, verified here by direct construction, with no appeal to
the general topological argument.

### General $d$-dependence

The same coordinate-counting argument (even-sum sites in a
$(2d-1)\times(2d-1)$ grid) generalizes cleanly:

| Quantity | Formula | at $d=3$ |
|---|---|---|
| Data qubits | $2d^2-2d+1$ | $13$ |
| $X$-stabilizers | $d(d-1)$ | $6$ |
| $Z$-stabilizers | $d(d-1)$ | $6$ |
| Independent generators | $2d(d-1)=n-1$ | $12$ |
| Logical qubits | $1$ | $1$ |
| Code distance | $d$ | $3$ |

$13-12=1$: exactly one logical qubit, for any $d$, the same $n-(n-k)=k$
counting Stabilizer Formalism Basics and CSS Codes and the General
Stabilizer Formalism both used, verified again here on a real 2D
lattice.

## Why Locality Is the Whole Point

The conceptual introduction already named the punchline: local
stabilizers matter because real hardware has local connectivity. This
lesson's explicit lattice shows why that isn't a vague
gesture. Every one of the 12 stabilizers built above touches at most 4
data qubits, and every qubit a given stabilizer touches sits at
grid-distance 1 from that stabilizer's own site (the part a picture
alone doesn't prove; the coordinates do). A two-qubit gate implementing part of $x_B$'s
measurement, say between the ancilla at $(2,1)$ and data qubit $q_4$ at
$(1,1)$, is a gate between *physically adjacent* sites on this exact
coordinate grid, not an abstract index pair.

That is the layout Quantum Hardware's Physical Qubit
Platforms course describes for superconducting qubits: a 2D chip with
fixed nearest-neighbor couplers, no long-range wiring between distant
qubits. A superconducting processor's qubits are arranged, and coupled,
the way this lattice needs its ancillas coupled to their 4 (or fewer, at
a boundary) neighbors, which is not a coincidence: the layouts were
chosen for each other. A code whose stabilizers needed qubits at arbitrary distance
(the Shor code's 9-qubit stabilizers, or worse, would need this on a
chip with no long-range coupling) could not be measured on this
hardware without expensive, error-prone SWAP chains to first bring
distant qubits together. The surface code's stabilizers never need
that: this is the concrete, geometric reason (not a hand-wave about
"scalability") that it became the dominant near-term fault-tolerance
architecture candidate. Google's 2024 Willow demonstration (cited in the
conceptual introduction) ran distance-3, -5, and -7 patches built from
this same local-checking pattern on this kind of chip, though
Willow itself uses the more qubit-efficient rotated layout described
above, where this lesson constructs the unrotated one.

## Commutation, Checked Qubit by Qubit

**Confirm the boundary-weight claim directly: show $z_A$ (the
$Z$-stabilizer at site $(1,0)$) has weight 3, and identify which
qubit "would" complete it to weight 4 if the lattice extended further.**

$z_A$ sits at $(1,0)$. Its four candidate neighbors at grid-distance 1
are $(0,0)=q_1$, $(2,0)=q_2$, $(1,-1)$, and $(1,1)=q_4$. The site
$(1,-1)$ has $y=-1$, outside the $[0,4]$ range the $d=3$ patch occupies:
there is no qubit there. So $z_A=Z_1Z_2Z_4$, weight 3, the
"one fewer than 4" pattern the conceptual introduction's own picture
allowed for ("4, or fewer at boundaries"). Extending the lattice one
more row upward (a $d=4$-ish patch in that direction) would add a data
qubit at $(1,-1)$ and promote $z_A$ to weight 4, concretely showing
*why* boundary stabilizers are weight-3 here and not a fundamentally
different kind of object: they are ordinary weight-4 vertex stabilizers
with one neighbor simply absent because the patch ends there.

## Weight-3 Boundaries and Other Traps

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming all stabilizers have the same weight.** This patch's 8
boundary generators are weight 3, not weight 4: a geometric
consequence of the patch ending somewhere, and nothing to do with
approximation. Only the 4 bulk generators ($x_B,x_E,z_C,z_D$) reach the
full weight-4 the conceptual introduction's "always 4 qubits" language
described in the interior.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking the logical operator has to be the exact string drawn in one
picture.** $\bar X=X_6X_7X_8$ (middle row) and $X_1X_2X_3$ (top row) are
*the same logical operator*: they differ by the product of
$x_A,x_B,x_C$, a stabilizer element, so they act identically on the
codespace. Distance asks for the *minimum* weight among all such
equivalent representatives, not the weight of any single one you happen
to draw.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing "rough" and "smooth" boundary labels with a universal
convention.** Different papers assign these labels to opposite pairs of
edges (and correspondingly swap which logical operator runs between
them). This lesson fixed one explicit, internally consistent
assignment (rough = left/right, where $X$-stabilizers truncate) and
verified $\bar X$ actually runs between those two edges by direct
computation. What must hold in any consistent convention is the
computed fact, not the label.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify by hand that $x_E$ (qubits $q_9,q_{10},q_7,q_{12}$) and $z_D$
   (qubits $q_7,q_8,q_5,q_{10}$) share 2 qubits, name them, and
   confirm they commute using the same two-sign-flips argument as the
   $x_B$/$z_C$ check above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The shared qubits are $q_7$ and $q_{10}$, and the pair commutes.

   Intersect the two supports directly. $x_E$ acts on
   $\{q_9,q_{10},q_7,q_{12}\}$ and $z_D$ acts on $\{q_7,q_8,q_5,q_{10}\}$.
   Going through $x_E$'s list one at a time: $q_9$ is absent from $z_D$,
   $q_{10}$ is present, $q_7$ is present, $q_{12}$ is absent. Two
   qubits in common.

   $$
   x_E = X_9X_{10}X_7X_{12}, \qquad z_D = Z_7Z_8Z_5Z_{10}
   $$

   At $q_7$ the operators are $X$ and $Z$, which anticommute: one sign
   flip. At $q_{10}$, the same: a second sign flip. Everywhere else at
   least one of the two acts as the identity, and identity commutes with
   anything. So $x_Ez_D=(-1)^2z_Dx_E=z_Dx_E$.

   The coordinates say why it had to be 2. $x_E$ sits at $(2,3)$ and
   $z_D$ at $(3,2)$, so the two sites are diagonal neighbours, one step
   apart in each direction. The sites at grid-distance 1 from *both* are
   then $(2,2)=q_7$ and $(3,3)=q_{10}$, and there are always exactly two
   of those for a diagonal pair. That is the same relationship $x_B$
   at $(2,1)$ and $z_C$ at $(1,2)$ have, which is why the two checks come
   out identically. Question 2 turns this observation into the general
   argument.

   </details>

2. The lesson's commutation check found $x_B$/$z_C$ sharing 2 qubits.
   Using the fact that an $X$-stabilizer's qubits are the edges
   bounding one face while a $Z$-stabilizer's qubits are the
   edges meeting at one vertex, explain why *any* face and *any* vertex
   in this lattice must share an even number of edges (0 or 2), never an
   odd number.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the edges of a face form a closed loop, and a closed loop
   passing through a vertex uses exactly two edges at that vertex: one to
   arrive, one to leave. A vertex the loop misses entirely contributes
   zero. There is no way for a loop to touch a vertex an odd number of
   times, so the overlap is 0 or 2 and never 1 or 3.

   That is the argument in words. The coordinate version proves the same
   thing and, unlike the loop picture, handles the truncated boundary
   generators without a special case, which is where the loop picture
   needs care.

   An $X$-stabilizer sits at a site $(a,b)$ with $a$ even and $b$ odd. A
   $Z$-stabilizer sits at $(c,e)$ with $c$ odd and $e$ even. So
   $a-c$ is odd and $b-e$ is odd, always. A data qubit shared by the two
   must sit at grid-distance 1 from each of them, which is only possible
   if the two sites are themselves at $L^1$ distance $2$ or less. Since
   both coordinate differences are odd, they cannot be $0$, and the only
   surviving case is $|a-c|=|b-e|=1$: the two sites are diagonal
   neighbours. Every other placement puts them at $L^1$ distance $4$ or
   more, with no common neighbour and overlap $0$.

   When they are diagonal neighbours, the common neighbours are exactly
   the two sites $(a,e)$ and $(c,b)$. Both have even coordinate sums
   (even $+$ even, and odd $+$ odd), so both are data-qubit sites rather
   than stabilizer sites, and there are precisely two of them. Overlap
   $2$.

   **Why the boundary does not spoil it,** which is the part worth
   checking rather than assuming. The weight-3 generators exist because
   some candidate neighbours fall outside $[0,4]$. But look at what the
   two common neighbours are made of: $(a,e)$ takes its $x$ from an
   in-bounds face and its $y$ from an in-bounds vertex, and $(c,b)$ does
   the reverse. Both coordinates are therefore already in range. A
   truncation can only ever delete a site that no in-bounds $X$/$Z$ pair
   shares in the first place. Concretely, $x_A$ at $(0,1)$ is missing its
   neighbour at $(-1,1)$; the only vertices that could have shared that
   qubit sit at $(-1,0)$ and $(-1,2)$, and neither is in the patch. The
   even overlap survives at the boundary because the deleted edges are
   the ones joining two deleted vertices.

   **The wrong turn** is to reason from weights: "$x_B$ has weight 4 and
   $z_C$ has weight 4, and the patch is small, so of course they overlap
   somewhere." Overlap size has nothing to do with weight. $x_A$ and
   $z_A$ are both weight 3 and share 2 qubits; $x_A$ and $z_B$ are also
   both weight 3 and share 0. What decides it is entirely the relative
   position of the two sites, and the parity of the coordinates is what
   forces that position into one of two cases.

   This is the geometric content of the CSS condition. The check matrices
   $H_X$ and $H_Z$ must satisfy $H_XH_Z^{\mathsf T}=0 \bmod 2$, and on
   this lattice that algebraic requirement is being met by a fact about
   loops and vertices in a plane. Any lattice, in any dimension, whose
   $X$ checks are cycles and whose $Z$ checks are vertex stars gets the
   commutation for free by the same argument, which is why the
   construction generalizes to codes that look nothing like this one.

   </details>

3. For a distance-5 patch (same convention), compute the exact number of
   data qubits, $X$-stabilizers, $Z$-stabilizers, and independent
   generators, and confirm the generator count is one less than
   the qubit count.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   41 data qubits, 20 $X$-stabilizers, 20 $Z$-stabilizers, 40
   independent generators, and $41-40=1$.

   From the formulas:

   $$
   n = 2d^2-2d+1 = 2(25)-10+1 = 41, \qquad d(d-1) = 5\cdot4 = 20 \ \text{of each type}.
   $$

   Worth re-deriving from the grid rather than trusting the formula,
   since the grid is what the formula summarizes. A distance-5 patch
   occupies a $(2d-1)\times(2d-1)=9\times9$ coordinate grid, 81 sites
   total, with data qubits on the even-sum sites.

   Even rows $y\in\{0,2,4,6,8\}$, five of them, each carrying data qubits
   at even $x\in\{0,2,4,6,8\}$: $5\times5=25$.
   Odd rows $y\in\{1,3,5,7\}$, four of them, each carrying data qubits at
   odd $x\in\{1,3,5,7\}$: $4\times4=16$.
   Total $25+16=41$.

   $X$-stabilizers sit at even $x$, odd $y$: $5\times4=20$.
   $Z$-stabilizers sit at odd $x$, even $y$: $4\times5=20$.
   And $41+20+20=81$, the whole grid accounted for, which is the check
   that no site was double-counted or dropped.

   So $n-k=40$ independent generators encode $k=41-40=1$ logical qubit,
   the same single logical qubit the $d=3$ patch carries. Distance bought
   nothing in capacity; it bought only protection. Going from $d=3$ to
   $d=5$ costs $41-13=28$ extra physical qubits for the same one logical
   qubit, which is the shape of every fault-tolerance resource estimate
   in this course.

   **The claim that needs checking, not asserting,** is that all 40
   generators are independent. They are, and the reason is the open
   boundary. On a torus the product of *all* $X$-stabilizers is the
   identity, one relation among the generators, and the same for $Z$;
   two relations means two fewer independent generators and hence $k=2$,
   the toric code's two logical qubits. Here that product is not the
   identity. At $d=3$ the lesson computes it explicitly and gets
   $X$ acting on $\{q_1,q_2,q_3,q_{11},q_{12},q_{13}\}$, the top and
   bottom rows: every bulk qubit appears in exactly two $X$-generators
   and cancels, while the top and bottom row qubits appear in exactly one
   and survive. Cutting the surface open destroys the relation, and $k$
   drops from 2 to 1.

   **The wrong turn** is to compute $2d(d-1)$ generators, notice it
   equals $n-1$, and conclude that $k=1$ is a numerical coincidence of
   this convention. It is not a coincidence and it is not about the
   numbers. $k=1$ is a topological fact about a disc with two rough and
   two smooth boundary segments; the algebra is obliged to agree with it,
   and $2d^2-2d+1-2d(d-1)=1$ identically in $d$ is what that obligation
   looks like when written as a polynomial.

   </details>

4. Corner qubit $q_1=(0,0)$ is touched by only one $X$-stabilizer
   ($x_A$) and one $Z$-stabilizer ($z_A$). Explain why a single-qubit
   $Y$ error on $q_1$ (both an $X$ and a $Z$ error at once) is still
   detected, even though $q_1$ has the lowest stabilizer-degree of any
   qubit in the patch.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because detection needs only one anticommuting generator, and $q_1$
   has two, one of each type. That is the minimum that works, and it is
   enough.

   Write $Y_1 = iX_1Z_1$ and take the two halves separately, which is
   legitimate here because the code is CSS and the two stabilizer types
   never mix.

   The $X_1$ half anticommutes with $Z$-type generators supported on
   $q_1$. There is one, $z_A=Z_1Z_2Z_4$, so $z_A$ fires.
   The $Z_1$ half anticommutes with $X$-type generators supported on
   $q_1$. There is one, $x_A=X_4X_1X_6$, so $x_A$ fires.

   The syndrome of $Y_1$ is therefore both $z_A$ and $x_A$ flipped, and
   it is distinct from the syndromes of the two errors taken singly:
   $X_1$ gives $\{z_A\}$ alone and $Z_1$ gives $\{x_A\}$ alone. A corner
   qubit is not merely detected, it is unambiguously identified.

   **The quantity that matters is not the degree, it is the type
   coverage.** Suppose some data qubit touched two generators and both
   were $Z$-type. Then $Z$ on that qubit would commute with every
   generator in the group, which makes it an element of the normalizer.
   Being weight 1 it cannot be a product of stabilizers, so it would be a
   weight-1 logical operator and the code distance would collapse to 1,
   whatever the lattice looked like. So the real requirement the lattice
   has to satisfy is that **every data qubit is touched by at least one
   $X$-generator and at least one $Z$-generator**, and the coordinate
   grid delivers exactly that: any even-sum site has an odd-$x$ neighbour
   (a $Z$ site) and an odd-$y$ neighbour (an $X$ site) still in bounds,
   corners included. $q_1$ sits at the floor of that requirement with
   nothing to spare, which is a fair thing to find uncomfortable, but the
   floor is where the requirement is met, not where it fails.

   **The wrong turn** is to read degree as a measure of protection, on
   the analogy that more checks watching a qubit means a safer qubit.
   Protection in a stabilizer code is the code distance, a global
   property of the shortest logical operator, and the lesson's
   construction already fixed it at 3 for every qubit at once. What a
   qubit's degree actually controls is how *localized* its syndrome is,
   which is a decoder's concern rather than a detection concern. A
   low-degree qubit produces a sparse syndrome, and a sparse syndrome is
   the harder one to trace back to a unique chain of errors. That is
   precisely why the next lesson's decoder treats the boundary as a place
   error chains can terminate: a single fired stabilizer near an edge is
   not a paradox, it is a chain whose other end ran off the patch.

   One honest caveat to carry into that lesson. Treating $Y_1$ as an
   independent $X$ error and $Z$ error is exactly right for detection and
   slightly wrong for decoding. Real hardware produces $Y$ errors as
   correlated events, so the $X$ and $Z$ syndromes are not statistically
   independent, and a decoder that solves the two matching problems
   separately is throwing away that correlation. Correlated decoders that
   keep it achieve a measurably higher threshold, at the cost of a harder
   inference problem.

   </details>

## Summary

- This lesson chose the **unrotated** surface-code convention
  ($n=2d^2-2d+1$ data qubits) specifically to extend the conceptual
  introduction's own vertex/face language without relabeling it.
- A distance-3 patch was built explicitly: 13 data qubits, 6
  $X$-stabilizers (face) and 6 $Z$-stabilizers (vertex), every one's
  qubit membership computed from real coordinates.
- A representative bulk $X$/$Z$ pair ($x_B$, $z_C$) was verified to
  share 2 qubits and therefore commute, the CSS commutation
  rule applied concretely; the same even-overlap property holds for
  every stabilizer pair in the lattice.
- Logical $\bar X$ and $\bar Z$ are weight-3 strings running between
  opposite boundaries (rough for $\bar X$, smooth for $\bar Z$),
  sharing only the center qubit $q_7$ and anticommuting there, giving
  code distance $d=3$, verified by direct construction.
- Local, small-weight stabilizers on a 2D coordinate grid are directly
  implementable on hardware with only nearest-neighbor connectivity
  (a superconducting chip's fixed nearest-neighbor couplers, for
  instance): the concrete engineering reason, not just a mathematical
  one, for the surface code's dominance.

## One Error at a Time Does Not Scale

This lesson traced one error at a time by hand. A running device sees
the syndromes of many simultaneous errors, all at once, every
microsecond, with no label saying which flipped stabilizer belongs to
which error.
