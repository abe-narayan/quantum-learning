## Why Transversality Runs Out

The fastest route to a fault-tolerant logical gate is a **transversal**
one: apply the identical physical gate to each corresponding physical
qubit of one or more code blocks, so that a single physical fault
touches only one physical qubit of the operation and can never spread
uncontrollably across it. Surface Codes in Depth already used
this trick for *single*-qubit logical gates within *one* patch: the
logical $X_L$ and $Z_L$ operators are nothing but a physical Pauli
string, applied qubit-by-qubit along a path connecting two opposite
boundaries of that same patch, a transversal logical operation in the
sense that it acts identically, physical qubit by physical qubit, and
a single physical error still only ever hits the string in one place.

A logical **two**-qubit gate between **two separate** patches is a
different problem in kind. The textbook reason transversal *two*-block
gates work at all (e.g. a transversal CNOT between two blocks of the
same CSS code) is that both blocks share the exact same stabilizer
index structure: conjugating physical qubit $i$ of block 1 and physical
qubit $i$ of block 2 through a CNOT sends an $X$-type stabilizer on some
qubit set $S$ of block 1 to that same stabilizer on block 1 **times**
the identical-looking operator on set $S$ of block 2, which is only a
valid stabilizer of the joint system because block 2 independently has
that same operator on set $S$ in its own stabilizer group. Two identical
surface-code patches *do* satisfy that algebraic condition: index the
second patch the same way as the first and the generators line up
qubit-for-qubit, so a transversal CNOT between them is a perfectly valid
logical CNOT on paper. The obstruction is not algebraic. It is that the
pairing the algebra asks for cannot be wired on a planar chip: two
patches occupying disjoint regions of a chip have, at
best, only their **touching boundary** qubits physically adjacent to
each other. Every interior data qubit of patch 1 has no nearby
partner qubit in patch 2 for a two-qubit gate to act on. A "transversal"
operation, by definition, needs a partner for *every* qubit across the
whole patch, not just the handful sitting at the seam, so a *local*
transversal two-patch gate is not physically realizable with
nearest-neighbor coupling on a 2D layout, however well the stabilizer
algebra cooperates. Fault-tolerant two-qubit logical gates between separate
surface-code patches need a mechanism that only ever touches that seam.
That mechanism is **lattice surgery**.

## Merge, Split, and the CNOT They Build

### The convention this lesson reuses

Recall the lattice: physical qubits sit on the edges of a 2D grid, a
**vertex stabilizer** is the $Z$-type product over the (up to 4) qubits
touching a vertex, and a **face stabilizer** is the $X$-type product
over the (up to 4) qubits bordering a face. A finite patch has two kinds
of boundary. Where the plaquette structure is cut short so that a face
stabilizer near the edge drops to weight 2, the edge is called
**rough**; the logical $X_L$ operator is a string of physical $X$'s
running between a patch's two rough boundaries. Where the vertex
structure is cut short so that a vertex stabilizer near the edge drops
to weight 2, the edge is called **smooth**; the logical $Z_L$ operator
is a string of physical $Z$'s running between a patch's two smooth
boundaries. Take patch $L_1$ and patch $L_2$ to be rectangular, each
with rough boundaries on its left and right edges and smooth boundaries
on top and bottom, the same compass assignment Surface Codes in Depth
made. Place them side by side on the chip so that $L_1$'s right
(rough) edge faces $L_2$'s left (rough) edge across a small physical
gap. $X_{L_1}$ is then a horizontal string spanning $L_1$'s full width;
$X_{L_2}$ spans $L_2$'s width the same way; $Z_{L_1}$ and $Z_{L_2}$ are
each vertical strings running top-to-bottom through their own patch.

<Callout type="note">
One naming difference from the literature is worth getting out of the
way, because it is the single most common source of confusion in this
corner of the subject. Surface Codes in Depth put $Z$-type stabilizers
on vertices and $X$-type on faces; much of the published work
(Kitaev's original toric code, and Horsman et al. on surgery) runs the
dual assignment, $X$ on vertices and $Z$ on faces. Nothing physical
changes, but every $X$ and $Z$ in the boundary story swaps, so those
papers describe the merge worked below as a *smooth* merge measuring
$Z\otimes Z$. What is convention-independent is the rule, not the
letters: **rough** is wherever the face stabilizers are the truncated
ones, and the logical operator of *face* type terminates there;
**smooth** is wherever the vertex stabilizers are truncated, and the
logical operator of *vertex* type terminates there. In this course's
assignment that reads: $X_L$ ends on rough, $Z_L$ ends on smooth. Which
compass edges carry which label is then a per-diagram choice, as
Surface Codes in Depth's own Common Mistakes box warned.
</Callout>

### MERGE: measuring a joint stabilizer along the boundary

To merge $L_1$ and $L_2$, fill the gap column between them. That column
gets a new line of **vertex** ($Z$-type) stabilizer sites running down
the seam, one opposite each row of the two patches, and a **bridge
qubit** on each short vertical edge between two consecutive seam
vertices. Those seam generators did not exist before: the gap column had
no stabilizer sites at all. Each one now touches the seam-facing data
qubit on $L_1$'s side and the one on $L_2$'s side, plus the bridge
qubits immediately above and below it, so it comes out weight 4 in the
middle of the seam and weight 3 at the seam's two ends, where there is a
bridge qubit on only one side. A seam of $b$ bridge qubits therefore
carries $b+1$ seam generators. This is
the same operation Syndrome Measurement and the Recovery Map already
justified, **measure a stabilizer, project onto the corresponding
eigenspace**, applied here to a stabilizer that spans two formerly
separate code blocks rather than sitting entirely inside one. Before the
merge, the two-patch system encodes 2 logical qubits (a 4-dimensional
code space, over and above the enormous physical Hilbert space): $X_{L_1}$,
$Z_{L_1}$, $X_{L_2}$, $Z_{L_2}$ are all independent logical operators.
The merge does *two* things at once, and the count only comes out right
if both are on the ledger. Write $b$ for the number of bridge qubits
added in the gap (two in the sketch above, $d$ of them for a
distance-$d$ seam). Those $b$ new physical qubits by themselves would
*raise* the number of logical qubits by $b$; the new independent seam
generators then lower it by one each. Starting from $n$ qubits with $m$
independent generators and $k=n-m=2$, the merged code has

$$
k_{\text{merged}} = (n+b) - (m + b + 1) = n - m - 1 = 1,
$$

so the seam has to supply exactly $b+1$ independent new generators to
cost one logical qubit. "Each new generator halves the code space" is
true only with the bridge qubits held fixed, which is precisely what a
merge does not do. Net effect: $b$ new qubits, $b+1$ new independent
generators, one logical qubit destroyed.
Once the whole seam is bridged, the two patches have become one larger
rectangular patch with **one** pair of logical operators left over:

- $Z_L^{\text{merged}}$: any top-to-bottom vertical string in the new,
  wider patch is an equally valid representative (they are all related
  by multiplying by vertex stabilizers), which forces
  $Z_{L_1}=Z_{L_2}$ as operators of the merged code: merging
  *identifies* the two patches' $Z$ logical operators with each other.
  Their product $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$ therefore stops being a logical
  operator and becomes an ordinary element of the stabilizer group,
  which is precisely what "it has been measured" means.
- $X_L^{\text{merged}}$: a left-to-right string spanning the full merged
  width, which is $L_1$'s original horizontal string through $L_1$ times
  $L_2$'s original horizontal string through $L_2$, i.e.
  $X_L^{\text{merged}} = X_{L_1}X_{L_2}$, up to the stabilizer group.
  Neither half survives on its own: $X_{L_1}$ alone now ends in the
  merged patch's bulk, where the seam's new vertex stabilizer detects it.

The outcome is not merely *some* joint parity, and the seam generators
say which one. Multiply all $b+1$ of them together. Every bridge qubit
sits in exactly two of them and cancels, leaving $Z$ on $L_1$'s
seam-side column times $Z$ on $L_2$'s seam-side column, and each of
those columns is one of that patch's own valid $Z_L$ representatives.
So the product of the seam measurement outcomes *is* the eigenvalue of
$Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$, derived rather than asserted.

<InsightBlock>
  The very act of measuring the new seam stabilizers is a projective
  measurement of $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$: its outcome is the product of the two
  eigenvalues the merge just fixed, while each patch's individual
  $Z_{L_1}$ and $Z_{L_2}$ eigenvalues are generically disturbed, just
  as measuring $Z\otimes Z$ on two ordinary qubits fixes their product
  without leaving either qubit's own $Z$ well-defined. Bringing two
  patches into contact and turning on new local checks along the seam is
  nothing but ordinary syndrome extraction, run once across a boundary
  instead of inside a single patch.
</InsightBlock>

### SPLIT: reintroducing the individual boundary

To split the merged patch back into two logical qubits, stop measuring
the seam's joint vertex stabilizers and restore the truncated face
($X$-type) generators each patch used to have at its own rough boundary.
Operationally that is one instruction: measure each bridge qubit
individually **in the $X$ basis** and discard it. An $X$ on a bridge
qubit anticommutes with every seam vertex generator that contains it, so
those drop out of the group, while multiplying each grown boundary face
by that same $X$ shrinks it back to the weight it had before the merge.
The basis matters: measuring the bridge qubits in $Z$ instead would
commute with the seam generators and leave the patches fused. The
mechanism is *subtraction*, not addition. Adding a
generator can only ever decrease $k$, never increase it, and no element
of the stabilizer group can anticommute with a logical operator at all:
logical operators are by definition elements of the group's
**normalizer**, so they commute with every stabilizer. What the split
does is run the merge's ledger backwards: it removes the $b+1$ seam
generators and discards the $b$ bridge qubits, and
$(n+b-b) - (m+b+1-(b+1)) = n - m = 2$ restores the second logical
degree of freedom. The seam measurement outcomes are not thrown away
either: they are the classical bits that fix which Pauli correction the
split needs afterwards. The two new individual logical operators $X_{L_1}'$,
$X_{L_2}'$ satisfy $X_{L_1}'X_{L_2}'=X_L^{\text{merged}}$, the operator the
merge left intact, but each carries its own fresh, generically random
bit from the split measurement itself, the same structure as
teleportation, where Bob's corrected state is only recovered *after*
combining a random local outcome with a previously-fixed joint one.

### From one ZZ measurement to a full logical CNOT

A single merge-then-split gives one logical two-qubit **measurement**
($Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$, or $X_{L_1}X_{L_2}$ using a smooth-boundary merge
instead), not yet a unitary gate. The standard construction assembles a
full logical CNOT (control patch $C$, target patch $T$) from two such
measurements and a disposable ancilla patch $A$, structurally identical
to Rigorous Teleportation and Superdense Coding's gate-teleportation
gadget one level up:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="|+⟩ is the state whose own Z-value is maximally undetermined, which is exactly what makes the next step's parity outcome carry no information about the control. Prepare A in |0⟩ instead and measuring Z_C Z_A would just be measuring Z_C, collapsing the control patch on the spot.">
    Prepare ancilla $A$ in $|+\rangle_L$ (an $X_A=+1$ eigenstate).
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A ZZ merge is the local stabilizer-measurement mechanism already justified above, applied here between the control patch and the ancilla.">
    Merge $C$ and $A$ along their rough boundaries, measuring
    $Z_CZ_A$ (outcome $m_1$), then split them apart again.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="An XX merge along the smooth boundary is the same mechanism with the complementary stabilizer type, now coupling the ancilla to the target. The two merges use perpendicular pairs of edges, which is why the ancilla has to be placed where it can reach both patches on the correct sides.">
    Merge $A$ and $T$ along their smooth boundaries, measuring
    $X_AX_T$ (outcome $m_2$), then split them apart again.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Z, not X. Z_A anticommutes with the X_A X_T just measured, so it breaks the ancilla-target correlation and returns a fresh random bit. Measuring X_A instead would commute with X_A X_T, which would fix X_T through the correlation and collapse the target: no Pauli correction can repair that, and the protocol stops being a CNOT.">
    Measure $A$ directly, in the $Z$ basis (a single-patch logical
    measurement, no further merge needed), outcome $m_3$.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Propagating the three measurement outcomes through the circuit gives a deterministic correction, the same way teleportation's X^m1 Z^m0 table was derived, just from three classical bits instead of two.">
    Apply $Z_C$ if $m_2=-1$, and $X_T$ if $m_1m_3=-1$.
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Written out over all eight branches, with the signs listed in the order
$\big(m_1,m_2,m_3\big)$:

| $(m_1,m_2,m_3)$ | correction | $(m_1,m_2,m_3)$ | correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| $(+,+,+)$ | $I$ | $(-,+,+)$ | $X_T$ |
| $(+,+,-)$ | $X_T$ | $(-,+,-)$ | $I$ |
| $(+,-,+)$ | $Z_C$ | $(-,-,+)$ | $Z_CX_T$ |
| $(+,-,-)$ | $Z_CX_T$ | $(-,-,-)$ | $Z_C$ |

Two structural features are worth reading off the table rather than
memorizing it. The control only ever needs a $Z$, and the target only
ever needs an $X$, which is the same asymmetry ordinary
gate teleportation has and the reason the corrections stay Pauli rather
than growing into something harder to apply. And $m_1$ and $m_3$ appear
only through their product: neither bit means anything on its own,
because each is individually a fair coin. That is the signature of a
measurement-based gate as opposed to a readout, and it is the same
structure teleportation's own correction table has.

After the correction, $(C,T)$ carries $\text{CNOT}_{C\to T}$ applied to
whatever states they held going in, and $A$ is fully consumed. Every
step is either a merge/split (local stabilizer measurements along one
seam) or a single-patch measurement: **no step ever asks for a
transversal gate reaching all the way across two disjoint patches.**
This is the same teleportation-based-gate idea Rigorous Teleportation
already made precise: a joint measurement plus classically-controlled
correction stands in for a gate that has no direct, local
implementation, now running on entire code patches rather than bare
qubits.

## The Cost the Mechanism Hides

<InsightBlock>
  Lattice surgery turns "how do two logical qubits interact" and "how
  does the code detect an error" into the same hardware primitive, a
  joint stabilizer measurement along a shared boundary, so a real
  two-qubit gate needs no new mechanism at all.
</InsightBlock>

Lattice surgery is not a theoretical curiosity standing in for
something better: it is the real, dominant mechanism essentially
every serious 2D-superconducting (and comparable) surface-code
fault-tolerant architecture proposal actually uses for logical two-qubit
gates. The reason ties directly back to Surface Codes in Depth's own
"why locality matters" point: a merge or split is built entirely from
the same local, nearest-neighbor stabilizer measurements the code
already has to perform continuously just to correct errors. It
introduces no new gate type, no long-range coupling, and no requirement
that hardware ever connect a qubit deep inside one patch to a qubit deep
inside another. "How do two logical qubits interact" and "how does the
code detect an error" turn out to be the same hardware primitive,
applied along a different boundary, which is why lattice
surgery, not some exotic inter-patch gate, is what real roadmaps build
around.

<ResearchConnection
  title="Lattice surgery, as originally proposed"
  source="Horsman, Fowler, Devitt & Van Meter, New Journal of Physics 14, 123011 (2012) (arXiv:1111.4022)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.4022"
>
  The paper that introduced merge and split as a replacement for
  braiding-based logical gates on planar surface codes, with the full
  CNOT protocol and its Pauli-correction table worked out. Its central
  argument is a resource one: lattice surgery reaches the same logical
  operations as defect braiding in roughly half the physical qubits,
  which is why the planar-patch-plus-surgery picture rather than the
  braided-defect picture is what later resource estimates assume.
</ResearchConnection>

### What surgery costs, which the mechanism above hides

Nothing in the merge/split description above mentions time or space, and
both are real:

- **A merge takes $d$ rounds, not one.** A single round of the new seam
  stabilizers is not fault-tolerant: a measurement error on a seam
  stabilizer is indistinguishable from a data error, so the seam must be
  measured for $O(d)$ consecutive rounds and the outcome decided by
  decoding the resulting spacetime syndrome history. A logical CNOT
  built from two merge/split pairs therefore costs on the order of $d$
  surface-code cycles, and $d$ grows with the target logical error rate.
  This is why fault-tolerant circuit costs are quoted in *spacetime
  volume* (qubit-rounds), not qubit count alone.
- **The two patches have to be adjacent.** Merging requires a shared
  boundary. Two logical qubits at opposite ends of a chip cannot merge
  until one is moved next to the other, and moving a patch is itself a
  sequence of surgeries through intervening routing space. Real
  architectures therefore reserve a substantial fraction of the chip as
  empty ancilla region for exactly this, and a compiler's choice of which
  logical qubits sit near which becomes a real optimization problem
  rather than a detail.

Neither cost changes the conclusion that surgery is the right mechanism.
Both are why "logical two-qubit gates are free once you have patches" is
the wrong summary of this lesson.

## The Merge's Back-Action, in Two Cases

**Confirm the ZZ-merge's back-action concretely, in two contrasting
cases.**

*Case 1: both patches already in $Z$ eigenstates.* Prepare $L_1$ in
$|0\rangle_L$ ($Z_{L_1}=+1$) and $L_2$ in $|1\rangle_L$ ($Z_{L_2}=-1$).
Merging measures $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$, but this operator's value is already
fixed: $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}=(+1)(-1)=-1$ with certainty, before the merge
even happens. The merge reports $m=-1$ every time and disturbs nothing,
which is what measuring an operator the state is already an eigenstate
of has to do.

*Case 2: one patch in a superposition.* Prepare $L_1$ in $|+\rangle_L$
(equal superposition of $Z_{L_1}=\pm1$) and $L_2$ in $|0\rangle_L$
($Z_{L_2}=+1$). Writing this out, $|+\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}
=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(|0\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}+|1\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}\big)$.
Measuring $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$ projects onto one of its two terms with
50/50 probability: outcome $m=+1$ leaves $|0\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}$,
outcome $m=-1$ leaves $|1\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}$. Either way, $L_1$
has been forced out of its original $X_{L_1}$-definite superposition and
into a definite $Z_{L_1}$ eigenstate: the back-action a joint
$ZZ$ measurement has on a state that was not already one of its
eigenstates, and the disturbance the derivation above
described in the abstract, now checked by hand on the smallest case
that shows it.

## Boundary Types and Where They Trip You

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking single-patch transversal logical operators contradict this
lesson's claim that transversal gates don't work here.** They don't:
a single patch's $X_L$/$Z_L$ string is a transversal *single-qubit*
operation on *one* code block. The claim this lesson makes is narrower
and different: there is no *locally implementable* transversal
*two*-qubit gate *between two separate patches*, because away from the
shared boundary their physical qubits are not adjacent, whatever
qubit-by-qubit correspondence the stabilizer algebra supplies.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a merge reveals $Z_{L_1}$ and $Z_{L_2}$ individually.** It
only ever fixes their *product*, $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$; Case 2 under The
Merge's Back-Action shows this explicitly: the individual patches' $Z$
eigenvalues are generically randomized by the same measurement that
pins down their product, just like measuring $Z\otimes Z$ on two
ordinary qubits.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does merging two patches along their **smooth** (rather than
   rough) shared boundary measure $X_{L_1}X_{L_2}$ instead of
   $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the boundary type decides which stabilizer type the seam's new
   generators are, and the merge measures the product of the two logical
   operators *of that same type*.

   Follow the chain on the merge already worked above, then run it again
   with the labels swapped. Merging along the rough edges puts the new
   generators in a gap column at odd $x$, which are **vertex**
   ($Z$-type) sites. Their product, with every bridge qubit cancelling
   between its two neighbours, is $Z$ on $L_1$'s seam-side column times
   $Z$ on $L_2$'s seam-side column, and each column is a valid $Z_L$
   representative for its own patch. A product of $Z$-type operators is
   $Z$-type, so the only thing such a seam can possibly report is a $Z$
   parity.

   Reflect the whole picture through the lattice's $X\leftrightarrow Z$
   duality, which exchanges vertices with faces and rough boundaries with
   smooth ones, and every step survives with the letters swapped. Merging
   along smooth edges puts the new generators at **face** ($X$-type)
   sites in the gap row, their product is $X$ on each patch's seam-side
   row, and the reported parity is $X_{L_1}X_{L_2}$. There is no second
   derivation to do. The two merges are the same construction seen in a
   mirror.

   The complementary half of the story is which logical operator gets
   *destroyed*, and it is worth checking that the ledger balances. A
   rough merge leaves $X_{L_1}$ ending in what is now the merged patch's
   bulk, where a new seam vertex generator overlaps it in exactly one
   qubit and detects it, so only the joined string $X_{L_1}X_{L_2}$
   survives as a logical operator. Meanwhile $Z_{L_1}$ and $Z_{L_2}$ run
   perpendicular to the seam, terminate on boundaries the merge never
   touched, and both remain valid representatives of the merged patch's
   single $Z_L$. Two operators that are the same logical operator have a
   product that is a stabilizer, which is another way of saying the merge
   measured it. One logical qubit went in, one came out, and one classical
   bit came out with it.

   **The wrong turn** is treating the boundary type as something chosen at
   merge time. It is not. A patch's rough and smooth edges are fixed by
   how it was laid out on the chip, so two patches sitting side by side
   can perform exactly one of the two joint measurements, whichever their
   facing edges support. Wanting the other one means physically rotating
   or relocating a patch, which is itself a sequence of surgeries through
   routing space. This is why a compiler's floorplan is a real
   optimization problem rather than a detail, and it is the same cost the
   lesson's adjacency requirement names.

   </details>

2. In the logical-CNOT protocol above, why must the ancilla $A$ be
   discarded (measured out) rather than reused directly as one of the
   two output logical qubits?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because after step 3 the ancilla is entangled with the target, and a
   CNOT is a unitary on $(C,T)$ alone. Anything left entangled with a
   third system would leave the output mixed rather than the pure state
   the gate is supposed to produce.

   Trace where the entanglement comes from. Step 3 leaves $X_AX_T$
   stabilized with a definite value. If $A$ and $T$ were in a product
   state at that point, then $X_A$ and $X_T$ would each be separately
   determined, which would mean the target had been forced into an
   $X$-eigenstate. That is true only for special inputs. For a general
   target state, $A$ and $T$ come out of step 3 entangled, and the only
   way to hand back a clean two-qubit output is to collapse $A$.

   Step 4 is what does the collapsing. Measuring $Z_A$ anticommutes with
   the $X_AX_T$ just fixed, so that correlation is removed from the
   stabilizer group and $A$ is left in $|0\rangle_L$ or $|1\rangle_L$: a
   definite classical state, carrying no quantum information, provably
   unentangled from anything. Only then does $(C,T)$ hold a pure state,
   and only then is a Pauli correction able to finish the job.

   There is a second reason, and it is the one that generalizes. The
   merge outcomes $m_1$ and $m_2$ are fair coins, so immediately after
   step 3 the state of the whole system is a branch structure, one branch
   per outcome pattern, each needing a different Pauli fix. A Pauli
   correction can only be applied once the branch is *known*. Leaving $A$
   unmeasured leaves the branch unresolved, and no operator on $(C,T)$
   can repair a superposition of corrections. This is the same reason
   ordinary teleportation cannot skip Alice's measurement and simply have
   Bob apply "the right correction."

   The practical reading is the cheerful one. $A$ is a bus, not a
   register. It never carried the answer at any point in the protocol,
   which is why consuming it costs nothing except its chip area
   and the rounds it occupied, and why that area is free again for the
   next gate the moment the measurement lands.

   </details>

3. Explain why a merge followed immediately by a split, with no other
   operation in between, still has a physical effect on the two patches
   even though they end up separate again. Tie your answer to Case 2 of
   The Merge's Back-Action.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the merge is a measurement, and measurements are not undone by
   putting the apparatus back the way it was.

   The stabilizer group does return to its starting form: same qubits,
   same generators, two logical qubits again. What does not return is the
   *state* inside the code space. The merge projected it onto an
   eigenspace of $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$, and projection destroys information
   that no later configuration change can restore.

   Case 2 is that in the smallest case that shows it. Start with
   $|+\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}$, whose $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$ is not
   determined, and write it as
   $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2}+|1\rangle_{L_1}|0\rangle_{L_2})$.
   The merge collapses that to one term or the other with equal
   probability. Split the patches immediately afterwards and $L_1$ is in
   $|0\rangle_L$ or $|1\rangle_L$, never back in $|+\rangle_L$. The
   original superposition is gone, and it was not stored anywhere for the
   split to give back.

   Case 1 is the useful contrast. Two patches already in $Z$
   eigenstates start out as an eigenstate of $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$, the
   outcome is certain, and the merge genuinely does nothing. So it is not
   that merge-then-split always disturbs the state; it disturbs exactly
   what a $Z\otimes Z$ measurement disturbs, which is the correct
   characterization of what the operation *is*.

   The frame worth carrying forward: merge-then-split is not "connect,
   then disconnect." It is a logical $ZZ$ parity measurement, and the
   merging and splitting are the mechanism that implements it, in the
   same way that pulling two stabilizer generators in and out of the
   measurement schedule is how any syndrome bit gets extracted. Asking
   why merge-then-split is not the identity is asking why measuring an
   observable is not the identity.

   **The wrong turn** is reasoning from reversibility: every step is a
   local stabilizer measurement, stabilizer measurements are implemented
   by unitary circuits plus ancilla readout, so surely the whole thing
   can be run backwards. The unitary part can. The readout cannot, and
   that is where the outcome bit came from. The protocol's own structure
   admits as much by recording $m$ and spending it on a later Pauli
   correction instead of trying to undo it.

   </details>

4. Surface Codes in Depth argued that surface codes are hardware-friendly
   because every stabilizer is local. Explain precisely why lattice
   surgery inherits that same property, and does not reintroduce a
   long-range requirement through the back door.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because every quantum operation in a merge or a split is a stabilizer
   measurement on a handful of mutually adjacent qubits, which is the
   same primitive the code already runs continuously, on the same
   hardware, at the same rate.

   Take the seam generators built above. Each acts on at most four data
   qubits: one or two bridge qubits in the gap, plus one qubit on each
   patch's facing column. Those sit within one lattice spacing of each
   other by construction, because the merge only happens between patches
   that were placed adjacent. No gate anywhere in the protocol couples a
   qubit in $L_1$'s interior to a qubit in $L_2$'s interior. Compare that
   with the transversal two-patch gate this lesson opened by ruling out,
   which demands a partner for every qubit across the whole patch.

   Three back doors are worth checking explicitly, since "it is local"
   is the kind of claim that quietly stops being true.

   **The logical operators are not local.** $X_L$ is a string spanning the
   patch, and the protocol's corrections act on whole logical qubits.
   This one is a non-issue for two independent reasons. A logical Pauli
   is a product of *single-qubit* Paulis along a string, each acting on
   its own qubit with no coupling to any other, so wide support is not
   the same thing as long-range interaction. And in practice the
   corrections are not applied at all: they are tracked in a classical
   Pauli frame and folded into the interpretation of later measurements,
   costing zero gates.

   **The decoding is global.** This one is real, and the honest answer is
   that the non-locality is classical. A merge must be repeated for
   $O(d)$ rounds and its outcome inferred by decoding the seam's whole
   spacetime syndrome history, which is a computation over data collected
   from the entire seam. That inference happens in control electronics,
   where a wire between distant points is cheap and a nanosecond of
   latency is the only cost. The quantum layer stays strictly local; the
   global reasoning is pushed into the classical layer, which is the
   division of labour the entire architecture is built around.

   **Distant logical qubits still need to interact.** They do, and
   surgery does not pretend otherwise. Two patches at opposite ends of a
   chip cannot merge; one must first be moved next to the other, through
   a chain of local surgeries across reserved routing space. So long
   range is not smuggled in through an exotic coupler, it is paid for in
   spacetime volume, in a currency the resource estimates already count.
   That is the right trade to make, because chip area is something a
   fabrication process can supply and a long-range coupler between two
   arbitrary superconducting qubits is not.

   </details>

## Summary

- A transversal two-qubit gate needs a physical-qubit-by-physical-qubit
  *coupling* between two code blocks. Two identical surface-code patches
  have the index correspondence the stabilizer algebra needs, but on a 2D
  chip only their shared boundary qubits are physically adjacent, which
  rules out a direct transversal two-patch gate on planar hardware.
- **MERGE** brings two patches into contact and turns on new joint
  stabilizers along the seam (ordinary syndrome-style stabilizer
  measurement, just spanning two formerly independent patches), which
  fuses them into one patch and projectively measures the product of
  their logical operators (e.g. $Z_{L_1}Z_{L_2}$). **SPLIT** reverses
  this by reintroducing each patch's own individual boundary
  stabilizers.
- A disposable ancilla patch prepared in $|+\rangle_L$, a $ZZ$
  merge/split, an $XX$ merge/split, a final single-patch $Z$ measurement
  of the ancilla, and classically-controlled Pauli
  corrections ($Z_C$ when $m_2=-1$, $X_T$ when $m_1m_3=-1$) compose into
  a full logical CNOT, the same
  gate-teleportation structure as ordinary qubit teleportation, one
  level up, at the code-patch level.
- This is the real, dominant mechanism proposed for logical two-qubit
  gates in 2D surface-code architectures, because it needs no
  hardware primitive beyond the local stabilizer measurements the code
  already performs for error correction.
