export function smallestOddAtLeast(x) {
  let n = Math.ceil(x);
  if (n % 2 === 0) n += 1;
  return n;
}

## Motivation

Five lessons, one running argument: **Surface Codes in Depth** gave the
2D lattice and its logical $X$/$Z$ operators, **Decoding Surface Codes**
gave the exponential-in-distance suppression law that makes a bigger
lattice actually pay for itself below threshold, **Lattice Surgery** gave
the real mechanism for a logical two-qubit gate, **Magic States and
Distillation** gave the unavoidable, non-Clifford cost of a universal
gate set, and **The Threshold Theorem** proved that all of this composes
into an arbitrarily low logical error rate, provided the physical error
rate sits below $p_{\mathrm{th}}$. Every one of those lessons ended with
a number. This capstone's only job is to chain those numbers together,
for one small, fully computable toy circuit, into a single physical
qubit count, and to be completely explicit about every assumption that
number depends on.

**The toy algorithm** (stated as a given, illustrative example, not a
real published algorithm's actual gate count): a circuit requiring
$N_T = 100$ T gates and $N_2 = 500$ two-qubit Clifford gates, which must
complete with overall success probability at least $99\%$. Nothing about
$100$ and $500$ is derived from any real compiled circuit; they're
chosen purely to be small enough to compute by hand while still
exercising every step this course built.

## Mathematical Development

### Step 1: the per-gate logical error budget

The circuit succeeds only if *every one* of its $N_T + N_2$ gates
succeeds. A union bound turns that into a budget: if $p_L$ is each
gate's logical error probability,

$$
P(\text{any gate fails}) \;\le\; \sum_{i=1}^{N_T+N_2} p_{L,i} \;\le\; (N_T+N_2)\, p_L,
$$

using the same $p_L$ for every gate as a simplifying (and conservative)
choice. Requiring the overall failure probability to be at most $1\%$
gives the per-gate target

$$
p_{L}^{\text{target}} \;\approx\; \frac{0.01}{N_T + N_2}.
$$

For this toy circuit, $N_T + N_2 = 100 + 500 = 600$
(`toyTotalGates`), so

$$
p_L^{\text{target}} = \frac{0.01}{600} \approx 1.667\times 10^{-5}.
$$

That is `perGateBudget` computed directly above: **{perGateBudget.toExponential(3)}**.

### Step 2: solving for the code distance

The Decoding Surface Codes lesson's scaling law is
$p_L \sim (p/p_{\mathrm{th}})^{(d+1)/2}$ for a physical error rate $p$
comfortably below threshold $p_{\mathrm{th}}$. Take the threshold value
that lesson established, $p_{\mathrm{th}} = 1\%$, and operate at a
standard "comfortably below threshold" point, $p = p_{\mathrm{th}}/10 =
0.001$, so $p/p_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.1$. Setting the scaling law equal to
the Step 1 target and solving for $d$:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Set the threshold-theorem scaling law equal to the Step 1 target and take the log of both sides.">
    $$
    \left(\frac{p}{p_{\mathrm{th}}}\right)^{(d+1)/2} \le p_L^{\text{target}}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \frac{d+1}{2}\ln\!\left(\frac{p}{p_{\mathrm{th}}}\right) \le \ln p_L^{\text{target}}.
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="ln(p/p_th) = ln(0.1) is negative, so dividing by it flips the inequality direction.">
    $$
    \frac{d+1}{2} \ge \frac{\ln p_L^{\text{target}}}{\ln(p/p_{\mathrm{th}})}
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    d \ge 2\,\frac{\ln p_L^{\text{target}}}{\ln(p/p_{\mathrm{th}})} - 1.
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Plugging in $p_L^{\text{target}} \approx 1.667\times10^{-5}$ and
$p/p_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.1$: `minCodeDistanceRaw` evaluates to
**{minCodeDistanceRaw.toFixed(3)}**. Surface-code distance must be an
odd integer (Surface Codes in Depth's convention, so the decoder's
majority vote never ties), so the smallest admissible distance is the
smallest odd integer at or above that raw value:

$$
d = 9.
$$

Checking the achieved rate against the target confirms the rounding was
necessary and sufficient: at $d=9$, $(0.1)^{(9+1)/2} = 10^{-5}$
(`achievedLogicalErrorRate` = **{achievedLogicalErrorRate.toExponential(3)}**),
just under the {perGateBudget.toExponential(3)} target, while $d=7$
would give $(0.1)^{4} = 10^{-4}$, an order of magnitude *over* budget.

### Step 3: magic-state cost

The Magic States and Distillation lesson's 15-to-1 Bravyi-Kitaev protocol
takes $15$ noisy input magic states at error rate $p_{\text{in}}$ and
outputs $1$ state at error rate

$$
p_{\text{out}} \approx 35\, p_{\text{in}}^3.
$$

That formula is reused as derived there; what's new here is
applying it to this lesson's own $N_T$ and target budget rather than
that lesson's worked numbers. Taking the raw injected magic-state error
to be comparable to the operating physical error rate, $p_{\text{in}} =
p = 0.001$:

$$
p_{\text{out}} = 35\,(0.001)^3 = 3.5\times10^{-8},
$$

which is `distillationOutputError` = **{distillationOutputError.toExponential(2)}**
above. That single round already sits **{magicStateHeadroom.toFixed(0)}×**
below the per-gate target of {perGateBudget.toExponential(3)}
(`magicStateHeadroom`), so **one level of 15-to-1 distillation is
sufficient** for this toy algorithm's $N_T = 100$ T gates. No second
distillation level is needed, unlike a circuit whose T-count or error
budget demanded far more suppression.

Each of the $15$ input patches for that one factory round is modeled
here, as a simplifying and conservative choice, at the same distance
$d=9$ as the main computation (a real design can often run the factory
at a smaller distance, since the cubic suppression already does most of
the work; this toy estimate skips that optimization so the
qubit-counting formula in Step 4 stays a single, reusable expression).

A distance-$d$ rotated surface-code patch holds $d^2$ data qubits and
$d^2-1$ ancilla (syndrome-measurement) qubits, using the more
qubit-efficient rotated-code convention Surface Codes in Depth
discussed but didn't build in full (that lesson constructed the
unrotated lattice instead). At $d=9$: `dataQubitsPerPatch` =
**{dataQubitsPerPatch}**, `ancillaQubitsPerPatch` =
**{ancillaQubitsPerPatch}**, so one logical patch costs
`qubitsPerLogicalPatch` = **{qubitsPerLogicalPatch}** physical qubits.

Assume the toy algorithm needs `logicalQubitCount` = **{logicalQubitCount}**
logical qubits (a small, stated assumption, well within this course's
scope). The compute register then costs:

$$
\text{data} = 6 \times 81 = 486, \qquad \text{ancilla} = 6\times 80 = 480,
$$

`totalDataQubits` = **{totalDataQubits}**, `totalAncillaQubits` =
**{totalAncillaQubits}**. The magic-state factory needs $15$ patches at
the same distance:

$$
\text{factory} = 15 \times 161 = 2415,
$$

`factoryQubits` = **{factoryQubits}**. Summing all three:

$$
N_{\text{physical}} = 486 + 480 + 2415 = 3381,
$$

`totalPhysicalQubits` = **{totalPhysicalQubits}**. The factory alone is
`factoryShareOfTotal` = **{(factoryShareOfTotal * 100).toFixed(1)}%** of
the total, and that share is not a coincidence of these particular
numbers. Because both the compute register's qubit cost ($6$ patches)
and the factory's qubit cost ($15$ patches) scale with the *same*
per-patch factor $2d^2-1$, the ratio collapses to
$15/(15+6) = 5/7 \approx 71.4\%$ **for any code distance**, as long as
the factory and the compute patches share that distance. Doubling $d$
would more than triple the total qubit count without changing this
ratio at all.

## Physical Interpretation: what generalizes and what doesn't

<InsightBlock>
  The four-step pipeline, not any single number it outputs, is the real
  takeaway: a target success probability plus a compiled gate count
  determines a code distance, a distillation depth, and a qubit total:
  the same structure a real published estimate uses, just at larger
  scale.
</InsightBlock>

<Callout type="note">
**This is how real resource estimates actually get built, not a simplified stand-in for it.** Published
estimates for algorithmically significant problems, including the
widely cited estimates for factoring cryptographically-relevant RSA
moduli with Shor's algorithm, follow these same four steps:
(1) a union-bound (or tighter) error budget from the compiled circuit's
total gate count and a target success probability, (2) the same
threshold-theorem scaling law solved for the minimum code distance,
(3) the same distillation error-suppression arithmetic scaled to
however many T gates the real circuit needs, (4) the same
data-plus-ancilla-plus-factory qubit sum. That methodology is not a
simplification unique to this toy example. It is the real pipeline.
</Callout>

What real papers do differently is scale, not method. A
cryptographically relevant factoring circuit needs billions of
Toffoli/T operations rather than $100$, and the headline number is
sobering: the standard published estimate for factoring a 2048-bit RSA
modulus lands at roughly **20 million physical qubits running for
about 8 hours**. Set that against this lesson's toy total of
{totalPhysicalQubits} physical qubits. The real workload costs nearly
four orders of magnitude more qubits, produced by the same four-step
pipeline with real inputs, and at the same assumed $10^{-3}$ physical
error rate this lesson's Step 2 chose as its operating point.

<ResearchConnection title="Factoring RSA-2048: 20 million qubits, 8 hours" source="Gidney & Ekerå, Quantum 5, 433 (2021)" url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.09749">
  Under concrete superconducting-hardware assumptions (a planar grid
  with nearest-neighbor connectivity, physical gate error rate
  $10^{-3}$, a 1 µs surface-code cycle), Gidney and Ekerå estimate
  factoring a 2048-bit RSA integer at about 20 million physical qubits
  for about 8 hours. That was itself a roughly hundredfold spacetime
  improvement over earlier comparable estimates, which is why this
  paper's numbers, not its predecessors', are the ones to quote.
</ResearchConnection>

<Callout type="warning">
**A qubit count is half a resource estimate.** Every number in this
lesson is a *space* figure, and space alone cannot be compared between
two proposals. A design using twice the qubits for a tenth of the time
is usually the better design, which is why the field quotes **spacetime
volume** (physical qubits × surface-code rounds) rather than qubit count.
This toy estimate's own time cost is visible in the choices it made: one
factory serving 100 T gates has to run 100 sequential distillation
rounds, and each logical two-qubit gate costs on the order of $d=9$
surface-code cycles of lattice surgery. Two designs with the same 3381
qubits can differ by an order of magnitude in runtime, and nothing in
Step 4 would tell them apart. Gidney and Ekerå's estimate below is quoted
as "20 million qubits **for 8 hours**" for exactly this reason: the
second half of that phrase is not colour.
</Callout>

Real estimates also model complications this toy example
deliberately dropped: **qubit connectivity and routing overhead** (real
hardware isn't an idealized fully-connected lattice; moving logical
information costs extra ancilla and time), **T-gate parallelization
limits** (this lesson assumed one factory reused sequentially $100$
times; a real circuit with a much larger T-count needs many factories
running in parallel to keep up with the rate gates are consumed, which
multiplies the factory qubit cost, not just the runtime), and **decoder
speed constraints** (the classical decoder must keep up with the syndrome
extraction rate in real time, or the whole computation stalls). None of
those complications changes the four-step *structure* derived above.
They change the *inputs* to Step 1 (gate count), Step 2 (achievable $p$),
and Step 3 (parallel factory count). A reader should take away the
structure of a real estimate, not treat any specific published qubit
count as a universal constant.

## Worked Example

Putting all four steps together end to end, for this toy circuit:

| Step | Quantity | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Total gates $N_T+N_2$ | {toyTotalGates} |
| 1 | Per-gate error budget $p_L^{\text{target}}$ | {perGateBudget.toExponential(3)} |
| 2 | Operating point $p/p_{\mathrm{th}}$ | {errorRatio} |
| 2 | Minimum code distance $d$ | {codeDistance} |
| 3 | Distillation output error $p_{\text{out}}$ | {distillationOutputError.toExponential(2)} |
| 3 | Headroom over target | {magicStateHeadroom.toFixed(0)}× |
| 4 | Data + ancilla qubits ({logicalQubitCount} logical qubits) | {computeQubits} |
| 4 | Factory qubits | {factoryQubits} |
| 4 | **Total physical qubits** | **{totalPhysicalQubits}** |

A natural follow-up question this same pipeline answers immediately:
*what if the physical error rate could be pushed an order of magnitude
lower*, to $p = p_{\mathrm{th}}/100 = 0.0001$ instead of
$p_{\mathrm{th}}/10$? Re-running Step 2 with `altErrorRatio` = 0.01
gives `altMinCodeDistanceRaw` = **{altMinCodeDistanceRaw.toFixed(3)}**,
so the smallest admissible distance drops to
`altCodeDistance` = **{altCodeDistance}**: from $d=9$ to $d=5$. Re-running
Step 4 at that smaller distance gives `altTotalPhysicalQubits` =
**{altTotalPhysicalQubits}**, roughly a **third** of the original
{totalPhysicalQubits}-qubit estimate, even though the *ratio* the
factory occupies stays at `altFactoryShareOfTotal` =
**{(altFactoryShareOfTotal * 100).toFixed(1)}%**, the same $5/7$ as
before, because that ratio depends only on the $15$-to-$6$ patch count,
not on $d$. One order of magnitude of hardware error-rate improvement
here is worth roughly a factor of three in total physical qubits, at
fixed factory-share overhead: the kind of quantitative,
checkable tradeoff this course's derivations, not intuition, can
produce.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying the per-gate budget to the whole circuit's error, not each
gate's.** The union bound in Step 1 gives a *per-gate* target
$p_L^{\text{target}} \approx 0.01/(N_T+N_2)$, not a single global
$0.01$ applied once. Using $p_L = 0.01$ directly in Step 2's scaling law
would drastically undershoot the actual code distance this circuit
needs, since $600$ gates each contribute their own failure chance.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating the {totalPhysicalQubits}-qubit total, or any other specific
number in this lesson, as "the" cost of fault tolerance.** Every number
in this worked example is downstream of stated toy choices: $N_T=100$,
$N_2=500$, $6$ logical qubits, $p = p_{\mathrm{th}}/10$, one factory
reused sequentially. Change any one of those (as the alternate-scenario
calculation above does for $p$) and the total changes, sometimes by a
large factor. What's stable across such changes is the four-step
*method*, not any individual output number, which is this
lesson's Physical Interpretation point about published estimates too.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A four-step pipeline turns a compiled circuit's gate counts into a
  physical qubit count: (1) a union bound converts a target overall
  success probability into a per-gate logical error budget,
  (2) the surface code's threshold-theorem scaling law is solved for
  the minimum code distance that budget requires, (3) the magic-state
  distillation error-suppression formula determines how many
  distillation rounds are needed to supply enough T gates at that
  budget, (4) data, ancilla, and factory qubit counts at that distance
  sum to a total.
- For this lesson's toy circuit ($N_T=100$, $N_2=500$, $99\%$ overall
  success, $6$ logical qubits, $p=p_{\mathrm{th}}/10$): a per-gate
  budget of $1.667\times10^{-5}$ requires code distance $d=9$, one
  round of $15$-to-$1$ distillation suffices with $476\times$ headroom,
  and the total physical-qubit estimate is $3381$, of which the
  magic-state factory alone is $2415$ qubits: $71.4\%$, exactly
  $5/7$, of the total.
- That factory-dominated ratio, and the four-step method that produced
  it, is the same methodology real published resource estimates use.
  Gidney and Ekerå's RSA-2048 estimate lands at about 20 million
  physical qubits for about 8 hours; what differs at real scale is the
  size of the inputs and additional engineering complications (routing,
  parallelization, decoder throughput) this toy example deliberately
  set aside, not the underlying structure of the calculation.
