## From Syndrome to Correction

Surface Codes in Depth built an explicit distance-3 patch (13 data
qubits, 12 stabilizers, 6 of each type, all weight 3 or 4): physical
qubits on the edges of a grid, a Z-type stabilizer at every vertex, an
X-type stabilizer on every face, and the single most important fact for
what comes next: away from a boundary, a lone Pauli error on one qubit
flips exactly **two** stabilizers of the opposite type, the two it
touches. That pair of flipped stabilizers is a **defect pair**. The
worked cases at the end of this lesson run on a smaller, deliberately
simplified lattice with its own counts, flagged explicitly where it is
introduced; what carries over from the prerequisite is the defect-pair
mechanism, not the qubit inventory.

That lesson stopped at "here's what one error does to the syndrome."
Real devices don't get one error at a time between rounds of correction:
many qubits fail simultaneously, every measurement round, for as long as
the computation runs. This lesson shows by direct construction that
decoding becomes **minimum-weight perfect matching** on a graph.

## Decoding as Graph Matching

### The decoding problem, stated precisely

Given: the full set of stabilizers that reported $-1$ (flipped) this
round, the **syndrome**. Wanted: a Pauli operator $E$ (a "correction")
such that applying $E$ returns the state to the code space, ideally
undoing whatever physical error $E_{\text{true}}$ actually occurred.
Since applying $E$ after $E_{\text{true}}$ leaves a net operator
$E \cdot E_{\text{true}}$ on the data qubits, the decoder doesn't need
to guess $E_{\text{true}}$ outright. It only needs $E\cdot E_{\text{true}}$
to be a **stabilizer** (acts trivially on the code space) rather than a
**logical operator** (flips the encoded information). Two different
guesses that differ only by a stabilizer are equally good corrections.
Two that differ by a logical operator are not: one of them causes a
**logical error**, silently, with no further syndrome signal to catch it.

### Why this is a graph-matching problem

Picture every currently-flipped stabilizer as a **vertex** in a new
graph, call it the decoding graph, completely separate from the
physical lattice. A single-qubit error is one edge of this
graph, connecting the two defects it would produce; a longer connected
chain of same-type errors produces a defect only at the chain's two
*endpoints* (every interior vertex along the chain gets touched twice,
once by each of its two neighboring errors in the chain, and flips
back to even parity). So under an independent-error noise model, the
probability that a specific pair of defects came from a single error
chain falls off with the chain's length, which makes $-\log(\text{probability})$
a sensible **edge weight** between any two defects: short plausible
chains get small weight, long unlikely ones get large weight. A
defect can also connect to a **virtual boundary vertex**, representing
an error chain that terminates on the patch's boundary instead of at
another defect. That boundary edge carries a weight too: the length of
the shortest correction chain from the defect to the boundary, which
is never less than 1. No edge in this graph is free, because clearing
a defect always means flipping at least one real qubit.

Given that weighted graph, "find the most probable error consistent
with this syndrome" becomes: **pair up every defect with a partner (a
real defect, or the virtual boundary) such that the total edge weight
is minimized**: a minimum-weight perfect matching. This reduction is
not a metaphor or a loose analogy. It's an exact restatement of the
same maximum-likelihood question, and it's why "decode a surface code"
and "solve a matching problem" are the same sentence in the literature.

### Minimum-weight perfect matching (MWPM) decoding, explicitly

1. Measure every stabilizer; collect the set of flipped ones (the defects).
2. Build the decoding graph: one vertex per defect, one virtual boundary
   vertex (or several, per boundary segment), edges weighted by a
   monotonic function of "how plausible is a single chain connecting
   these two." A defect's edge to the boundary is weighted by its
   distance to that boundary, never less than 1.
3. Find a **perfect matching** (every defect gets exactly one partner)
   of **minimum total weight**.
4. For each matched pair, apply the correction corresponding to *some*
   shortest chain connecting them (any chain with the right two
   endpoints is an equally valid correction, since two such chains
   differ by a closed loop, and a closed loop that bounds a region of
   faces is itself a stabilizer; see Case 2 under Three Syndromes,
   Matched by Hand below).

X errors (caught by vertex/Z-type stabilizers) and Z errors (caught by
face/X-type stabilizers) are decoded on **two separate, independent**
graphs, since under the standard independent-error model an X error and
a Z error contribute independent syndrome information. The same
reduction runs twice, once per error type.

### The threshold and the distance-scaling law

Simulating and experimenting on real surface-code patches gives a
well-established empirical/theoretical relationship: **below** some
threshold physical error rate $p_{th}$, the logical error rate $p_L$
falls off *exponentially* with code distance $d$,

  terms={[
    { id: "pL", symbol: "p_L", gloss: "Logical error rate: how often the decoder ends up applying the wrong correction." },
    { id: "A", symbol: "A", gloss: "A roughly constant prefactor, not the interesting part of the scaling." },
    { id: "p", symbol: "p", gloss: "The physical error rate per gate/qubit: the hardware's actual noise level." },
    { id: "pth", symbol: "p_th", gloss: "The threshold: decoder- and noise-model-dependent, roughly percent-level in real work, not a universal constant." },
    { id: "d", symbol: "d", gloss: "Code distance: roughly, the size of the surface-code patch." },
  ]}
>
  $$
  p_L \sim A\left(\frac{p}{p_{th}}\right)^{(d+1)/2}
  $$
</EquationReveal>

for some roughly constant prefactor $A$. $p_{th}$ itself is
decoder- and noise-model-dependent, cited in real work as roughly
percent-level (commonly in the ballpark of $\sim 1\%$ for circuit-level
noise with a strong decoder), not a single universal constant that
applies to every code variant or every decoder equally. Deriving this
exponent rigorously is a nontrivial statistical-mechanics calculation.

than verbal. Four physical error rates, three of them below threshold and
one just above it, evaluated at code distances 3 through 15:

The slopes carry the point here; the values do not. Every step of $2$ in distance
multiplies $p_L$ by exactly one more factor of $p/p_{th}$, so going from
$d=7$ to $d=9$ multiplies the logical error rate by
{scalingGainPerTwoDistance[0].factor.toFixed(2)} at $p/p_{th}=0.1$, by
{scalingGainPerTwoDistance[2].factor.toFixed(2)} at $p/p_{th}=0.9$, and
by {scalingGainPerTwoDistance[3].factor.toFixed(2)} at $p/p_{th}=1.1$,
where a multiplier above $1$ means the error rate goes up rather than
down. Those two extra rows and columns of physical qubits buy
a tenfold improvement in the first case, a $10\%$ improvement in the
second, and a $10\%$ regression in the third. Operating just barely
below threshold is technically below threshold and practically almost
useless: the reason hardware groups chase error rates far below
$p_{th}$, rather than merely under it, is that the exponent's base is
what converts extra qubits into reliability, and a base of $0.9$ converts
them very slowly.

The prefactor $A$ is set to $0.1$ here for a legible plot. It is not a
universal constant, and none of the slopes above depend on it: changing
$A$ slides every curve vertically and leaves the fan shape, which is the
whole content of the law, untouched.

## What a Threshold Buys the Engineer

<InsightBlock>
  A decoder doesn't need to guess the true error, only a correction
  that differs from it by a stabilizer, not a logical operator. That's
  what turns "infer the error" into "find the cheapest matching," an
  exact reduction, not an analogy.
</InsightBlock>

### What threshold buys you, operationally

"Threshold" does not mean errors stop happening below $p_{th}$:
physical qubits keep failing at rate $p$ regardless. What changes is
what a **bigger patch** does for you: below threshold, each two extra
steps of distance multiply $p_L$ by another full factor of $(p/p_{th})$,
so a logical qubit can be made **arbitrarily reliable** just by building
a larger patch out of the same, unchanged physical qubits. The entire
promise of fault-tolerant quantum computing rests on this one
inequality holding. Above threshold, the sign of the exponent's effect
flips against you: $(p/p_{th})>1$ raised to a *larger* power is a
*larger* number, so a bigger patch gets **less** reliable, not more.
This is why "just add more qubits" is not automatically a fix for a
noisy device. Every additional physical qubit and every additional
syndrome-extraction gate is one more chance to introduce an error, and
above threshold the corrections start introducing errors faster than
they remove them.

<HistoricalMoment date="December 2024" place="Google Quantum AI">
  The Willow chip runs this experiment on real hardware:
  distance-3, distance-5, and distance-7 patches of the same repeated
  stabilizer pattern, with the logical error rate dropping roughly in
  half at each larger distance. That is a direct demonstration that a
  physical device was operating below its own threshold, not merely a
  simulation of the scaling law above.
</HistoricalMoment>

### Why this matters: decoding is a live engineering problem

Minimum-weight perfect matching can be solved efficiently
(polynomial-time, via variants of Edmonds' blossom algorithm), but
"efficient" in the complexity-theory sense is not the same as "fast
enough." A real superconducting device runs syndrome-extraction rounds
on the order of a microsecond; the decoder has to keep up with that rate
indefinitely, or the backlog of unprocessed syndromes grows without
bound and the whole point of real-time correction is lost. An
asymptotically optimal decoder that's too slow for the hardware it's
attached to is, operationally, not a working decoder at all. This is
why decoder speed and parallelism are an **active
current research area**, not a solved footnote: real implementations
use **Union-Find decoders** (near-linear time, trading a little accuracy
for a lot of speed) and increasingly **neural-network decoders** (trained
rather than derived, aiming to match MWPM's accuracy while running
faster or more easily in parallel on custom hardware), alongside
continued work on parallelizing MWPM itself. Which decoder wins on which
hardware, and at which code distance, is not settled, and real
fault-tolerance roadmaps are still being written around that gap.

<ResearchConnection
  title="Trading a little accuracy for a lot of speed"
  source="Delfosse & Nickerson, Quantum 5, 595 (2021) (arXiv:1709.06218)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.06218"
>
  The union-find decoder: an almost-linear-time algorithm that grows
  clusters around defects and merges them until each cluster contains a
  correctable error, rather than solving the matching problem exactly.
  Its threshold is measurably below MWPM's on the same noise model, and
  that is the point. The trade this lesson describes in words is made
  quantitative here, and the paper is explicit that a slightly worse
  threshold is worth having if it is the difference between keeping up
  with the syndrome stream and not.
</ResearchConnection>

## Three Syndromes, Matched by Hand

All three cases below use the same small patch: {vertexList.length}
vertices (Z-type stabilizers, weight {vertexWeights.N00} at each corner,
weight {vertexWeights.N01} along each edge, weight {vertexWeights.N11}
at the center) and {allQubits.length} data qubits sitting on the edges
between them. Every number below is computed directly from that
lattice's incidence structure.

<Callout type="note">
**This is a deliberately simplified lattice, not the distance-3 patch
from Surface Codes in Depth.** That lesson's $5\times5$ coordinate patch
carries 13 data qubits and 12 stabilizers, 6 of each type, every one of
them weight 3 or 4, with an explicit callout that its boundary
generators are the weight-3 ones. The toy used here is a plain
$3\times3$ grid of vertices: {allQubits.length} data qubits,
{vertexList.length} vertex ($Z$) stabilizers of weight
{vertexWeights.N00}, {vertexWeights.N01} and {vertexWeights.N11}, and
{faceList.length} face ($X$) stabilizers. Lattice Surgery makes the same
kind of departure and flags it in its own callout, trimming the patch
until its boundary generators drop to weight 2; the reason here is the
same one: the matching arithmetic below is then small enough to check by
hand at every step.

One consequence is worth stating rather than gliding past.
{allQubits.length} qubits carry {vertexList.length - 1} *independent*
vertex generators (the product of all {vertexList.length} of them is the
identity, since every qubit is touched exactly twice) plus
{faceList.length} independent face generators, leaving
{allQubits.length - (vertexList.length - 1) - faceList.length} logical
qubits. This closed toy lattice encodes nothing at all until Case 3
opens a boundary by adding half-edges. So Cases 1 and 2 below are about
returning to the **code space**, and only Case 3, once a boundary
exists, is about a logical operator corrupting encoded information. Each
case demonstrates the real mechanism; the counts are this toy's, not a
distance-3 patch's.
</Callout>

### Case 1: a single-qubit error (success)

An X error strikes only qubit {singleErrorQubit[0]}, the edge connecting
N01 and N11. Computed directly from the incidence table, the syndrome is
the two vertices that qubit touches and nothing else:
**{singleErrorSyndrome.join(" and ")}**, the defect pair away-from-boundary
example from Surface Codes in Depth, reproduced explicitly. The decoding
graph has a single edge between these two defects (weight 1, the
qubit itself), so the minimum-weight matching is unambiguous: pair
{singleErrorSyndrome[0]} with {singleErrorSyndrome[1]}, correction =
$X$ on {singleErrorQubit[0]} again. $X^2=I$, so this is an exact
recovery, checked directly.

### Case 2: a 2-qubit chain (still a success, with a degeneracy)

X errors strike **both** {chainErrorQubits[0]} and {chainErrorQubits[1]},
a connected path N01 → N11 → N12. The middle vertex N11 is touched by
*both* errors, so its parity flips twice and returns to even; only the
two chain endpoints show up. Computed syndrome:
**{chainErrorSyndrome.join(" and ")}**. The shortest path between N01
and N12 on the lattice has length 2, realized by the chain
{chainMatchVertices.join(" → ")}: the two qubits that actually
failed, so MWPM reconstructs the true error qubit for qubit.

That shortest path is **not unique**. The alternate 2-edge route through N02,
{chainErrorAltPath.join(" and ")}, produces the identical computed
syndrome, **{chainAltSyndrome.join(" and ")}**, so a decoder could
equally well have matched along that path instead. Does guessing the
"wrong" (but equally short) chain matter? Computed directly: the
symmetric difference between the two candidate chains is
{chainDegeneracyDifference.join(", ")}. Checking every combination of
the patch's four face stabilizers shows this set equals the TR
face stabilizer{chainDegeneracyIsTrivial ? " (confirmed: it is a stabilizer product)" : ""}.
A correction differing from the true error by a stabilizer returns the
state to the code space and acts trivially there, so both candidate
chains are equally valid corrections. This is ordinary, harmless decoding **degeneracy**,
not an error.

### Case 3: a chain that reaches the boundary

Now X errors strike {boundaryErrorQubits[0]} and {boundaryErrorQubits[1]},
the path N10 → N11 → N12 straight across the patch's middle row, from
the **left boundary column to the right boundary column**. By the same
cancellation as Case 2, the computed syndrome is only the two endpoints:
**{boundaryErrorSyndrome.join(" and ")}**.

To decode this, the graph needs boundary edges, and the patch as drawn
cannot supply them on its own: all 12 of its qubits touch two vertices
each, so every chain announces both endpoints and nothing slips off the
edge invisibly. A full surface-code patch has open boundaries on two
sides. Each boundary vertex there carries one extra data qubit, a
half-edge dangling off the patch that touches that vertex alone. Add
those half-edges to the left and right columns of the toy patch. An X
error on a half-edge flips a single stabilizer, so the decoder can
clear one defect by itself: match it to the virtual boundary vertex and
apply the one-qubit correction on the half-edge. A boundary edge in the
decoding graph therefore costs 1 for N10 and N12, and in general costs
the length of the shortest correction chain from the defect to the
boundary, never 0.

The state is not restored. The net operator on the data is the true

{boundaryResidualIsLogical ? "**not** expressible as any product of them" : "expressible as a product of them"},
so no stabilizer relabeling can undo it. A boundary-to-boundary chain
is a logical operator: the decoder has completed the error into a
logical $X$ on the encoded qubit, silently, with a clean syndrome. This
is not a harmless degenerate relabeling like Case 2's.

Count what that failure required: two physical errors. On a genuine
distance-3 patch that is exactly where the guarantee runs out, since
such a code corrects any $\lfloor (d-1)/2 \rfloor = 1$ error and makes
no promise about two, and the guarantee is tight. Once a
real chain's length reaches about $d/2$, the boundary stubs that
complete it into a logical operator become jointly as cheap as, or
cheaper than, the chain itself, and the decoder can no longer separate
the true explanation from the fatal one. A logical failure therefore
needs at least $\lceil d/2 \rceil$ simultaneous errors, which is where
the exponent in $p_L \sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$ comes from: it counts
the minimum number of errors a fatal chain requires.

## Misreading What the Syndrome Says

<Callout type="warning">
**Thinking "threshold" means errors stop happening below $p_{th}$.**
They don't: physical qubits keep failing at the same rate $p$ regardless
of which side of threshold you're on. What changes is the *direction*
a bigger patch pushes you: below threshold, more qubits make the logical
error rate exponentially **better**; above threshold, more qubits make
it exponentially **worse**, since the correction machinery itself is now
failing often enough to out-error the errors it's meant to fix.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a decoder failure means the decoder made a mistake.** Case 3
above is minimum-weight matching working as designed: the boundary
matching it chose really was a minimum-weight explanation of the
observed syndrome. The failure is that two explanations tied and the
true one lost the tie-break. Whenever a real chain reaches about half
the code distance, a rival explanation becomes at least as cheap under
the decoder's own model, and no decoder can tell the two apart from the
syndrome alone.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. In Case 2's worked example, why does the middle vertex N11 show no
   defect at all, even though both error qubits touch it?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a stabilizer reports a *parity*, not a count, and two is even.

   N11 is a weight-4 vertex generator on $\{$H10, H11, V01, V11$\}$. The
   error is $X$ on V01 and $X$ on H11, and both of those qubits are in
   N11's support. $X$ and $Z$ anticommute at V01, giving one sign flip,
   and again at H11, giving a second. Two sign flips cancel, so N11
   commutes with the error and measures $+1$: no defect, indistinguishable
   from a vertex nothing happened to.

   The general form of this is worth extracting, because everything else
   in the lesson depends on it. A stabilizer measurement returns one bit.
   For a chain of $X$ errors, each vertex reports the parity of how many
   chain qubits touch it, which for a connected path is $2$ at every
   interior vertex (one edge arriving, one leaving) and $1$ at each of the
   two ends. So a chain of any length announces exactly two defects, at
   its endpoints, and reveals nothing at all about the route between them.
   In the language the lesson uses later, the syndrome is the *boundary*
   of the error chain over $\mathbb{F}_2$.

   Three consequences follow immediately, and they are the whole lesson.
   Decoding is a matching problem because endpoints come in pairs.
   Case 2's degeneracy exists because two routes with the same endpoints
   produce byte-identical syndromes. And Case 3 fails because a route
   that leaves the patch has no second endpoint to announce.

   **The wrong turn** is to expect the syndrome to report how many errors
   touched each stabilizer. If it did, decoding would be close to
   trivial: read the multiplicities and the chain reconstructs itself. It
   cannot, because measuring a stabilizer projects onto its $\pm1$
   eigenspaces and there is no third outcome to carry a count. That
   single-bit-per-check restriction is the reason a nontrivial inference
   problem exists at all.

   </details>

2. Case 3's failure specifically exploited both defects sitting *near
   opposite boundaries*. Would the same failure happen if the true error
   chain instead connected two defects both deep in the patch's
   interior, far from any boundary? Explain using edge weights.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   No, and the edge weights say why in one line: deep in the interior the
   boundary edges are expensive, so the competing explanation is not
   competitive.

   Put the same weight-2 chain in the middle of a distance-5 patch. The
   defect-to-defect edge still costs 2, the length of the shortest chain
   joining them. But each defect now sits roughly $(d-1)/2\approx2$ steps
   from the nearest boundary, so the rival matching costs about
   $2+2=4$. Minimum-weight matching picks the direct pairing by a margin
   of 2, not by a coin flip, and the correction reproduces the true error.
   Case 3's tie existed only because both boundary edges cost 1, which is
   the cheapest a boundary edge can ever be.

   The sharper statement is that boundary proximity is not really the
   variable. What competes with the true explanation is *the cheapest
   completion of the true chain into a logical operator*, and a decoder
   fails exactly when that completion costs no more than the chain
   itself. Near a boundary the completion is two short stubs. In the
   interior it is a whole crossing of the patch.

   That turns into a clean guarantee. Let $E$ be the true error and $C$
   the decoder's chosen correction. Since $C$ is minimum weight among
   chains with the observed endpoints, and $E$ is one such chain,
   $|C|\le|E|$. The residual $E+C$ is a closed chain, so it is either a
   stabilizer or a logical operator, and every logical operator has
   weight at least $d$. Therefore

   $$
   |E+C| \le |E| + |C| \le 2|E| < d \quad\text{whenever}\quad |E| < d/2,
   $$

   which forces the residual to be a stabilizer. Any error of weight
   below $d/2$ is decoded correctly wherever it sits on the patch, and
   the position, the boundary distances, and the tie-breaking rule are
   all irrelevant to that conclusion.

   Run the numbers back through Case 3 as a check. There $|E|=2$ and
   $d=3$, so $|E|=2\ge d/2=1.5$ and the guarantee does not apply: the
   failure is permitted, and it happens. Move the identical weight-2 chain
   onto a distance-5 patch and $2 < 2.5$, so no weight-2 error fails
   anywhere on that patch, interior or boundary. The failure was never
   about geometry. It was about a patch too small for the error it was
   asked to absorb.

   **The wrong turn** is concluding that interior errors are therefore
   safe and boundaries are the weak spot to engineer around. There is
   nothing to engineer around. Every logical operator must run
   boundary-to-boundary, so every logical failure ends at a boundary by
   definition, and shrinking or reshaping the boundary does not remove
   it. The only lever is the length of the cheapest crossing, which is
   the code distance, which is why the response to a failure rate is
   always a bigger patch and never a cleverer perimeter.

   </details>

3. A distance-11 patch and a distance-7 patch run at the same physical
   error rate, both comfortably below threshold. Without computing an
   exact number, which patch's logical error rate falls faster as $p$ is
   reduced further, and why does the exponent $(d+1)/2$ make that true?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The distance-11 patch, and by a lot. Its logical error rate scales as
   $p^6$ against the distance-7 patch's $p^4$.

   Read the exponent as a power of $p$ directly. Holding $A$ and
   $p_{th}$ fixed,

   $$
   p_L \propto p^{(d+1)/2},
   $$

   which is $p^4$ at $d=7$ and $p^6$ at $d=11$. On log-log axes those are
   straight lines of slope 4 and 6, so halving $p$ divides the
   distance-7 patch's logical error rate by $2^4=16$ and the distance-11
   patch's by $2^6=64$. The larger patch was already the lower curve, and
   it also descends more steeply, so the gap widens with every
   improvement to the hardware.

   The exponent is not a fitted parameter, which is what makes this more
   than a plotting observation. It counts errors. Question 2 established
   that a logical failure requires an error chain of weight at least
   $d/2$, so at odd $d$ it takes at least $\lceil d/2\rceil = (d+1)/2$
   simultaneous physical faults: 4 of them at $d=7$, 6 at $d=11$. Under
   an independent-error model a specified coincidence of $k$ faults has
   probability of order $p^k$, so the leading term in $p_L$ is $p^{(d+1)/2}$
   and the exponent in the scaling law is a headcount. Rarer coincidences
   thin out faster when the individual event gets rarer, which is the
   whole of the answer.

   The comparison worth making, since it is the one an engineer faces.
   At $p/p_{th}=0.5$, going from $d=11$ to $d=13$ buys one more factor of
   $0.5$: a $2\times$ improvement, at a cost of $13^2-11^2=4d+4=48$ extra
   data qubits per logical qubit in the rotated layout (about $96$ once
   each patch's measurement ancillas are counted). Halving the
   physical error rate instead buys $2^6=64\times$ at $d=11$, for no extra
   qubits at all. Below threshold both levers work, but they do not work
   equally, and the lesson's fan chart is that inequality drawn: better
   hardware moves you to a steeper curve, while more qubits only move you
   further along the one you are on.

   **The wrong turn** is reading "exponential suppression with distance"
   as a claim about how $p_L$ responds to $p$. Those are two different
   derivatives of the same law. At fixed $p$ below threshold, $p_L$ falls
   exponentially in $d$, which is the statement the whole field rests on.
   At fixed $d$, $p_L$ falls only polynomially in $p$, with degree
   $(d+1)/2$. This question asks the second one, and the answer happens to
   be that a larger $d$ raises the polynomial's degree, which is why the
   two statements point the same way here. They do not always: above
   threshold the first reverses sign while the second does not.

   </details>

## Summary

- Decoding is the problem of inferring the most probable physical error
  from an observed syndrome, and for the surface code this reduces
  to minimum-weight perfect matching on a graph of defects.
- A single-qubit error is one graph edge; a connected chain of errors
  only shows defects at its two *endpoints*, since interior vertices get
  touched (and re-flipped to even) twice.
- Worked directly on a deliberately simplified toy lattice, whose own
  counts are named where it is introduced rather than borrowed from the
  prerequisite: a 1-qubit and a 2-qubit chain both
  decode correctly (the second with a harmless, checked degeneracy). A
  2-qubit chain spanning the toy's middle row creates a
  decoding **tie**: pairing the defects costs 2, and matching each to
  its nearby boundary costs 1 + 1 = 2. Broken the wrong way, the tie
  completes the chain into a boundary-to-boundary logical operator, so
  a logical failure needs at least $\lceil d/2 \rceil$ errors.
- Below threshold $p_{th}$ (real, decoder- and noise-model-dependent,
  roughly percent-level), $p_L \sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$: logical error
  rate falls exponentially with distance, the entire basis for scaling
  fault-tolerant quantum computing by building bigger patches. Above
  threshold, bigger patches get worse, not better.
- Decoder speed is a live, unsolved engineering problem, not a footnote:
  MWPM must run fast enough for real hardware's correction-cycle rate,
  which is why Union-Find and neural-network decoders are active
  current research, not settled alternatives.
