## A Probability Without Its Error Bar

A headline says a quantum computer measured a probability of $0.85$ for
some outcome. Is that impressive? It depends entirely on information the
headline just left out: which exact circuit, on which exact device (and
how well-calibrated it was that day), after what classical
post-processing, and, the question this lesson answers concretely, based
on how many actual runs. Evaluating Quantum Advantage Claims built the
habit of checking a claim's classical baseline. This lesson builds the
other half of that habit: knowing what has to be specified for anyone
else to check the result at all, and being able to tell from the shot
count alone whether a reported number is statistically meaningful. The
second half of the lesson turns the critique around and designs a
complete, decidable benchmark experiment from scratch.

## The Four Components of a Reproducible Claim

A quantum-computing experimental claim is reproducible only if
it specifies all four of the following. Missing any one of them means a
second team, handed only the paper, cannot actually check the result.

1. **The exact circuit.** Not "we ran a variational circuit" or "we used
   QAOA," but the actual gate sequence, which qubits it acts on, and in
   what order. QAOA: A Worked Max-Cut Example didn't just say "QAOA
   finds good cuts"; it specified the exact graph, the exact number of
   layers ($p=1$), and the exact angles ($\gamma\approx0.6,\
   \beta\approx0.3$) a grid search found. Without that level of detail,
   "we ran QAOA" describes a family of circuits, not one experiment.
2. **The exact hardware or simulator, including calibration data if real
   hardware was used.** Calibration established that a real device's
   physical error rates (gate fidelities, $T_1$/$T_2$ times, readout
   asymmetry) drift day to day from temperature fluctuations and slow
   electronic drift, and that a calibration is only valid until the next
   recalibration cycle. A result reported from "IBM's 127-qubit device"
   with no calibration snapshot or date attached is not fully
   reproducible: the *same physical device*, one week later, can
   perform differently. A simulator claim needs the same
   specificity in kind: which simulator, which noise model (if any),
   which version.
3. **The exact classical post-processing / error-mitigation pipeline.**
   Quantum Error Mitigation showed that the *same* raw circuit
   execution can report substantially different numbers depending on
   what was done to it afterward. That lesson's own worked example
   turned a raw $80\%$ measured $|1\rangle$ probability into a
   readout-corrected $94\%$, a $14$-point swing from post-processing
   alone, before zero-noise extrapolation is even considered. A claim
   that doesn't state whether readout-error correction, zero-noise
   extrapolation, or nothing at all was applied is comparing an unknown
   quantity to whatever baseline you assume.
4. **Honest statistical uncertainty reporting.** The subject of the rest
   of this lesson: a single central number, with no shot count and no
   confidence interval, doesn't say whether the result is a precise
   measurement or a coin flip that happened to land favorably.

## Shot Noise, Quantified

Suppose a circuit is run $N$ times ("shots") and a specific outcome is
observed $k$ of those times. The natural estimate of that outcome's true
probability is

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep annotation="The natural estimate of the true probability, from k successes out of N shots.">
$$
\hat p = \frac{k}{N}
$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Each run is an independent Bernoulli trial with true probability p, so k is binomially distributed with mean Np and variance Np(1-p), a standard fact about the binomial distribution that is taken on citation here.">
$$
k\sim\text{Binomial}(N,p), \qquad \text{Var}(k) = Np(1-p)
$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Dividing by the constant N to get p̂ = k/N scales the variance by 1/N², since Var(cX) = c²Var(X) for a constant c.">
$$
\text{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{\text{Var}(k)}{N^2} = \frac{Np(1-p)}{N^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{N}
$$
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="The standard error is the standard deviation of the estimator p̂ itself, not of a single trial: the square root of the variance just derived.">
$$
\text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}}
$$
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

This is the single most important formula in this lesson: it says the
uncertainty on an estimated probability shrinks as $1/\sqrt N$, not as
$1/N$. Quadrupling the shot count only halves the uncertainty. For large
$N$, the binomial distribution is well approximated by a normal
distribution (central limit theorem), which justifies the standard
**95% confidence interval** approximation

$$
\hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}(\hat p)
$$

<Callout type="warning">
**Where this formula stops describing a real experiment.** $\text{SE}(\hat
p)$ accounts for one error source only: the randomness of sampling a
fixed distribution $N$ times. It says nothing about whether the
distribution being sampled is the one the paper claims. Calibration
drift, readout bias, crosstalk, and leakage all shift $p$ itself, and
none of them shrink with $N$. Past some shot count, a reported
uncertainty is dominated by that systematic floor rather than by shot
noise, and a paper quoting a $10^{-4}$ statistical error bar on a device
whose calibration moved between the first and last shot is quoting the
wrong number. The honest form of the claim states both: a statistical
uncertainty from the shot count, and a systematic uncertainty estimated
some other way, typically by repeating the whole experiment after a
recalibration and seeing how far the answer moves.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
The $1.96$ factor is the two-sided 95% critical value of the standard
normal distribution, with nothing quantum-specific about it. The same
formula applies to a poll, an A/B test, or a coin-flip experiment; the
physics only enters in what $p$ *means* (a measured circuit outcome
probability) and in what determines its true value ($p$ itself is a
prediction of quantum mechanics, not a nuisance parameter).
</Callout>

## What Separates a Claim From an Anecdote

<InsightBlock>
  A scientific claim, as opposed to an anecdote, states enough about how
  a result was obtained that someone else could check it, and enough
  about its statistical uncertainty that they would know how much to
  trust it even without checking.
</InsightBlock>

The four components above are what "enough" means for a quantum-computing
experiment specifically; the standard-error formula is what "how much to
trust the number" means quantitatively. Neither is quantum-specific in
its logic. Anyone who can list what a reproducible claim needs, and can
reason from a shot count to a confidence interval, can read a drug trial's
effect size or a machine-learning benchmark the same way. The
physics-specific machinery this platform built, circuits and noise and
error mitigation, becomes a research skill only when it is paired with
the discipline to report and question results honestly.

## The Same Number, Two Different Claims

### Shot noise: the same number, two very different claims

**Two papers report a measured probability of $p=0.85$ for their
circuit's target outcome. Paper A ran $N=1000$ shots; Paper B ran
$N=20$ shots. Compute the standard error and 95% confidence interval for
each, and compare.**

Using $\text{SE}=\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$ with $p=0.85$, so $p(1-p)=0.1275$:

**Paper A ($N=1000$):**

$$
\text{SE}_A = \sqrt{\frac{0.1275}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0001275} \approx 0.0113
$$

$$
p = 0.85 \pm 0.0113\ (1\sigma), \qquad 95\%\ \text{CI} \approx 0.85 \pm 1.96(0.0113) \approx 0.85 \pm 0.022 \approx [0.828,\ 0.872]
$$

**Paper B ($N=20$):**

$$
\text{SE}_B = \sqrt{\frac{0.1275}{20}} = \sqrt{0.006375} \approx 0.0798
$$

$$
p = 0.85 \pm 0.0798\ (1\sigma), \qquad 95\%\ \text{CI} \approx 0.85 \pm 1.96(0.0798) \approx 0.85 \pm 0.156 \approx [0.694,\ 1.006]
$$

Paper B's standard error is $\sqrt{1000/20}=\sqrt{50}$ times Paper A's,
and dividing the two computed values gives {seRatio.toFixed(4)}
(`seRatio`), which is $\sqrt{50}$ to every digit shown, exactly as
$\text{SE}\propto 1/\sqrt N$ requires. Paper B's 95% interval even pokes
slightly above $1$ (a reminder that the normal approximation underlying
the $\pm1.96\,\text{SE}$ formula is itself only an approximation, least
accurate near $p$ close to $0$ or $1$ with small $N$). The identical
reported number, $p=0.85$, means something close to "we're confident
it's between $0.83$ and $0.87$" in Paper A, and something closer to "it's
probably somewhere between $0.7$ and $1$, we really can't say much more
precisely" in Paper B. **The shot count is not a footnote; it determines
whether the headline number means anything at all.**

### Design exercise: a complete benchmark for a QAOA claim

**Claim to test:** "$p=1$ QAOA, at the grid-searched angles
$\gamma\approx0.6,\ \beta\approx0.3$, genuinely outperforms random
guessing on triangle Max-Cut." QAOA: A Worked Max-Cut Example already
computed the *theoretical* expectation value at those angles exactly,
`expectedCutSize`$\,\approx 1.999$, against a random-guessing baseline of
$|E|/2=1.5$ and a true maximum of $2$. A real experiment doesn't get to
read off the theoretical expectation value directly. It has to *measure*
it by sampling. Here is a complete design:

1. **Circuit:** exactly the triangle-graph QAOA circuit from that lesson
   ($p=1$, edges $(0,1),(1,2),(0,2)$, angles $\gamma\approx0.6,\
   \beta\approx0.3$). That is component 1 of a reproducible claim, already
   satisfied by reusing a fully specified circuit rather than a vague
   description.
2. **Run it for $N=1000$ shots.** For each shot, record the measured
   cut value $C_i$. The pigeonhole argument in that lesson's worked
   example narrows the possibilities further than you might expect: two
   of the three vertices always share a color, so the edge between them
   is never cut, and a triangle's cut value is therefore always $0$ or
   $2$, never $1$ and never $3$. Compute the sample mean
   $\bar C = \frac1N\sum_i C_i$.
3. **Compute the standard error of $\bar C$.** Because $C$ takes only
   the values $0$ and $2$, $C/2$ is a Bernoulli variable and the formula
   just derived applies after rescaling:
   $\text{SE}(\bar C) = 2\sqrt{q(1-q)/N}$, where $q$ is the probability
   of measuring a cut of $2$. Designing the experiment means bounding
   that *before* knowing $q$, and $q(1-q)\le 1/4$ gives the worst case,
   $\sigma_C \le 1$, so

$$
\text{SE}(\bar C) = \frac{\sigma_C}{\sqrt N} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1000}} \approx 0.0316
$$

   the same $1/\sqrt N$ shrinkage the Bernoulli case had: the worst-case
   standard error at $N=1000$ shots is at most about $0.032$, whatever
   $q$ turns out to be. That $\sigma_C\le 1$ bound is also what
   Popoviciu's inequality, $\sigma\le(\max-\min)/2$, supplies for *any*
   distribution confined to $[0,2]$, which is the version to reach for
   when an experiment's outcome values are not two-valued and no
   Bernoulli rescaling is available. Popoviciu's inequality is quoted
   here, not proved.
4. **Decision rule, stated in advance:**
   - **Confirms the claim** if $\bar C$ falls within about $2\,\text{SE}$
     ({qaoaGapToTheory.toFixed(3)}, `qaoaGapToTheory`) of the theoretical
     prediction $1.999$, i.e. roughly $\bar C \gtrsim 1.94$, **and** sits
     statistically significantly above the random baseline $1.5$. The
     second test is the easy one: the gap from the theoretical value down
     to the baseline is {qaoaGapToBaseline.toFixed(3)}
     (`qaoaGapToBaseline`), about
     {(qaoaGapToBaseline / qaoaSEBound).toFixed(0)} times the worst-case
     standard error, so any measured mean near the theoretical value is
     far from random guessing.
   - **Refutes or calls the claim into question** if $\bar C$ is not
     significantly above $1.5$ (e.g. lands within a couple of standard
     errors of the baseline), or if $\bar C$ differs from $1.999$ by far
     more than the computed standard error bound would allow by chance.
     Either outcome would mean the circuit was not implementing the
     claimed angles, or the claimed theoretical value does not describe
     what the hardware or simulator actually produced.
   - **Inconclusive** if $N$ were too small for the standard error to
     rule out either outcome, which is the Paper-B failure mode above,
     scaled to this experiment: a claim run at, say, $N=20$ shots here
     would have a standard error bound of $1/\sqrt{20}\approx 0.224$,
     wide enough that even $\bar C = 1.6$ (barely above baseline)
     couldn't be confidently distinguished from the theoretical $1.999$.

This is what "designing a benchmark," not just critiquing one, actually
looks like: a fully specified circuit, a stated shot count with its
resulting uncertainty computed *before* looking at the data, and an
explicit rule for what counts as confirmation versus refutation. That is
every one of the four components from this lesson's opening framework,
used together.

## Reporting a Number Without Its Conditions

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating a single reported number, with no shot count or confidence
interval, as a complete scientific claim, regardless of how impressive
it sounds.** "We measured $p=0.85$" is not, by itself, a statement
anyone can evaluate. As this lesson's worked example showed, the exact
same central value can come from a precise measurement (SE $\approx
0.011$ at $N=1000$) or from a measurement barely better than a guess
about its own uncertainty (SE $\approx 0.080$ at $N=20$). A complete
claim states the shot count and the resulting uncertainty every time,
not just the number that looks good.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming "more decimal places" means "more precision."** Reporting
$p=0.8534$ instead of $p=0.85$ says nothing about statistical confidence
by itself. Decimal places describe the *arithmetic* of $k/N$, not the
*uncertainty* in the estimate. A number reported to four decimal places
from $N=20$ shots is not more trustworthy than one reported to two
decimal places from $N=10{,}000$ shots; if anything it's a mild red flag,
since it suggests the reporter didn't think about (or hasn't computed)
the actual standard error.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A claim reports $p=0.6$ from $N=400$ shots. Compute $\text{SE}(\hat
   p)$ and the 95% confidence interval. Is this claim's precision closer
   to this lesson's $N=1000$ example or its $N=20$ example?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{0.6\times0.4}{400}} = \sqrt{0.0006} = 0.0245,
   $$

   $$
   95\%\ \text{CI} \approx 0.6 \pm 1.96(0.0245) = 0.6 \pm 0.048 = [0.552,\ 0.648].
   $$

   Closer to the $N=1000$ example. Its standard error is about twice that
   example's $0.0113$ and about a third of the $N=20$ example's $0.0798$,
   so on both an absolute and a ratio comparison it sits nearer the
   precise end.

   **One effect worth separating out, since two things changed at once.**
   The shot count is not the only difference; $p$ moved too, and
   $p(1-p)$ is largest at $p=0.5$. At $p=0.6$ that factor is $0.24$
   against $0.1275$ at $p=0.85$, so an estimate near a half-half split is
   intrinsically noisier by $\sqrt{0.24/0.1275}=1.37$ at the same shot
   count. Concretely, $213$ shots at $p=0.85$ would give the same
   precision as $400$ shots at $p=0.6$. Precision is bought with shots,
   and how much a shot buys depends on where the true probability sits.

   **Whether $\pm0.048$ is good enough is not a statistical question.**
   It depends entirely on what the interval has to separate. If the claim
   is "better than chance," the interval sits wholly above $0.5$ and the
   result is decisive. If the claim is "our measured $0.60$ agrees with
   the predicted $0.60$ and rules out the competing prediction of
   $0.63$," it is worthless, because both predictions are inside the
   interval. The same number is strong evidence and no evidence at all
   depending on the hypothesis, which is why a confidence interval has to
   be read next to the claim rather than admired on its own.

   </details>

2. Suppose you wanted to shrink the $N=20$ example's standard error
   ($\approx 0.080$) down to match the $N=1000$ example's
   ($\approx 0.011$) by increasing shots alone (keeping $p=0.85$ fixed).
   Roughly how many shots would you need? (Use $\text{SE}\propto
   1/\sqrt N$; an order-of-magnitude estimate showing you understand the
   scaling is enough.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $N=1000$, which is the point of the exercise: the answer is the other
   example, because the two share $p$ and differ only in shot count.

   The scaling does it in one line. Since $\text{SE}\propto1/\sqrt N$,
   reducing the error by a factor $r$ costs a factor $r^2$ in shots, and
   here $r = 0.080/0.011 \approx 7.1$, so $r^2 \approx 50$, giving
   $20\times50=1000$.

   **The number worth memorizing is the exponent, not the answer.** One
   more decimal digit of precision is a tenfold reduction in standard
   error and therefore a **hundredfold** increase in shots. Precision in
   a sampled quantity is bought at quadratic cost, permanently, and no
   improvement in gate fidelity or qubit count changes that exponent.

   Carry it into real numbers and it stops being an abstraction. At a
   circuit repetition rate around $10$ kHz, which is a reasonable figure
   for a superconducting device, $1000$ shots take a tenth of a second.
   Reaching $\text{SE}=10^{-4}$ at $p=0.85$ needs
   $N = 0.1275/10^{-8} = 1.3\times10^{7}$ shots, about $21$ minutes.
   Reaching $10^{-5}$ needs $1.3\times10^{9}$ shots, about $35$ hours,
   for one number.

   That is the same wall the Quantum Simulation of Molecules lesson runs
   into from the other side. VQE needs energies at chemical accuracy, and
   the measurement cost of estimating them is exactly this $1/\epsilon^2$
   scaling multiplied by the number of Hamiltonian terms. The reason
   published VQE resource estimates report measurement times in days is
   this formula, not any shortcoming in the hardware.

   </details>

3. A paper claims its quantum circuit's target-outcome probability
   "exceeds 90%," citing only that single number with no shot count.
   List, in order, the four components from this lesson that are
   missing or unverifiable from that sentence alone, and explain
   concretely what additional information would need to be reported for
   each.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   All four are missing. Taking them in the order this lesson introduced
   them:

   **1. The exact circuit.** "Its quantum circuit" names a family, not an
   experiment. What is needed: the gate sequence, which physical qubits
   each gate acts on, the ordering, and every numerical parameter,
   including any angles a classical optimizer landed on. Without those,
   nobody can attempt the experiment, so the claim is not reproducible in
   the plainest sense of the word.

   **2. The hardware or simulator, with calibration.** What is needed:
   the device, the date, and a calibration snapshot with per-gate
   fidelities, $T_1$ and $T_2$, and readout error rates, since the same
   device a week later is a different device. For a simulator: which one,
   which version, and which noise model, or an explicit statement that
   the simulation was noiseless, which would make the $90\%$ a property
   of the algorithm rather than of any machine.

   **3. The post-processing and mitigation pipeline.** This is the one
   that can move the reported number the most, and the lesson's own
   worked example is the demonstration: an $80\%$ raw measurement became
   $94\%$ after readout correction alone, a $14$-point swing with no
   change to the quantum execution. So "exceeds $90\%$" is consistent
   with a raw result in the seventies. What is needed: the raw number
   alongside the corrected one, and a statement of exactly which
   corrections were applied, in what order, with what calibration data.

   **4. Statistical uncertainty.** What is needed: the shot count $N$,
   the raw success count $k$, and a confidence interval. The reason this
   matters is not pedantic. Eighteen successes out of twenty gives
   $\hat p = 0.9$ with $\text{SE}=0.067$ and a $95\%$ interval reaching
   down to $0.77$, so a true probability well under $0.8$ is entirely
   consistent with the observation. Nine thousand out of ten thousand
   gives the same $\hat p$ with $\text{SE}=0.003$ and an interval of
   $[0.894, 0.906]$. Identical sentence, completely different claim.

   **And a fifth point that the phrasing itself invites.** "Exceeds
   $90\%$" is a one-sided claim, so what supports it is the *lower* end
   of the confidence interval, not the point estimate. At $9000/10000$
   the lower bound is $0.894$, which does not exceed $0.90$, so even ten
   thousand shots landing exactly on $0.9$ fail to support the sentence
   as written. Supporting it needs a point estimate meaningfully above
   the threshold, and reporting the estimate alone quietly substitutes a
   two-sided quantity for a one-sided question.

   Worth being fair about what this does and does not indict. A single
   summary sentence in an abstract is not where any of this belongs, and
   the four components may well all be in the methods section or a
   supplement. The failure is only a failure if they are nowhere. What is
   never acceptable is a number reported to two significant figures with
   no shot count anywhere in the paper, because the digits are then
   claiming a precision the data may not contain.

   </details>

## Summary

- A reproducible quantum-computing claim needs all four of:
  the exact circuit, the exact hardware/simulator with calibration data
  (Calibration established that real-device performance drifts day to
  day), the exact classical post-processing/error-mitigation pipeline
  (Quantum Error Mitigation), and honest statistical uncertainty
  reporting.
- Shot noise: estimating a probability from $N$ shots carries a standard
  error $\text{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$, shrinking as $1/\sqrt N$.
  Worked numerically: $p=0.85$ from $N=1000$ gives
  $\text{SE}\approx0.011$; the identical $p=0.85$ from only $N=20$ gives
  $\text{SE}\approx0.080$, about $7\times$ larger.
- A complete benchmark design states the circuit, the shot count and its
  resulting standard error computed in advance, and an explicit,
  stated-before-the-fact rule for what result would confirm versus
  refute the claim, illustrated end to end for a QAOA Max-Cut claim
  above, including the worst-case bound that lets a shot count be fixed
  before the outcome distribution is known.
- "A single number with no shot count" is not a complete claim, no
  matter how good the number looks.
