## Motivation

A headline says a quantum computer measured a probability of $0.85$ for
some outcome. Is that impressive? It depends entirely on information the
headline just left out: which exact circuit, on which exact device (and
how well-calibrated it was that day), after what classical
post-processing, and — the question this lesson answers concretely —
based on how many actual runs. Evaluating Quantum Advantage Claims built
the habit of checking a claim's classical baseline. This lesson builds
the other half of the same habit: knowing exactly what has to be
specified for anyone else to check the result at all, and being able to
tell, from the shot count alone, whether a reported number is even
statistically meaningful. By the end, you'll design a complete,
decidable benchmark experiment yourself, not just critique someone
else's.

## The Four Components of a Reproducible Claim

A quantum-computing experimental claim is genuinely reproducible only if
it specifies all four of the following. Missing any one of them means a
second team, handed only the paper, cannot actually check the result.

1. **The exact circuit.** Not "we ran a variational circuit" or "we used
   QAOA" — the actual gate sequence, which qubits it acts on, and in
   what order. QAOA: A Worked Max-Cut Example didn't just say "QAOA
   finds good cuts"; it specified the exact graph, the exact number of
   layers ($p=1$), and the exact angles ($\gamma\approx0.6,\
   \beta\approx0.3$) a grid search found. Without that level of detail,
   "we ran QAOA" describes a family of circuits, not one experiment.
2. **The exact hardware or simulator, including calibration data if real
   hardware was used.** Calibration established that a real device's
   physical error rates — gate fidelities, $T_1$/$T_2$ times, readout
   asymmetry — drift day to day from temperature fluctuations and slow
   electronic drift, and that a calibration is only valid until the next
   recalibration cycle. A result reported from "IBM's 127-qubit device"
   with no calibration snapshot or date attached is not fully
   reproducible: the *same physical device*, one week later, can
   genuinely perform differently. A simulator claim needs the same
   specificity in kind — which simulator, which noise model (if any),
   which version.
3. **The exact classical post-processing / error-mitigation pipeline.**
   Quantum Error Mitigation showed that the *same* raw circuit
   execution can report substantially different numbers depending on
   what was done to it afterward — that lesson's own worked example
   turned a raw $80\%$ measured $|1\rangle$ probability into a
   readout-corrected $94\%$, a $14$-point swing from post-processing
   alone, before zero-noise extrapolation is even considered. A claim
   that doesn't state whether readout-error correction, zero-noise
   extrapolation, or nothing at all was applied is comparing an unknown
   quantity to whatever baseline you assume.
4. **Honest statistical uncertainty reporting.** The subject of the rest
   of this lesson: a single central number, with no shot count and no
   confidence interval, doesn't say whether the result is a precise
   measurement or a coin flip that happened to land favorably.

## Mathematical Development

Suppose a circuit is run $N$ times ("shots") and a specific outcome is
observed $k$ of those times. The natural estimate of that outcome's true
probability is

$$
\hat p = \frac{k}{N}
$$

Each run is an independent Bernoulli trial (outcome happens or doesn't,
with true probability $p$), so $k$ is binomially distributed,
$k\sim\text{Binomial}(N,p)$, with mean $Np$ and variance $Np(1-p)$.
Dividing by the constant $N$ to get $\hat p = k/N$ scales the variance by
$1/N^2$:

$$
\text{Var}(\hat p) = \frac{\text{Var}(k)}{N^2} = \frac{Np(1-p)}{N^2} = \frac{p(1-p)}{N}
$$

so the **standard error** — the standard deviation of the estimator
$\hat p$ itself, not of a single trial — is

$$
\text{SE}(\hat p) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}}
$$

This is the single most important formula in this lesson: it says the
uncertainty on an estimated probability shrinks as $1/\sqrt N$, not as
$1/N$. Quadrupling the shot count only halves the uncertainty. For large
$N$, the binomial distribution is well approximated by a normal
distribution (central limit theorem), which justifies the standard
**95% confidence interval** approximation

$$
\hat p \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}(\hat p)
$$

<Callout type="note">
The $1.96$ factor is the two-sided 95% critical value of the standard
normal distribution — nothing quantum-specific about it. The same
formula applies to a poll, an A/B test, or a coin-flip experiment; the
physics only enters in what $p$ *means* (a measured circuit outcome
probability) and in what determines its true value ($p$ itself is a
prediction of quantum mechanics, not a nuisance parameter).
</Callout>

## Physical Interpretation: Why This Matters

None of this is bureaucratic box-checking. A scientific claim, as
opposed to an anecdote, is exactly one that states enough about *how* a
result was obtained that someone else could check it and would know how
much to trust the number even without checking it themselves. The four
components above are what "enough" means for a quantum-computing
experiment specifically; the standard-error formula is what "how much to
trust the number" means quantitatively. A student who can list what a
reproducible claim needs, and can correctly reason from a shot count to
a confidence interval, has a skill that transfers directly to reading
*any* experimental claim — a new drug's effect size, a machine-learning
benchmark, a poll result — not just quantum-computing papers. That
transferability is precisely why this lesson exists as the second-to-last
stop in the whole curriculum: the physics-specific machinery this
platform built (circuits, noise, error mitigation) only becomes a
*research skill* once it's paired with the discipline to report and
question results honestly.

## Worked Example

### Shot noise: the same number, two very different claims

**Two papers report a measured probability of $p=0.85$ for their
circuit's target outcome. Paper A ran $N=1000$ shots; Paper B ran
$N=20$ shots. Compute the standard error and 95% confidence interval for
each, and compare.**

Using $\text{SE}=\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$ with $p=0.85$, so $p(1-p)=0.1275$:

**Paper A ($N=1000$):**

$$
\text{SE}_A = \sqrt{\frac{0.1275}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0001275} \approx 0.0113
$$

$$
p = 0.85 \pm 0.0113\ (1\sigma), \qquad 95\%\ \text{CI} \approx 0.85 \pm 1.96(0.0113) \approx 0.85 \pm 0.022 \approx [0.828,\ 0.872]
$$

**Paper B ($N=20$):**

$$
\text{SE}_B = \sqrt{\frac{0.1275}{20}} = \sqrt{0.006375} \approx 0.0798
$$

$$
p = 0.85 \pm 0.0798\ (1\sigma), \qquad 95\%\ \text{CI} \approx 0.85 \pm 1.96(0.0798) \approx 0.85 \pm 0.156 \approx [0.694,\ 1.006]
$$

Paper B's standard error is $\sqrt{1000/20}=\sqrt{50}\approx 7.07\times$
larger than Paper A's — matching $0.0798/0.0113\approx 7.06$ exactly,
since $\text{SE}\propto 1/\sqrt N$. Paper B's 95% interval even pokes
slightly above $1$ (a reminder that the normal approximation underlying
the $\pm1.96\,\text{SE}$ formula is itself only an approximation, least
accurate near $p$ close to $0$ or $1$ with small $N$). The identical
reported number, $p=0.85$, means something close to "we're confident
it's between $0.83$ and $0.87$" in Paper A, and something closer to "it's
probably somewhere between $0.7$ and $1$, we really can't say much more
precisely" in Paper B. **The shot count is not a footnote; it determines
whether the headline number means anything at all.**

### Design exercise: a complete benchmark for a QAOA claim

**Claim to test:** "$p=1$ QAOA, at the grid-searched angles
$\gamma\approx0.6,\ \beta\approx0.3$, genuinely outperforms random
guessing on triangle Max-Cut." QAOA: A Worked Max-Cut Example already
computed the *theoretical* expectation value at those angles exactly,
`expectedCutSize`$\,\approx 1.999$, against a random-guessing baseline of
$|E|/2=1.5$ and a true maximum of $2$. A real experiment doesn't get to
read off the theoretical expectation value directly — it has to *measure*
it by sampling. Here is a complete design:

1. **Circuit:** exactly the triangle-graph QAOA circuit from that lesson
   ($p=1$, edges $(0,1),(1,2),(0,2)$, angles $\gamma\approx0.6,\
   \beta\approx0.3$) — component 1 of a reproducible claim, already
   satisfied by reusing a fully specified circuit rather than a vague
   description.
2. **Run it for $N=1000$ shots.** For each shot, record the measured
   cut value $C_i\in\{0,1,2\}$ (a triangle can never have all 3 edges cut,
   established by the pigeonhole argument in that lesson's worked
   example). Compute the sample mean $\bar C = \frac1N\sum_i C_i$.
3. **Compute the standard error of $\bar C$.** $C$ is no longer a
   simple 0/1 Bernoulli outcome, so the exact variance of the cut-value
   distribution would need the full measurement distribution to pin down
   precisely — but a rigorous, computable-without-that bound is enough
   to design the experiment: since $C$ is confined to $[0,2]$,
   Popoviciu's inequality bounds its standard deviation by
   $\sigma_C \le (\max-\min)/2 = 1$, so

$$
\text{SE}(\bar C) = \frac{\sigma_C}{\sqrt N} \le \frac{1}{\sqrt{1000}} \approx 0.0316
$$

   (the same $1/\sqrt N$ shrinkage as the Bernoulli case, generalized —
   the worst-case standard error at $N=1000$ shots is at most about
   $0.032$, regardless of the exact shape of the cut-value
   distribution).
4. **Decision rule, stated in advance:**
   - **Confirms the claim** if $\bar C$ falls within about $2\,\text{SE}$
     ($\approx 0.063$) of the theoretical prediction $1.999$ — i.e.
     roughly $\bar C \gtrsim 1.94$ — **and** is unambiguously,
     statistically significantly above the random baseline $1.5$ (the
     gap between $1.999$ and $1.5$ is $0.499$, more than $15\times$ the
     worst-case standard error, so any measured mean anywhere near the
     theoretical value is trivially distinguishable from random
     guessing).
   - **Refutes or calls the claim into question** if $\bar C$ is not
     significantly above $1.5$ (e.g. lands within a couple of standard
     errors of the baseline), or if $\bar C$ differs from $1.999$ by far
     more than the computed standard error bound would allow by chance
     — either result would mean either the circuit wasn't actually
     implementing the claimed angles, or the claimed theoretical value
     doesn't describe what the hardware or simulator actually produced.
   - **Inconclusive** if $N$ were too small for the standard error to
     rule out either outcome — exactly the Paper-B failure mode above,
     scaled to this experiment: a claim run at, say, $N=20$ shots here
     would have a standard error bound of $1/\sqrt{20}\approx 0.224$,
     wide enough that even $\bar C = 1.6$ (barely above baseline)
     couldn't be confidently distinguished from the theoretical $1.999$.

This is what "designing a benchmark," not just critiquing one, actually
looks like: a fully specified circuit, a stated shot count with its
resulting uncertainty computed *before* looking at the data, and an
explicit rule for what counts as confirmation versus refutation — every
one of the four components from this lesson's opening framework, used
together.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating a single reported number, with no shot count or confidence
interval, as a complete scientific claim — regardless of how impressive
it sounds.** "We measured $p=0.85$" is not, by itself, a statement
anyone can evaluate. As this lesson's worked example showed, the exact
same central value can come from a precise measurement (SE $\approx
0.011$ at $N=1000$) or from a measurement barely better than a guess
about its own uncertainty (SE $\approx 0.080$ at $N=20$). A complete
claim states the shot count and the resulting uncertainty every time,
not just the number that looks good.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming "more decimal places" means "more precision."** Reporting
$p=0.8534$ instead of $p=0.85$ says nothing about statistical confidence
by itself — decimal places describe the *arithmetic* of $k/N$, not the
*uncertainty* in the estimate. A number reported to four decimal places
from $N=20$ shots is not more trustworthy than one reported to two
decimal places from $N=10{,}000$ shots; if anything it's a mild red flag,
since it suggests the reporter didn't think about (or hasn't computed)
the actual standard error.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A genuinely reproducible quantum-computing claim needs all four of:
  the exact circuit, the exact hardware/simulator with calibration data
  (Calibration established that real-device performance drifts day to
  day), the exact classical post-processing/error-mitigation pipeline
  (Quantum Error Mitigation), and honest statistical uncertainty
  reporting.
- Shot noise: estimating a probability from $N$ shots carries a standard
  error $\text{SE}(\hat p)=\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$, shrinking as $1/\sqrt N$.
  Worked numerically: $p=0.85$ from $N=1000$ gives
  $\text{SE}\approx0.011$; the identical $p=0.85$ from only $N=20$ gives
  $\text{SE}\approx0.080$, about $7\times$ larger.
- A complete benchmark design states the circuit, the shot count and its
  resulting standard error computed in advance, and an explicit,
  stated-before-the-fact rule for what result would confirm versus
  refute the claim — illustrated end to end for a QAOA Max-Cut claim
  above, including a Popoviciu-inequality bound generalizing the
  standard-error formula beyond simple 0/1 outcomes.
- "A single number with no shot count" is not a complete claim, no
  matter how good the number looks.
