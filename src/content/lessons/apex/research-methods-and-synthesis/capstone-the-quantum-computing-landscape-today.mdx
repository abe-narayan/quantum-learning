## A Lesson With No Next Lesson

Until now, each lesson has opened by connecting to what came
immediately before it. This one is different: it opens by looking back
across all of them at once.

The path is worth actually retracing rather than just gesturing at,
because the distance covered is the whole reason this lesson can attempt
what it attempts.

**Quantum Mechanics** started from almost nothing: vector spaces, inner
products, and the plain observation that classical probability cannot
explain interference. Out of that came the four postulates that are
still, to this day, the entire mathematical content of the theory. A
state is a normalized vector. An observable is a Hermitian operator. A
measurement is a probabilistic collapse onto an eigenspace. Time
evolution is a unitary flow generated by the Hamiltonian. By the time
the hydrogen atom and the quantum harmonic oscillator were on the
table, "quantum mechanics" had stopped being a philosophical curiosity
and turned into a machine you could compute with.

That machine got shrunk down to something small and combinatorial in
**Quantum Computing**: a qubit turned out to be nothing but a two-level
quantum system, a gate nothing but a unitary, entanglement nothing but a
state the tensor-product structure refuses to factor. That was already
enough to build the reason a quantum computer might be worth
constructing at all (Grover's quadratic speedup, Shor's exponential
one), plus a first, conceptual pass at the error correction either one
would eventually need to survive on real, noisy hardware.

Then came the question those algorithms leave dangling: built out of
*what*, physically? **Quantum Hardware** answered it four different
ways. Superconducting transmons, trapped ions, neutral atoms, and
photonic qubits are each an engineered two-level system standing in for
a postulate. That track was also frank, from its first lesson on
decoherence onward, about what the engineering costs: $T_1$ and $T_2$
times, crosstalk, and the compounding, multiplicative arithmetic of
Scaling Challenges' own $p^N$, which is what makes "just add more
qubits" such a dramatically incomplete plan.

Somebody still has to turn an algorithm written on paper into a circuit
an actual device (or a simulator standing in for one) can run. That is
what **Quantum Software** built: state-vector and tensor-network
simulation, compilation down to a real hardware graph's native gate
set, and the hybrid classical-quantum loops that are how near-term
noisy hardware is actually used today, imperfections and all.

**Quantum Mastery** then went back through all of it a second time,
now with a mathematician's insistence on proof rather than
plausibility: rigorous Hilbert-space theory in place of
finite-dimensional intuition, the actual quantum information theory
sitting underneath the word "entanglement," and quantum algorithms and
complexity pushed to the scale and precision a research paper, rather
than a textbook exercise, demands.

And then **APEX**, which took every one of those threads to research
depth at once. Algorithmic Frontiers showed that the individual
algorithms Quantum Computing built one at a time (Grover's search,
phase estimation, Hamiltonian simulation) are one construction,
the quantum singular value transformation, wearing different clothes.
Fault Tolerance Frontiers carried error correction from a conceptual
introduction to a real 2D surface-code lattice, a real decoder, lattice
surgery, magic-state distillation, and a full physical-qubit resource
estimate. Quantum Complexity Theory pushed algorithms and complexity to
QMA, the Local Hamiltonian problem, and query lower bounds, and along
the way built the very three-tier
**proven / strongly-evidenced-conjecture / genuinely-open** framework
this lesson is about to reuse at the scale of the whole field.
Simulation & Compilation Frontiers pushed compilation and complexity
theory to the actual boundary of classical simulability, and to quantum
chemistry, the single most concretely useful application anyone has
proposed. And **Research Methods and Synthesis**, this very course,
took every one of those results and asked the only question left once
you're done learning: *now that you know all of this, how do you
evaluate something new?*

That question is this lesson's entire content.

## The State of the Field

Quantum Complexity Theory's capstone built a three-tier framework for
this purpose: **proven theorems**, established by complete mathematical
proof; **strongly-evidenced conjectures**, believed on specific,
namable evidence but not proven; and **genuinely open questions**,
where no decisive evidence points either way. There it classified
results *within* complexity theory. This section reuses the framework,
unchanged, and widens it to the whole field: what hardware has actually
built, what algorithms actually deliver, what error correction has
actually demonstrated, and where simulation and chemistry stand.

<Callout type="note">
  Two notes before the map itself. First, "current" here means current
  as of the moment these lessons were written, a fact the closing
  section treats as a feature to plan for, not a flaw to apologize for.
  Second, some cells below use deliberately calibrated, hedged language
  (*"in the best reported cases,"* *"on the order of,"* *"more than one
  group"*) rather than a single precise number, because a single
  confidently-stated number is often the first thing a badly-calibrated
  headline gets wrong.
</Callout>

| Domain | Proven | Strongly-evidenced (not proven) | Genuinely open |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hardware** | Multiple physical platforms (superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonics) can implement individually-characterized, coherent single- and two-qubit gates; this is the postulates of quantum mechanics engineered into hardware, not a theoretical possibility. | The best reported two-qubit gate fidelities in leading platforms are, under optimized conditions, comfortably into the "several nines" range (in some cases below commonly cited code thresholds in isolated benchmarks), though sustained, system-wide error rates during a real algorithm are typically worse than a best-case isolated gate. | Whether any device has run a full, sustained, universal fault-tolerant computation (not an isolated demonstration) at the qubit counts and error rates a useful algorithm's own resource estimate requires. Raw physical qubit count, by itself, answers none of this; the Scaling Challenges lesson already made that point about noisy qubits, and it applies with equal force to qubit-count headlines about "progress toward fault tolerance." |
| **Algorithms** | Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ optimality (proven twice, independently, in the black-box model; Query Complexity and Lower Bounds); Shor's algorithm runs in polynomial time on a quantum computer (a proven fact about the algorithm itself). | Factoring's classical intractability (the live cryptographic assumption Shor's significance depends on); a structural quantum speedup for the broad class of problems the quantum singular value transformation unifies, wherever a good block encoding exists. | The ceiling on achievable quantum advantage for problems people actually want solved (chemistry, optimization, materials science), as opposed to problems engineered specifically to be classically hard. This is Quantum Complexity Theory's own Tier 3 question, restated here at the scale of the entire field. |
| **Error correction** | The threshold theorem: below a threshold physical error rate, a code of sufficient distance suppresses logical error rate arbitrarily far below the physical rate, at only polylogarithmic qubit overhead. | More than one hardware group has reported the qualitative signature the threshold theorem predicts on real devices: logical error rate going *down*, not up, as code distance increases. That is real evidence of operating below threshold, not a simulation of it. | Whether that favorable scaling can be sustained through a complete, universal, fault-tolerant computation at the code distances, magic-state supply rates, and decoder throughput a real algorithm's resource estimate (Fault Tolerance Frontiers' own capstone) actually requires, as opposed to a research demonstration on one or a few logical qubits in isolation. |
| **Simulation & chemistry** | A quantum computer can, in principle, efficiently simulate the time evolution of any local Hamiltonian: a proven complexity-theoretic fact, and BQP's own defining example. | Certain molecular and materials simulations are believed to need exponential classical resources (Simulation & Compilation Frontiers' classical-simulability boundary) while staying efficient for a quantum computer: chemistry's original, Feynman-era motivating argument for building one at all. | Whether, and when, a quantum simulation reaches a molecule or material that is simultaneously large enough to be classically intractable and practically significant. Every demonstration to date is small enough to remain checkable classically, which is why it counts as evidence for the *promise* of the approach rather than proof that the promise has been cashed in. |

Notice the shape repeating in every row: a real proof anchors one end, a
real (sometimes very exciting) piece of evidence sits in the middle, and
an unresolved question (usually "does this work *at the scale that
would actually matter*?") sits at the other end. That shape is not a
coincidence. Recognizing it on sight, in a domain this table didn't
even cover, is the skill this lesson is trying to leave you with.

Try the framework on a headline before reading further. "New 1,200-qubit
processor achieves quantum advantage on an industrially relevant catalyst
molecule; fault-tolerant quantum computing is five years away." Three
claims, one sentence.

## On Not Knowing When

<InsightBlock>
  These lessons are frozen at the moment they were written, and the
  field is not. The lasting value of the curriculum is not any specific
  number in its tables, but knowing how to update them yourself.
</InsightBlock>

Notice what the table above does *not* contain: a date. That omission is
deliberate, and it is itself part of what this curriculum is teaching
you, not a gap in it.

Confident predictions about when large-scale fault-tolerant quantum
computing will arrive have a long, poor track record across this entire
field, in both directions: "it's imminent" and "it's essentially
impossible" have each been stated with more confidence than the
evidence at the time supported, repeatedly, by serious people. That
history is itself a data point worth taking seriously. A specific
five-year or ten-year forecast is not even the kind of claim the
three-tier framework can classify as a conjecture, because a conjecture
needs *specific, namable evidence*, and a timeline forecast usually
rests on extrapolating an engineering trend rather than on anything
with the evidentiary weight of, say, factoring's cryptographic track
record. A resource estimate, built the way Fault Tolerance Frontiers'
own capstone builds one, is a valuable thing: it tells
you how many physical qubits, at what code distance and error rate, a
target algorithm needs. Converting that number into a calendar date
requires an *additional*, separate assumption (a rate of future
hardware improvement) that carries none of the same evidentiary
backing.

This is not a hedge to avoid an uncomfortable answer. It is the honest
answer, and one you are now equipped to defend, because you can point
to which step in the reasoning (proven engineering requirement versus
assumed future improvement rate) a specific timeline claim is skipping.

One more thing follows directly, and it is the most important sentence
in this entire lesson: **this platform is frozen at whatever point it
was written, and the field is not.** New hardware results, new
algorithms, new resource estimates, and probably new claims just like
the ones this lesson taught you to interrogate, will appear after this
curriculum's content was last updated. Nothing above should be read as
the final word. Read it instead as a demonstration of *method*, applied
to the specific facts available at one point in time. Every number in
the table above will eventually be out of date. The lasting value is
that you now know how to update the table yourself.

## Worked Example: Classifying a New Claim

Back to the headline from two sections ago: **"New 1,200-qubit processor
achieves quantum advantage on an industrially relevant catalyst molecule;
fault-tolerant quantum computing is five years away."** Pulling its three
claims apart and classifying each is the exercise this whole capstone was
built to make routine.

**Claim 1: "1,200-qubit processor."** A qubit count alone establishes
almost nothing on its own (the Hardware row above, and Scaling
Challenges before it). The first question isn't "is 1,200 impressive?"
but "what is the two-qubit gate error rate, what is the connectivity,
and, separately, is this qubit count physical or logical?" A processor
can be impressive engineering in its own right and still be nowhere
near the conditions the threshold theorem requires to be invoked
honestly.

**Claim 2: "achieves quantum advantage on an industrially relevant
molecule."** This needs the same treatment Quantum Complexity Theory's
capstone gave random circuit sampling. What specific classical
algorithm was the comparison against, and is that the *best known*
classical method, or a convenient weak one? Does "industrially
relevant" mean the molecule is actually large enough to be classically
intractable, or merely large enough to sound impressive while remaining
classically checkable, which, per the table above, is where every
simulation demonstration to date actually sits? A real advantage claim
here would need to state its classical baseline as carefully as
Google's Sycamore paper stated its polynomial-hierarchy assumption; the
absence of that statement is itself informative.

**Claim 3: "fault-tolerant quantum computing is five years away."**
This is a timeline forecast, not a theorem or an evidenced conjecture:
the category just named above. The
right response isn't to believe it or dismiss it. Ask what resource
estimate, if any, it's built on, and whether that estimate carries
forward the magic-state distillation, routing overhead, and
code-distance requirements Fault Tolerance Frontiers' capstone showed
actually dominate a real qubit count.

<InsightBlock>
  None of these three claims can be evaluated with the same question.
  That, itself, is the finding: a single confident-sounding sentence
  about quantum computing is very often three claims of three
  completely different epistemic types, stitched together with "and,"
  and the entire value of this course has been building the habit of
  noticing the seams.
</InsightBlock>

## What You Can Now Do

This is not generic encouragement. Each item below is a specific,
checkable skill this curriculum actually built, lesson by lesson, and
that you did not have when you started.

- **You can read a graduate-level paper's abstract and know which kind of
  separation to expect.** "We prove an oracle separation" and "we show an
  unconditional separation" are different in kind, not just in strength.
  Query Complexity and Lower Bounds' adversary and polynomial methods,
  and the relativization barrier that stops them from generalizing, are
  why you now know to ask which one a new result actually claims before
  you update on it.
- **You can evaluate a resource-estimate claim.** Fault Tolerance
  Frontiers' capstone walked the full pipeline (union-bound error
  budget, code distance from the threshold-theorem scaling law,
  magic-state distillation rounds, data-plus-ancilla-plus-factory qubit
  count), so you know which inputs (gate count, target error rate,
  T-count) a real published estimate depends on, and which of its
  conclusions (the factory usually dominates the qubit count) are
  structural rather than specific to one paper's numbers.
- **You can tell a proven guarantee from a numerically-estimated one.**
  The threshold theorem's suppression guarantee is a Tier 1 proof; a
  specific reported logical error rate on a specific real device is a
  Tier 2, empirically-measured number, subject to the same caveats as
  any experimental result. Conflating the two, treating a good
  measurement as if it had a proof's permanence, is the mistake the
  complexity capstone flagged for random circuit sampling, and it is the
  single most common error in casual reporting on this field.
- **You can ask a new quantum-advantage claim the right questions, in
  order.** What specific classical baseline was it compared against, and
  is that baseline actually the best known one? What assumption does the
  claimed hardness rest on, and is that assumption itself proven,
  evidenced, or merely assumed? And the question that catches the most
  claims: is the task the demonstration was run on actually useful, or
  was it chosen (like random circuit sampling) specifically because it
  is classically hard, with usefulness left for later?
- **You can hold open uncertainty without either panicking or
  overclaiming.** You now know the difference between "this is proven,"
  "this is believed for stated reasons," and "this is genuinely
  unknown," and you know that a field's most publicly visible claims
  often live in the second or third category while being reported in
  language that implies the first.

## Timelines and Other Things Nobody Knows

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "strongly evidenced" as "proven," in either direction.**
Factoring's classical hardness, favorable error scaling with code
distance, and a specific reported gate fidelity are all strong, real
evidence, and all are still Tier 2, not Tier 1. Reporting them with
theorem-level confidence overclaims; dismissing them because they aren't
theorems underclaims. Both errors erase the distinction this whole
course was built to preserve.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating a confident timeline as if it were a finding rather than a
forecast.** "X years away" is a claim about future engineering
execution, not a mathematical result or an empirically-measured
quantity. This field's track record with such forecasts, in both
directions, is poor enough that confident phrasing alone should never
substitute for a stated resource estimate and its assumptions.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A device announcement leads with a physical-qubit count and states
   that fault-tolerant quantum computing has effectively arrived. Using
   this lesson's Hardware row, identify what additional evidence
   (beyond qubit count) would be needed to support that stronger claim,
   and name the proven theorem whose conditions that evidence would need
   to satisfy.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The theorem is the **threshold theorem**, and the first thing to
   notice about it is that physical qubit count appears nowhere in its
   hypotheses. A qubit count is not weak evidence for fault tolerance; it
   is evidence about a quantity the theorem does not mention. A million
   qubits above threshold are worse than a thousand below it, since above
   threshold a larger code makes the logical error rate exponentially
   worse.

   Before anything else, ask whether the number is physical or logical.
   Twelve hundred *logical* qubits would be a transformative claim.
   Twelve hundred physical qubits is an ordinary contemporary device.

   **What the theorem's conditions actually demand as evidence.**

   *A per-gate error rate below threshold, measured the right way.* Not a
   best-case isolated two-qubit benchmark, which is the number press
   releases quote. The sustained, system-wide rate during a running
   circuit, including idling, measurement, and reset errors, all of which
   are error locations the code has to correct. And the comparison has to
   name *which* threshold, since it is decoder- and noise-model-specific
   and quoted values differ by a factor of several.

   *Noise that is local and not too correlated.* The proof's two-fault
   counting assumes faults are roughly independent. Evidence needed:
   characterization of crosstalk, of leakage out of the computational
   subspace, and of burst events. Cosmic rays are the concrete worry for
   superconducting devices, producing correlated multi-qubit failures
   that a local code has no defence against, and getting more likely as
   patches get larger.

   *The scaling signature, sustained.* Logical error rate falling as code
   distance rises, measured across several distances, with a fitted
   suppression factor per two steps of distance, held over many rounds
   rather than observed once.

   *A universal logical gate set, running.* A logical CNOT between two
   logical patches by lattice surgery, and a magic-state factory
   producing distilled states at the rate the circuit consumes them.
   Clifford-only fault tolerance is, by Gottesman-Knill, classically
   simulable and therefore not fault-tolerant *computing*.

   *A decoder keeping up in real time.* Syndrome rounds arrive on the
   order of a microsecond, indefinitely. A decoder slower than that
   accumulates an unbounded backlog and is operationally not a decoder.

   **The sentence the announcement could honestly have written** is
   something like: "sustained two-qubit error rate of $X$ under
   algorithmic conditions, below the $Y$ threshold for decoder $Z$, with
   logical error rate suppressed by a factor $\Lambda$ per two steps of
   code distance across distances 3, 5, and 7." That is a real and
   impressive claim, it is checkable, and it is a different sentence from
   the one that led with a qubit count.

   </details>

2. Pick any one of the four domains in this lesson's state-of-the-field
   table (hardware, algorithms, error correction, simulation/chemistry)
   and explain why its "genuinely open" entry is open: what specific
   thing would have to be demonstrated to resolve it, and why that
   hasn't happened yet.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Taking the **error-correction** row, whose open entry asks whether the
   favourable distance scaling can be sustained through a complete,
   universal, fault-tolerant computation at the parameters a real
   resource estimate demands.

   **What would resolve it.** One demonstration, running a nontrivial
   algorithm on several logical qubits, for a duration set by a published
   resource estimate rather than by what the apparatus can manage, with
   four things happening at once: logical two-qubit gates by lattice
   surgery between separate patches, a magic-state factory delivering
   distilled states at the rate the circuit consumes them, a real-time
   decoder that never falls behind over the whole run, and a final
   logical error rate matching what the distance predicted. All four
   simultaneously, sustained, on one machine.

   **Why it has not happened, in the numbers this course computed.**

   *Scale.* The Simulation and Compilation capstone's deliberately tiny
   circuit, two logical qubits and 600 T gates, needed 4097 physical
   qubits at distance 11. That is a toy with no purpose. Published
   estimates for cryptographically relevant factoring run into the
   millions of physical qubits. Devices today are in the hundreds to low
   thousands.

   *The factory dominates and has never run.* In that same estimate
   $88\%$ of the machine was magic-state factory. No experiment has yet
   operated a distillation factory feeding a live logical computation at
   rate, which means the single largest line item in every resource
   estimate is the one with the least experimental grounding.

   *Decoder throughput.* Minimum-weight matching is polynomial-time and
   that is not the same as fast enough for a microsecond cycle,
   indefinitely, at distance 11 or more. The Decoding lesson is explicit
   that this is live engineering rather than a settled footnote.

   *Correlated and leakage errors.* Every demonstration so far has been
   short enough that burst events are rare. A computation running for
   hours will meet them, and they violate the independence the whole
   analysis rests on.

   *Duration.* Existing results run tens to hundreds of syndrome rounds
   on one or a few logical qubits. An algorithm needs billions of rounds
   across thousands of them.

   **Why this is the right shape of open question, and not pessimism.**
   Every individual ingredient has been demonstrated: below-threshold
   scaling, lattice surgery, distillation, real-time decoding. What has
   never been demonstrated is all of them composed, at scale, for a
   duration. So the open question is about *composition and endurance*
   rather than about any missing mechanism, and that is a genuinely
   different kind of uncertainty from "we do not know if this is
   possible." Nobody expects a new theorem to be needed. Nobody can tell
   you what the composition costs until someone builds it either.

   </details>

3. Explain why this lesson declines to give a specific year by which
   large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing will exist, and what kind
   of evidence (as opposed to what kind of confident phrasing) would
   actually change that answer.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a date is not the kind of claim the three-tier framework can
   hold. Tier 1 requires a proof. Tier 2 and Tier 3 require specific,
   namable evidence about the world. A date requires an assumed rate of
   future engineering improvement, which is neither.

   Decompose a timeline claim and the seam is obvious. It is the product
   of two factors.

   **A resource requirement:** how many physical qubits at what error
   rate a target algorithm needs. This half is real, derivable, and
   exactly what the two fault-tolerance capstones compute. It is
   evidenced.

   **A closure rate:** how fast hardware will cover the remaining
   distance. This half is an extrapolation of a trend, for a process
   nobody has run to completion, over a range nobody has observed.

   And the second factor is unstable in a way that is easy to miss,
   because **the target moves too.** Published estimates for factoring
   RSA-2048 have fallen by roughly two orders of magnitude over about
   fifteen years, from something like a billion physical qubits to
   something like twenty million, driven by better circuit constructions
   and better factory designs rather than by any hardware progress at
   all. So the gap is closing from both ends at rates neither of which
   anybody can forecast, and multiplying two unforecastable rates does
   not produce a date, it produces a mood with a number attached.

   **What would actually move the answer.** Not a longer trend line and
   not a more confident speaker. Three things, in order of how much each
   would settle.

   *Data on the logical quantities.* The relevant curves are logical
   qubits per year and logical error rate per year, not physical qubit
   count per year. There is barely enough history on either to fit a
   line, and those are the quantities a date depends on.

   *A resource estimate that has stopped falling.* A stable target for a
   specific, useful algorithm would make the distance a fixed number
   rather than a moving one.

   *The composition demonstration from question 2.* The dominant
   remaining uncertainty is what it costs to run every ingredient
   together, sustained. Until someone does it once, at any scale, the
   error bar on that cost is not small, it is unknown.

   **What declining to give a date is not.** It is not doubt that this
   will happen. The threshold theorem proves it can, and multiple groups
   have now demonstrated the below-threshold signature it requires. The
   uncertainty is entirely about schedule, and being unwilling to name a
   schedule is a different thing from doubting the destination. The
   useful response to any timeline claim is therefore one question, not
   an argument: **which resource estimate is that derived from, and what
   improvement rate does it assume?** A claim that can answer both is
   worth discussing. A claim that can answer neither has told you
   everything you needed to know about it.

   </details>

## Summary

- Five foundational tracks (Quantum Mechanics, Quantum Computing,
  Quantum Hardware, Quantum Software, and Quantum Mastery) built, in
  order, the mathematical postulates of quantum theory, the algorithms
  and information theory built on them, the real physical devices that
  realize them, the software layer that programs those devices, and the
  rigorous mathematics underneath all of it. APEX's five courses then
  extended every one of those threads to research depth, ending in this
  course's own practice of evaluating new claims.
- The state of the field, reusing the three-tier framework at the scale
  of the whole field: real, proven hardware and theoretical building
  blocks exist (working qubits across multiple platforms, the threshold
  theorem, Grover's and Shor's proven properties); strong, real, but
  non-final evidence supports several major claims (favorable error
  scaling with code distance, factoring's classical hardness, quantum
  chemistry's theoretical promise); and genuinely open questions remain
  about scale, useful-problem advantage, and whether fault tolerance
  has been demonstrated where it would actually matter.
- No specific timeline for large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing
  is asserted here, deliberately: confident predictions in this field
  have a poor track record, and the calibrated uncertainty itself is
  part of what this curriculum leaves you equipped to reason about.
- What you can now do: read a paper's abstract and know what kind of
  separation to expect; evaluate a resource-estimate claim by its
  underlying assumptions; tell a proven guarantee from a numerically
  estimated one; and ask any new quantum-advantage or timeline claim
  what classical baseline, what assumption, and what usefulness it
  actually rests on.
- These lessons are frozen in time; the field is not. The lasting value
  of everything learned here is the ability to evaluate new information
  as it arrives, not any specific fact in this lesson, all of which
  will eventually need updating.

## Where to Go from Here

A curriculum ends; a reading life starts. Here is a concrete starting
kit, chosen so that every item extends something you have already built.

**Four texts.** Nielsen and Chuang's *Quantum Computation and Quantum
Information* is the field's standard reference and the natural place to
see this curriculum's material in its canonical adult form. Wilde's
*Quantum Information Theory* (2nd ed., Cambridge, 2017) deepens the
Shannon-theory thread the Mastery track opened: channels, capacities,
and entropies with full proofs. Watrous's *The Theory of Quantum
Information* (Cambridge, 2018) is the mathematically strictest of the
three, and the right companion for the semidefinite-programming and
norm machinery research papers lean on. Preskill's lecture notes for
Caltech's Ph219/CS219, free online, sit somewhere between a textbook
and a colleague explaining things at a blackboard, and they cover
topological quantum computation, which this curriculum only brushed.

**Seven papers you are now equipped to read.** Each of these already
appeared somewhere in this curriculum's research citations. One
sentence each on what to extract:

- **Gilyén, Su, Low & Wiebe, "Quantum singular value transformation and
  beyond" (STOC 2019, arXiv:1806.01838).** Extract the block-encoding
  definition and the main QSVT theorem, and watch how Algorithmic
  Frontiers' entire course was a guided tour of this one paper.
- **Martyn, Rossi, Tan & Chuang, "Grand Unification of Quantum
  Algorithms" (2021, arXiv:2105.02859).** Extract the single table
  mapping Grover, phase estimation, and Hamiltonian simulation onto QSP
  phase sequences: the unification claim, made precise.
- **Aaronson & Gottesman, "Improved Simulation of Stabilizer Circuits"
  (Phys. Rev. A 70, 052328 (2004)).** Extract the tableau
  representation and why Clifford circuits are classically easy, which
  is the exact boundary the simulability course mapped.
- **Bravyi & Kitaev, "Universal quantum computation with ideal Clifford
  gates and noisy ancillas" (Phys. Rev. A 71, 022316 (2005)).** Extract
  the 15-to-1 distillation protocol and the reason magic-state
  factories dominate every serious resource estimate.
- **Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann, "A Quantum Approximate Optimization
  Algorithm" (2014, arXiv:1411.4028).** Extract the precise worst-case
  guarantee actually proven at $p=1$, then compare it against how QAOA
  gets described in press coverage.
- **Gidney & Ekerå, "How to factor 2048 bit RSA integers in 8 hours
  using 20 million noisy qubits" (Quantum 5, 433 (2021),
  arXiv:1905.09749).** Extract the full pipeline from logical algorithm
  to physical-qubit count, the same pipeline Fault Tolerance Frontiers'
  capstone taught, executed at professional depth.
- **Google Quantum AI, "Quantum error correction below the surface code
  threshold" (Nature 638, 920 (2025); announced December 2024).**
  Extract the error-suppression factor as code distance grows, and
  check the claim against the threshold theorem's actual conditions,
  the way this lesson's error-correction row does.

**Staying current.** New quantum-computing papers appear daily on
arXiv's quant-ph listing (arxiv.org/list/quant-ph/new); skimming titles
and reading one abstract a day with this course's anatomy questions is
a sustainable habit, and it compounds faster than binge-reading ever
does.

**Four open problems, honestly tiered.** If you want questions to grow
toward rather than papers to read, the course's own map already
contains them:

1. **Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$?** Unconditionally open, and
   a good final test of this course's own vocabulary. Most researchers
   expect the answer is no, and that expectation rests on real evidence
   rather than taste: Raz and Tal exhibited an oracle relative to which
   $\mathsf{BQP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$, and $\mathsf{NP}$ sits inside
   $\mathsf{PH}$. Read that the way How to Read a Quantum Computing
   Paper taught you to and the shape is familiar: rigorous evidence
   *in the query model*, supporting a Tier 3 belief, with the
   unrelativized question still untouched.
2. **The quantum PCP conjecture.** Open; the NLTS theorem (2022) proved
   one necessary consequence, so the conjecture survived a real test
   without becoming any less open.
3. **The ceiling on quantum advantage for useful problems.** Open, and
   partly empirical: every advantage demonstrated so far lives on tasks
   engineered for classical hardness, not usefulness.
4. **Sustained fault tolerance at algorithmic scale.** Open as a
   demonstration rather than as theory: the threshold theorem is
   proven, below-threshold scaling is now evidenced on real hardware,
   and the gap between one excellent logical qubit and a
   million-physical-qubit factory-fed computation remains the field's
   central engineering question.
