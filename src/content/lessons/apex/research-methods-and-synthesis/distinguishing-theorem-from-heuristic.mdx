## Motivation

How to Read a Quantum Computing Paper already told you where a paper's
precise, checkable claim lives, and warned you that a numerical
experiment and a proven bound are "different kinds of evidence." This
lesson exists to turn that warning into a usable tool: a checklist
precise enough to apply to a specific sentence, not just a general
attitude of caution.

The reason this matters goes well past reading etiquette. Consider four
claims, all real, all already built on this platform: Grover's algorithm
cannot be beaten by any black-box quantum algorithm. QAOA finds
near-optimal Max-Cut solutions on real, practically-sized graphs.
Factoring has no efficient classical algorithm. Barren plateaus make
variational circuits untrainable at scale. All four sound like the same
kind of statement: confident, technical, stated by experts. Exactly one
of them is a proven mathematical theorem. The other three are, at best,
extremely well-supported, and knowing *which* is which, and *why*,
is not a pedantic footnote. It is the single most common place where
public discussion of quantum computing goes wrong: a heuristic gets
reported as a theorem, a conjecture gets reported as settled fact, or
— just as damaging in the other direction — a genuinely useful heuristic
gets dismissed as worthless simply because it isn't a theorem. This lesson
builds the checklist that keeps those three failure modes apart, and
then runs it, in full, on results you have already checked yourself.

## The Checklist: Operationalizing Proven / Evidenced / Open

The Quantum Complexity Theory capstone sorted every result in that
course into exactly three tiers: **proven theorems**, **strongly-evidenced
conjectures**, and **genuinely open questions**. That framework was built
for one narrow kind of claim — statements about complexity classes. The
checklist below is the same three-way epistemic distinction, generalized
to *any* quantum-computing claim, with the middle tier split in two,
because "well-supported by broad numerical testing" and "believed on
theoretical, structural grounds despite no proof" are different kinds of
unproven evidence and deserve different labels.

<DefinitionBox title="The four-question classification checklist">

Given a specific, precisely-stated quantum-computing claim, ask these
questions **in order**, stopping at the first "yes":

1. **Is there a complete mathematical proof — an explicit, checkable
   argument that establishes the claim for every case it covers?** If
   yes: **Theorem.** Nothing about future cleverness, faster hardware, or
   a bigger dataset could ever overturn it.
2. **Is there strong numerical or simulation evidence across many
   independent test cases, without a matching general proof?** If yes:
   **Heuristic / empirical finding.** The claim is a real, checkable
   regularity — just not one anybody has derived from first principles
   for the general case.
3. **Is there a plausible, motivated argument — a restricted-case proof,
   a structural analogy, a citable theoretical basis — with *some* real
   supporting evidence, but genuinely unverified in the setting the claim
   is actually about?** If yes: **Conjecture.** Believed, sometimes very
   strongly, but not proven and not (yet) backed by the kind of broad
   empirical sweep that would make it a heuristic finding instead.
4. **None of the above** — no decisive proof, no broad numerical sweep,
   no strong motivating argument either way? **Genuinely open.**

Then, regardless of which tier the claim landed in, ask one more
question every single time: **what SPECIFIC additional result would move
this claim up a tier?** A vague "more research" does not count as an
answer — the honest version of this question has a concrete answer (a
general proof, a benchmark across a stated instance class, a restricted-
case theorem extended to the general case), and stating that answer
explicitly is exactly what separates a precise classification from a
vague gesture at uncertainty.

</DefinitionBox>

Tier 2 (heuristic) and Tier 3 (conjecture) both sit inside what the
complexity capstone called "strongly-evidenced conjecture": this
lesson's split exists because *how* a claim is evidenced changes what
would settle it. A heuristic algorithm's performance claim is normally
upgraded by a proof of a matching worst-case bound; a conjecture like
factoring's classical hardness is normally upgraded by an unconditional
lower bound. Different target, different research program, same
underlying "not yet proven" status.

## Worked Examples: Four Claims This Platform Already Settled

Four claims, four different answers to the checklist, all drawn directly
from real results built earlier on this platform.

### (a) Grover's algorithm's optimality — Theorem

**The claim:** No black-box quantum algorithm can search an unsorted
list of size $N$ in fewer than $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ queries: Grover's
algorithm is optimal, not merely good.

**Checklist:** Question 1 — yes. The Quantum Complexity Theory capstone
records that this bound is "established independently by both the
adversary method and the polynomial method." Two separate, complete,
checkable mathematical arguments, each sufficient on its own, arriving at
the same bound. That is about as strong as Tier 1 evidence gets.

<TheoremBox title="Grover optimality, doubly proven" provenance="cited">

Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is proven optimal in the
black-box model by two independent techniques: the adversary method and
the polynomial method (Query Complexity and Lower Bounds; restated in the
Quantum Complexity Theory capstone). No future algorithmic cleverness
can beat this bound in that model — a genuinely closed question.

</TheoremBox>

**What would move it up a tier:** Nothing — it is already Tier 1, the
ceiling of the checklist. (The genuinely open question nearby is
different: whether query-complexity techniques like these can be
extended to *unconditional circuit-complexity* separations outside the
black-box model — itself Tier 4 on this claim's own checklist, and
explicitly listed as open in the capstone.)

### (b) QAOA's real-world performance — mostly heuristic

**The claim:** QAOA finds near-optimal solutions to large, practically
relevant Max-Cut instances.

**Checklist:** Question 1 — no general proof exists for this claim as
stated. This platform's own QAOA lessons are explicit about that: QAOA
and Combinatorial Optimization's Common Mistakes callout warns against
"assuming QAOA always finds the exact maximum cut," and QAOA: A Worked
Max-Cut Example's own Physical Interpretation states plainly that how
the approximation ratio improves with circuit depth $p$, and whether it
can be pushed arbitrarily close to $1$ for hard instances, is "an open,
actively studied question this course's two small worked examples can
illustrate the flavor of but not settle." Those two worked examples
themselves (a single edge and a 3-node triangle, both solved by grid
search and checked against `bruteForceMaxCut`) are honestly scoped:
they demonstrate the *mechanism* on tiny, exactly-checkable instances,
not a general performance guarantee, and that same lesson's Common
Mistakes callout explicitly warns against "assuming QAOA's near-100%
result here generalizes to larger, harder graphs."

Question 2 — yes, at the level of the broader field: QAOA's approximation
ratio on large, practically-sized instances is evaluated the way most
heuristic algorithms are evaluated anywhere in computer science — by
benchmarking across many problem instances and circuit depths, not by a
matching general proof.

Question 3 is worth a beat, though, because this claim is genuinely
mixed rather than purely heuristic: the *original* QAOA construction
(Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann 2014) does carry a real, narrow, proven
guarantee — a specific worst-case approximation ratio for $p=1$ QAOA on
a specific restricted graph class (3-regular, triangle-free graphs).
That is a genuine Tier 1 result, but for a specific $p$ and a specific
instance family — not for "large, practically relevant instances" in
general, which is exactly the claim under evaluation here, and exactly
the kind of restricted-case-versus-general-case gap How to Read a
Quantum Computing Paper's whole worked example was built to catch.

<ResearchConnection
  title="QAOA's original worst-case approximation guarantee"
  source="Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann (2014)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4028"
>
  The construction that introduced QAOA proves a specific worst-case
  approximation ratio for $p=1$ QAOA, but only on a restricted graph
  class — 3-regular, triangle-free graphs. It's a real Tier 1 theorem for
  that exact scope, not a general performance guarantee for the
  large, practical instances a headline claim usually means.
</ResearchConnection>

**Classification:** Tier 2, heuristic/empirical, for the claim as stated
("real-world performance on large, practical instances"), sitting
alongside a genuine, narrower Tier 1 result for one specific $p=1$,
specific-graph-class special case.

**What would move it up a tier:** A proof of a matching worst-case
approximation-ratio bound for QAOA at general depth $p$, on the actual
instance classes practitioners care about — not a restricted graph
family, and not limited to $p=1$. This is real, active research, and
its difficulty is exactly why the claim currently sits at Tier 2, not
Tier 1.

### (c) Factoring's classical hardness — Conjecture

**The claim:** No efficient (polynomial-time) classical algorithm exists
for integer factoring: the assumption underlying RSA's security and the
real significance of Shor's algorithm.

**Checklist:** Question 1 — no proof exists; nobody has an unconditional
lower bound ruling out a fast classical factoring algorithm. Question 2
— no, this isn't a broad-numerical-sweep kind of claim; there's no
"benchmark" that would even make sense here. Question 3 — yes: as the
Quantum Complexity Theory capstone put it, the evidence is "decades of
attempted classical attacks, none successful," reinforced by factoring's
structural relationship to other believed-hard number-theoretic
problems. That is real, namable, motivated evidence — just not a proof.

**Classification:** Tier 3, conjecture — exactly the capstone's own
classification, carried over unchanged, since this claim is a pure
complexity-theoretic belief statement, not an algorithm's performance
claim.

**What would move it up a tier:** An unconditional proof that no
polynomial-time classical factoring algorithm exists. Nobody has any
credible strategy for proving this today — which is itself part of why
it remains a conjecture rather than a heuristic (there is no numerical
sweep that could settle it either) or an open question with no
supporting argument at all (the decades of failed attacks are real
evidence, just not proof).

### (d) Barren plateaus — a genuinely mixed case

**The claim:** "Variational quantum circuits become untrainable —
gradients vanish — as the number of qubits grows."

This one does not have a single answer, and pulling it apart is the
point. Barren Plateaus and Variational Trainability actually proves two
separate results with two separate epistemic statuses:

- **The gradient's mean is exactly zero.** This lesson derives it fully,
  in closed form, "for *any* joint distribution of the other parameters,
  no genuinely deep theory required" — true for every circuit depth and
  every qubit count. Checklist Question 1: yes, unconditionally.
  **Theorem**, full stop.
- **The gradient's *variance* vanishes exponentially in qubit count.**
  This is the actual barren-plateau phenomenon, and the lesson is
  explicit about its scope: it holds "for sufficiently deep, sufficiently
  random ansätze and global cost functions," proved via concentration
  of measure (Lévy's lemma, cited to McClean, Boixo, Smelyanskiy, Babbush
  & Neven 2018 — a real, rigorous proof, but for that specific ansatz
  class, not for every possible variational circuit design). Checklist
  Question 1: yes, but *for a restricted class of circuits* — a proven
  theorem with a stated scope, not a universal one.

The lesson's own numerical verification (scaling a real ansatz from
$n=2$ to $6$ qubits, $300$ samples per size, a seeded deterministic
generator) is Question-2-shaped supporting evidence for that scoped
theorem holding up in practice — and it is refreshingly honest about
where the numerics get messy: the Common Mistakes callout there records
that at *insufficient* circuit depth, "the measured variance at
$n=2\to3$ *increased* before decreasing," exactly the kind of real,
reproducible wrinkle that separates an honest numerical check from a
cherry-picked one.

**Classification:** Tier 1 (zero-mean gradient, unconditionally) **plus**
Tier 1-for-a-restricted-class (vanishing variance, for sufficiently
deep/random ansätze and global costs), reinforced by Tier-2-style direct
numerical confirmation. The claim that goes wrong is the *unscoped*
version — "variational circuits are always untrainable at scale" — which
that same lesson's second Common Mistakes callout flags directly:
shallower, problem-tailored ansätze and *local* cost functions are real,
partially effective mitigations, so the unscoped claim overclaims a
real, narrower, proven result exactly the way How to Read a Quantum
Computing Paper's worked example warned an unqualified "exponential
speedup" can overclaim an oracle-model theorem.

**What would move the restricted-class result up to fully general:**
Nothing can — a circuit that is *not* deep/expressive enough to
approximate Haar-randomness, or a *local* rather than global cost
function, genuinely falls outside the proven theorem's own hypotheses,
so "generalizing" it further isn't a missing proof so much as a
different (and, for many ansätze, false) claim.

### Practice Round: Classify Four More Claims

Same checklist, four new claims, reusing more of this platform's own
real content.

**Practice claim 1 — "The surface code's threshold is approximately
1%."** Full walkthrough:

- **Step 1, what was actually shown:** Decoding Surface Codes states
  that simulating real surface-code patches gives "a well-established
  empirical/theoretical relationship" between logical error rate $p_L$
  and code distance $d$ below some threshold $p_{th}$, and that $p_{th}$
  itself is "cited in real work as roughly percent-level (commonly in
  the ballpark of $\sim 1\%$ for circuit-level noise with a strong
  decoder)."
- **Step 2, Question 1 — is the *specific number* proven?** No. The
  lesson is explicit that $p_{th}$ is "genuinely decoder- and
  noise-model-dependent... not a single universal constant that applies
  to every code variant or every decoder equally." A single clean
  percent-level number is not what any proof delivers.
- **Step 3, Question 2 — is there strong numerical evidence?** Yes,
  directly stated: this is "simulating and experimenting on real
  surface-code patches," i.e., numerical/simulation-based estimation for
  a specific noise model and decoder.
- **Step 4, is there *also* real theory underneath it?** Yes, and this
  is worth separating out honestly: the *scaling law's shape*,
  $p_L\sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$, is tied to a genuine phase-transition
  argument (the Nishimori point of a random-bond Ising model, Dennis,
  Kitaev, Landahl & Preskill 2002) — real, citable theory, but it proves
  the *functional form* of the suppression, not the specific $\sim1\%$
  numerical value for any given decoder and noise model.

  <ResearchConnection
    title="The surface-code threshold's scaling law"
    source="Dennis, Kitaev, Landahl & Preskill (2002)"
    url="https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0110143"
  >
    Ties the logical-error suppression $p_L\sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$ to
    a genuine phase-transition argument at the Nishimori point of a
    random-bond Ising model. Real, citable theory for the *shape* of the
    curve — it does not by itself pin down the specific $\sim 1\%$
    numerical threshold for any particular decoder and noise model.
  </ResearchConnection>
- **Step 5, classification:** Tier 2, heuristic/empirical, for the
  specific $\sim1\%$ figure — a real, well-supported, decoder-specific
  numerical estimate, resting on (but not equal to) a proven theoretical
  scaling law.
- **Step 6, what would move it up a tier:** An analytic derivation of
  the *exact* threshold value for a fully specified decoder and noise
  model, from first principles, rather than from simulation — a much
  harder target than proving the scaling law's shape, which is already
  done.

**Practice claim 2 — "Random circuit sampling is classically hard."**
Already classified in full by the Quantum Complexity Theory capstone:
Tier 3, conjecture — a real, citable theoretical basis (the
polynomial-hierarchy-collapse argument) provides Question-3-style
motivated evidence, but that basis itself rests on an unproven, if
widely believed, assumption, so the claim never reaches Question 1.

**Practice claim 3 — "Below a constant physical error rate, concatenated
quantum error correction drives logical error arbitrarily close to
zero."** The Threshold Theorem proves this outright: independently
established by Shor, Aharonov–Ben-Or, Kitaev, Knill–Laflamme–Zurek and
others via the concatenation recursion $p_{k+1}\approx cp_k^2$, unrolled
to a double-exponential collapse below threshold. Question 1: yes.
**Theorem.** (Notice the contrast with practice claim 1: *that* a
threshold exists and *what happens* on either side of it is proven;
*where, numerically,* that threshold sits for a real decoder and noise
model is the separate, heuristic question.)

**Practice claim 4 — "Quantum computers will eventually be shown to
solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time" (i.e., $\mathsf{NP}
\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$).** Question 1 — no proof either way. Question 2
— no broad numerical sweep would even settle a question about
worst-case asymptotic complexity. Question 3 — this is the interesting
part: there isn't a strong *motivated* argument for this claim, either.
If anything, the field's evidence (Grover's proven optimality, BBBV,
this platform's own adversary- and polynomial-method lower bounds) points
the other way, in the black-box model — but that evidence is explicitly
scoped to black-box query complexity and, as the capstone states, "there
isn't even a strong heuristic argument pointing either way" once
explicitly-given, structured instances are considered. **Genuinely
open** — and open in the specific sense of "no decisive evidence in
either direction for the general, structured setting," not "open because
nobody has looked."

## Why This Matters

<InsightBlock>
  "Not yet proven" and "not worth trusting" are entirely different
  claims: a well-supported heuristic sits in the same respectable
  company as simulated annealing and industrial SAT solvers, not in some
  lesser category invented for quantum algorithms.
</InsightBlock>

Being unable to tell "proven" from "well-supported heuristic" from
"hopeful conjecture" is, honestly, one of the single most common sources
of confusion and overclaiming in how quantum computing results get
reported: a headline says "proven," a press release says "breakthrough,"
and a casual reader has no tool for asking which of this lesson's four
tiers the underlying claim actually occupies. Worse, the confusion runs
in both directions: treating a heuristic as a theorem overclaims it, but
treating a heuristic as *worthless because it isn't a theorem* is its own
serious error, and one this lesson's QAOA example was chosen specifically
to correct. Heuristic algorithms with strong empirical support but no
general worst-case proof are not a quantum-computing quirk — they are
one of the most common and useful categories in *all* of computer
science. Simulated annealing has driven real optimization results for
decades with no general convergence-rate guarantee for hard instances.
Many of the SAT solvers used in real industrial verification pipelines
have no proven polynomial bound on worst-case runtime and are used
anyway, constantly, because they work extremely well on the instances
that actually show up in practice. QAOA sits in exactly this company,
not in some lesser category invented for quantum algorithms specifically.

And none of this skill is actually specific to quantum computing. The
four-question checklist built above — proof, broad empirical support,
motivated-but-unverified belief, or genuinely open — is a general-purpose
tool for evaluating *any* technical claim you will ever encounter,
inside or outside this field: a machine-learning paper's benchmark
numbers, a medical study's effect size, an economic model's prediction.
Quantum computing is simply where this lesson practiced it, using
results precise and well-documented enough to classify with total
confidence.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Reporting a well-supported heuristic as a proven theorem.** "QAOA
solves Max-Cut" and "Grover's algorithm is provably optimal" are not the
same kind of sentence, even though both sound equally confident. Before
repeating either claim, ask this lesson's Question 1: is there an actual
proof, or is the confidence coming from broad numerical support instead?
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Dismissing a heuristic as unscientific or worthless because it isn't a
proven theorem.** QAOA's lack of a general worst-case guarantee places it
in exactly the same epistemic category as simulated annealing and most
practical SAT solvers — genuinely useful, extensively studied, and
completely normal for a heuristic algorithm to be. "Not yet proven" and
"not worth studying" are entirely different claims, and conflating them
is the mirror image of the first mistake above.
</Callout>

   problem: "Shor's algorithm factors an $n$-bit integer using
   $O(n^3)$-scale quantum gates" and "no efficient classical algorithm
   for factoring exists." Run this lesson's four-question checklist on
   each separately, and explain why they land in different tiers even
   though both describe the exact same problem.
2. Classify the claim "the HHL algorithm provides an exponential speedup
   for solving linear systems of equations." Be precise about which
   restrictions are structurally *part of* the claim before it can be
   evaluated at all — the matrix must be sparse and well-conditioned, and
   the algorithm outputs a quantum state encoding the solution rather
   than the solution vector itself — the same way this lesson's barren-
   plateau example required specifying "sufficiently deep, sufficiently
   random ansätze" before its variance-decay theorem applied. State
   whether the unrestricted, unqualified version of the claim survives
   Question 1.

   Then state the specific additional assumption — about the physical
   implementation, not the idealized protocol — that would need to be
   violated for a real-world BB84 deployment to fall outside that
   guarantee, in the same spirit as this lesson's barren-plateau example
   separating a proven theorem from the unscoped claim it gets mistaken
   for.

## Summary

- The checklist: (1) is there a complete mathematical proof? → Theorem.
  (2) Strong numerical/simulation evidence across many cases, no general
  proof? → Heuristic/empirical finding. (3) A plausible, motivated
  argument with some real evidence, genuinely unverified? → Conjecture.
  (4) None of the above? → Genuinely open. Always follow up by naming the
  SPECIFIC result that would move a claim up a tier.
- Four settled examples: Grover's optimality is a Theorem (proved twice,
  by the adversary and polynomial methods); QAOA's real-world Max-Cut
  performance is mostly Heuristic (a narrow $p=1$, restricted-graph-class
  proof exists, but general large-instance performance is evaluated
  empirically); factoring's classical hardness is a Conjecture (decades
  of failed attacks, no proof); barren plateaus are genuinely mixed — a
  fully general, unconditional Theorem (zero-mean gradient) alongside a
  Theorem proven only for a restricted, sufficiently-deep/random ansatz
  class (vanishing variance), reinforced by direct numerical
  confirmation.
- A well-supported heuristic is not a lesser or unscientific result — it
  is one of the most common, useful categories in all of computer
  science, and treating "unproven" as "untrustworthy" is as serious a
  misreading as treating "unproven" as "proven."
- This four-tier classification skill generalizes completely beyond
  quantum computing: it is a reusable tool for evaluating any technical
  claim, in any field, that you did not personally derive yourself.
