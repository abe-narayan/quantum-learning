## Four Claims That Sound Alike

Four claims, all real, all built in earlier courses. Grover's algorithm
cannot be beaten by any black-box quantum algorithm. QAOA finds
near-optimal Max-Cut solutions on real, practically-sized graphs.
Factoring has no efficient classical algorithm. Barren plateaus make
variational circuits untrainable at scale. Each sounds like the same
kind of statement: confident, technical, stated by experts. Only one is
a proven mathematical theorem.

Knowing which one, and why, is not a pedantic footnote. It marks the
single most common place where public discussion of quantum computing
goes wrong. A heuristic gets reported as a theorem. A conjecture gets
reported as settled fact. Or, just as damaging in the other direction,
a useful heuristic gets dismissed as worthless because it lacks a
proof. The previous lesson warned that a numerical experiment and a
proven bound are different kinds of evidence; this lesson turns that
warning into a usable tool. What follows is a checklist precise enough
to apply to a specific sentence, then a full run of it on results you
have already checked yourself.

Commit to an answer before reading the checklist. The value of the
exercise is in noticing which way your instinct pulls, not in scoring.

## The Checklist: Operationalizing Proven / Evidenced / Open

The Quantum Complexity Theory capstone sorted every result in that
course into three tiers: **proven theorems**, **strongly-evidenced
conjectures**, and **genuinely open questions**. That framework was
built for one narrow kind of claim: statements about complexity
classes. The checklist below is the same three-way epistemic
distinction, generalized to *any* quantum-computing claim, with the
middle tier split in two, because "well-supported by broad numerical
testing" and "believed on theoretical, structural grounds despite no
proof" are different kinds of unproven evidence and deserve different
labels.

<DefinitionBox title="The four-question classification checklist">

Given a specific, precisely-stated quantum-computing claim, ask these
questions **in order**, stopping at the first "yes":

1. **Is there a complete mathematical proof, an explicit and checkable
   argument that establishes the claim for every case it covers?** If
   yes: **Theorem.** Nothing about future cleverness, faster hardware, or
   a bigger dataset could ever overturn it.
2. **Is there strong numerical or simulation evidence across many
   independent test cases, without a matching general proof?** If yes:
   **Heuristic / empirical finding.** The claim is a checkable
   regularity that nobody has yet derived from first principles for the
   general case.
3. **Is there a plausible, motivated argument (a restricted-case proof,
   a structural analogy, a citable theoretical basis) with *some* real
   supporting evidence, but no verification in the setting the claim
   is actually about?** If yes: **Conjecture.** Believed, sometimes very
   strongly, but not proven and not (yet) backed by the kind of broad
   empirical sweep that would make it a heuristic finding instead.
4. **None of the above?** No decisive proof, no broad numerical sweep,
   no strong motivating argument either way: **Genuinely open.**

Then, whichever tier the claim landed in, ask one more question every
time: **what specific additional result would move this claim up a
tier?** A vague "more research" does not count as an answer. A precise
classification names a concrete target (a general proof, a benchmark
across a stated instance class, a restricted-case theorem extended to
the general case); a vague gesture at uncertainty does not.

</DefinitionBox>

Tier 2 (heuristic) and Tier 3 (conjecture) both sit inside what the
complexity capstone called "strongly-evidenced conjecture": this
lesson's split exists because *how* a claim is evidenced changes what
would settle it. A heuristic algorithm's performance claim is normally
upgraded by a proof of a matching worst-case bound; a conjecture like
factoring's classical hardness is normally upgraded by an unconditional
lower bound. Different target, different research program, same
underlying "not yet proven" status.

## Worked Examples: Four Claims, Four Verdicts

Four claims, four different answers to the checklist, all drawn directly
from real results built earlier in this curriculum.

### (a) Grover's algorithm's optimality: Theorem

**The claim:** No black-box quantum algorithm can search an unsorted
list of size $N$ in fewer than $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ queries: Grover's
algorithm is optimal, not merely good.

**Checklist:** Question 1: yes. Two separate, complete, checkable
mathematical arguments establish the bound, the adversary method and
the polynomial method, each sufficient on its own. Tier 1 evidence
does not get much stronger than a theorem proved twice.

<TheoremBox title="Grover optimality, doubly proven" provenance="cited">

Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is proven optimal in the
black-box model by two independent techniques: the adversary method and
the polynomial method (Query Complexity and Lower Bounds; restated in the
Quantum Complexity Theory capstone). No future algorithmic cleverness
can beat this bound in that model. The question is closed.

</TheoremBox>

**What would move it up a tier:** Nothing. It is already Tier 1, the
ceiling of the checklist. (The open question nearby is different:
whether query-complexity techniques like these can be extended to
*unconditional circuit-complexity* separations outside the black-box
model. On its own checklist that question lands at the bottom, and the
capstone lists it as open.)

### (b) QAOA's real-world performance: mostly heuristic

**The claim:** QAOA finds near-optimal solutions to large, practically
relevant Max-Cut instances.

**Checklist:** Question 1: no general proof exists for this claim as
stated, and the QAOA lessons themselves say so. QAOA and Combinatorial
Optimization warns against assuming QAOA always finds the exact maximum
cut, and the worked Max-Cut example is plain that how the approximation
ratio improves with circuit depth $p$, and whether it can be pushed
arbitrarily close to $1$ for hard instances, is an open, actively
studied question. The two worked examples there (a single edge and a
3-node triangle, both solved by grid search and checked against
`bruteForceMaxCut`) demonstrate the *mechanism* on tiny, fully
checkable instances, not a general performance guarantee, and that
lesson warns against assuming its near-100% result generalizes to
larger, harder graphs.

Question 2: yes, at the level of the broader field. QAOA's approximation
ratio on large, practically-sized instances is evaluated the way most
heuristic algorithms are evaluated anywhere in computer science: by
benchmarking across many problem instances and circuit depths, not by a
matching general proof.

Question 3 is worth a beat, though, because this claim is mixed rather
than purely heuristic. The *original* QAOA construction (Farhi,
Goldstone & Gutmann 2014) does carry a proven guarantee:
a specific worst-case approximation ratio for $p=1$ QAOA on a specific
restricted graph class (3-regular graphs). That is a Tier 1 result for
one $p$ and one instance family, not for "large,
practically relevant instances" in general, which is the claim under
evaluation here. This restricted-case-versus-general-case gap is the
one How to Read a Quantum Computing Paper's worked example was built
to catch.

<ResearchConnection
  title="QAOA's original worst-case approximation guarantee"
  source="Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann (2014)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.4028"
>
  The construction that introduced QAOA proves a specific worst-case
  approximation ratio for $p=1$ QAOA, but only on a restricted graph
  class: 3-regular graphs. It is a real Tier 1 theorem for that scope,
  not a general performance guarantee for the large, practical instances
  a headline claim usually means.
</ResearchConnection>

**Classification:** Tier 2, heuristic/empirical, for the claim as stated
("real-world performance on large, practical instances"), sitting
alongside a narrower Tier 1 result for the $p=1$, restricted-graph-class
special case.

**What would move it up a tier:** A proof of a matching worst-case
approximation-ratio bound for QAOA at general depth $p$, on the actual
instance classes practitioners care about: not a restricted graph
family, and not limited to $p=1$. This is active research, and
its difficulty is why the claim currently sits at Tier 2, not Tier 1.

### (c) Factoring's classical hardness: Conjecture

**The claim:** No efficient (polynomial-time) classical algorithm exists
for integer factoring: the assumption underlying RSA's security and the
real significance of Shor's algorithm.

**Checklist:** Question 1: no proof exists; nobody has an unconditional
lower bound ruling out a fast classical factoring algorithm. Question 2:
no. This isn't a broad-numerical-sweep kind of claim; there is no
"benchmark" that would even make sense here. Question 3: yes. The
evidence is decades of attempted classical attacks, none successful,
reinforced by factoring's structural relationship to other believed-hard
number-theoretic problems. That is motivated evidence.
It is not a proof.

**Classification:** Tier 3, conjecture. This matches the capstone's own
classification, carried over unchanged, since the claim is a pure
complexity-theoretic belief statement, not an algorithm's performance
claim.

**What would move it up a tier:** An unconditional proof that no
polynomial-time classical factoring algorithm exists. Nobody has any
credible strategy for proving this today, which is itself part of why
it remains a conjecture rather than a heuristic (there is no numerical
sweep that could settle it either) or an open question with no
supporting argument at all (the decades of failed attacks are real
evidence, just not proof).

### (d) Barren plateaus: a mixed case

**The claim:** "Variational quantum circuits become untrainable as the
number of qubits grows: their gradients vanish."

This one does not have a single answer, and pulling it apart is the
point. Barren Plateaus and Variational Trainability actually proves two
separate results with two separate epistemic statuses:

- **The gradient's mean is exactly zero.** The lesson derives this
  fully, in closed form, for *any* joint distribution of the other
  parameters, at every circuit depth and every qubit count. Checklist
  Question 1: yes, unconditionally. **Theorem.**
- **The gradient's *variance* vanishes exponentially in qubit count.**
  This is the actual barren-plateau phenomenon, and its scope is part
  of the statement: it holds for sufficiently deep, sufficiently random
  ansätze and global cost functions, proved via concentration of
  measure (Lévy's lemma, cited to McClean, Boixo, Smelyanskiy, Babbush
  & Neven 2018). A rigorous proof for that ansatz class, not for
  every possible variational circuit design. Checklist Question 1: yes,
  *for a restricted class of circuits*. A proven theorem with a stated
  scope, not a universal one.

The lesson's own numerical verification (scaling a real ansatz from
$n=2$ to $6$ qubits, $300$ samples per size, a seeded deterministic
generator) is Question-2-shaped supporting evidence that the scoped
theorem holds up in practice. The numerics also get messy in an
instructive place: at *insufficient* circuit depth, the measured
variance at $n=2\to3$ *increased* before decreasing. A reproducible
wrinkle like that is what separates an honest numerical check from a
cherry-picked one.

**Classification:** Tier 1 (zero-mean gradient, unconditionally) **plus**
Tier 1-for-a-restricted-class (vanishing variance, for sufficiently
deep/random ansätze and global costs), reinforced by Tier-2-style direct
numerical confirmation. The claim that goes wrong is the *unscoped*
version, "variational circuits are always untrainable at scale". That
same lesson's second Common Mistakes callout flags it directly:
shallower, problem-tailored ansätze and *local* cost functions are
partially effective mitigations. The unscoped claim overclaims a
narrower proven result, the same way an unqualified "exponential
speedup" can overclaim an oracle-model theorem.

**What would move the restricted-class result up to fully general:**
Nothing can. A circuit that is *not* deep and expressive enough to
approximate Haar-randomness, or a *local* rather than global cost
function, falls outside the proven theorem's own hypotheses.
"Generalizing" it further isn't a missing proof so much as a different
(and, for many ansätze, false) claim.

### Practice Round: Classify Four More Claims

Same checklist, four new claims, drawn again from the curriculum's own
content.

**Practice claim 1: "The surface code's threshold is approximately
1%."** Full walkthrough:

- **Step 1, what was actually shown:** Decoding Surface Codes reports a
  well-established empirical and theoretical relationship between
  logical error rate $p_L$ and code distance $d$ below some threshold
  $p_{th}$, with $p_{th}$ cited in real work as roughly percent-level
  (commonly in the ballpark of $\sim 1\%$ for circuit-level noise with
  a strong decoder).
- **Step 2, Question 1: is the *specific number* proven?** No. The
  threshold is decoder- and noise-model-dependent, not a single
  universal constant that applies to every code variant or every
  decoder equally. A single clean percent-level number is not what any
  proof delivers.
- **Step 3, Question 2: is there strong numerical evidence?** Yes:
  simulation and experiment on real surface-code patches, estimating
  the threshold for a specific noise model and decoder.
- **Step 4, is there *also* real theory underneath it?** Yes, and it
  deserves to be separated out. The *scaling law's shape*,
  $p_L\sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$, is tied to a phase-transition
  argument (the Nishimori point of a random-bond Ising model, Dennis,
  Kitaev, Landahl & Preskill 2002). That is citable theory, but
  it proves the *functional form* of the suppression, not the specific
  $\sim1\%$ numerical value for any given decoder and noise model.

  <ResearchConnection
    title="The surface-code threshold's scaling law"
    source="Dennis, Kitaev, Landahl & Preskill (2002)"
    url="https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0110143"
  >
    Ties the logical-error suppression $p_L\sim A(p/p_{th})^{(d+1)/2}$ to
    a phase-transition argument at the Nishimori point of a random-bond
    Ising model. Real, citable theory for the *shape* of the curve; it
    does not by itself pin down the specific $\sim 1\%$ numerical
    threshold for any particular decoder and noise model.
  </ResearchConnection>
- **Step 5, classification:** Tier 2, heuristic/empirical, for the
  specific $\sim1\%$ figure: a well-supported, decoder-specific
  numerical estimate, resting on (but not equal to) a proven theoretical
  scaling law.
- **Step 6, what would move it up a tier:** An analytic derivation of
  the *exact* threshold value for a fully specified decoder and noise
  model, from first principles rather than from simulation. That is a
  much harder target than proving the scaling law's shape, which is
  already done.

**Practice claim 2: "Random circuit sampling is classically hard."**
Already classified in full by the Quantum Complexity Theory capstone:
Tier 3, conjecture. A citable theoretical basis (the
polynomial-hierarchy-collapse argument) provides Question-3-style
motivated evidence, but that basis itself rests on an unproven, if
widely believed, assumption, so the claim never reaches Question 1.

**Practice claim 3: "Below a constant physical error rate, concatenated
quantum error correction drives logical error arbitrarily close to
zero."** The Threshold Theorem proves this outright: independently
established by Shor, Aharonov–Ben-Or, Kitaev, Knill–Laflamme–Zurek and
others via the concatenation recursion $p_{k+1}\approx cp_k^2$, unrolled
to a double-exponential collapse below threshold. Question 1: yes.
**Theorem.** (Contrast this with practice claim 1: *that* a threshold
exists and *what happens* on either side of it is proven; *where,
numerically,* that threshold sits for a real decoder and noise model is
the separate, heuristic question.)

**Practice claim 4: "Quantum computers will eventually be shown to
solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time" (i.e., $\mathsf{NP}
\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$).** Question 1: no proof either way. Question 2:
no broad numerical sweep would even settle a question about worst-case
asymptotic complexity. Question 3 is the interesting part: there isn't
a strong *motivated* argument for this claim, either. If anything, the
field's evidence (Grover's proven optimality, BBBV, the adversary- and
polynomial-method lower bounds) points the other way, in the black-box
model. But that evidence is scoped to black-box query complexity, and
once explicitly-given, structured instances are considered, the
capstone's verdict holds: not even a strong heuristic argument points
either way. **Genuinely open**, and open in the specific sense of "no
decisive evidence in either direction for the general, structured
setting," not "open because nobody has looked."

## Unproven Is Not Untrustworthy

<InsightBlock>
  "Not yet proven" and "not worth trusting" are entirely different
  claims: a well-supported heuristic sits in the same respectable
  company as simulated annealing and industrial SAT solvers, not in some
  lesser category invented for quantum algorithms.
</InsightBlock>

Being unable to tell "proven" from "well-supported heuristic" from
"hopeful conjecture" is one of the most common sources of confusion and
overclaiming in how quantum computing results get reported. A headline
says "proven," a press release says "breakthrough," and a casual reader
has no tool for asking which of this lesson's four tiers the underlying
claim occupies. Worse, the confusion runs in both directions. Treating
a heuristic as a theorem overclaims it. Treating a heuristic as
*worthless because it isn't a theorem* is its own serious error, and
the one this lesson's QAOA example was chosen to correct. Heuristic
algorithms with strong empirical support but no general worst-case
proof are not a quantum-computing quirk; they are one of the most
common and useful categories in *all* of computer science. Simulated
annealing has driven real optimization results for decades with no
general convergence-rate guarantee for hard instances. Many of the SAT
solvers used in real industrial verification pipelines have no proven
polynomial bound on worst-case runtime and are used anyway, constantly,
because they work extremely well on the instances that actually show up
in practice. QAOA sits in that company, not in some lesser category
invented for quantum algorithms specifically.

None of this skill is specific to quantum computing, either. Proof,
broad empirical support, motivated-but-unverified belief, or open: the
same four questions evaluate *any* technical claim you will ever
encounter, inside or outside this field. A machine-learning paper's
benchmark numbers, a medical study's effect size, an economic model's
prediction, all classify the same way. Quantum computing is simply
where you practiced, on results precise and well-documented enough to
classify with confidence.

## Collapsing the Tiers Into One Word

<Callout type="note">
**Reporting a well-supported heuristic as a proven theorem.** "QAOA
solves Max-Cut" and "Grover's algorithm is provably optimal" are not the
same kind of sentence, even though both sound equally confident. Before
repeating either claim, ask this lesson's Question 1: is there an actual
proof, or is the confidence coming from broad numerical support instead?
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Dismissing a heuristic as unscientific or worthless because it isn't a
proven theorem.** QAOA's lack of a general worst-case guarantee places
it in the same epistemic category as simulated annealing and most
practical SAT solvers: useful, extensively studied, and completely
normal for a heuristic algorithm to be. "Not yet proven" and "not worth
studying" are entirely different claims, and conflating them is the
mirror image of the first mistake above.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Consider these two related claims about the same computational
   problem: "Shor's algorithm factors an $n$-bit integer using
   $O(n^3)$-scale quantum gates" and "no efficient classical algorithm
   for factoring exists." Run this lesson's four-question checklist on
   each separately, and explain why they land in different tiers even
   though both describe the exact same problem.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Tier 1 and Tier 3, and the gap between them is the whole reason this
   checklist exists.

   **Claim 1: Shor's gate count. Theorem.** Question 1 is answered yes
   immediately. The algorithm is a construction: modular exponentiation
   built from reversible arithmetic, a quantum Fourier transform, and a
   classical continued-fraction post-processing step, each with a derived
   gate count and an analyzed success probability. Nothing about the
   claim is measured or extrapolated; the count comes from adding up the
   circuit. The checklist stops at Question 1.

   One precision worth attaching, since it is the kind of clause this
   lesson exists to notice. The exponent is implementation-dependent:
   $O(n^3)$ assumes schoolbook modular multiplication, and faster
   multiplication algorithms improve it, while nearest-neighbour
   connectivity makes it worse. The robust theorem is "polynomial-size
   uniform circuit family," and the cubic is one instantiation of it. So
   the claim as worded is Tier 1 for a stated arithmetic subroutine and
   the *polynomiality* is Tier 1 unconditionally.

   **Claim 2: no efficient classical factoring algorithm. Conjecture.**
   Question 1: no. There is no unconditional lower bound, and nobody is
   close to one. Question 2: no, and the reason is worth stating rather
   than skipping, because "decades of failed attempts" *sounds* like
   empirical evidence. It is not a numerical sweep across test cases. A
   heuristic finding is a regularity that has been *observed*; this is
   the absence of a discovery, which is a different kind of thing and is
   settled by a different kind of result. Question 3: yes. There is a
   real motivating basis, namely centuries of number theory that has
   produced nothing better than the subexponential general number field
   sieve, plus the largest adversarial test ever conducted, which is that
   public-key infrastructure has stood on the assumption in the open for
   decades. **Tier 3, conjecture.**

   What would move it up: an unconditional superpolynomial lower bound.
   That has a price tag attached, since factoring's decision version is
   in $\mathsf{NP}$, so proving it is not in $\mathsf{P}$ proves
   $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ on the way past. Anyone promoting this
   claim to Tier 1 has also settled the field's most famous open problem.

   **Why the same problem yields two different tiers.** The two claims
   have opposite logical shapes.

   The first is an existence claim about one specific object: here is a
   circuit, here is its size. Existence claims are settled by exhibiting
   the object, and exhibiting is something a person can do.

   The second is a universal non-existence claim quantified over every
   classical algorithm that could ever be written. Nobody can enumerate
   that space, so the only route is a lower-bound technique, and
   complexity theory has essentially none that bite against general
   classical computation at this level.

   That asymmetry is not special to factoring. **Almost every quantum
   advantage claim is half theorem and half conjecture in exactly this
   way**: the quantum side is a construction and is provable, the
   classical side is a non-existence claim and is not. Reading a speedup
   claim well starts with splitting it along that seam and tiering the
   two halves separately.

   </details>
2. Classify the claim "the HHL algorithm provides an exponential speedup
   for solving linear systems of equations." Be precise about which
   restrictions are structurally *part of* the claim before it can be
   evaluated at all (the matrix must be sparse and well-conditioned, and
   the algorithm outputs a quantum state encoding the solution rather
   than the solution vector itself), the same way this lesson's barren-
   plateau example required specifying "sufficiently deep, sufficiently
   random ansätze" before its variance-decay theorem applied. State
   whether the unrestricted, unqualified version of the claim survives
   Question 1.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Fully qualified, it is **Tier 1**. Unqualified, it does not reach
   Question 1 at all, because it is not unproven, it is false.

   **The restrictions, and each one is load-bearing rather than
   decorative.**

   *Sparsity, or an efficient block encoding.* The algorithm begins at
   "there is a cheap circuit implementing $A$." For an arbitrary dense
   matrix handed over as $N^2$ numbers, there is not, and merely reading
   the input already costs $\Omega(N^2)$, which ends the discussion.

   *Well-conditioning.* The cost carries a factor of the condition number
   $\kappa$, so an exponential advantage requires $\kappa$ to stay
   polylogarithmic in $N$. This is the restriction most often waved past
   and it has the sharpest teeth. The Applications lesson computes
   $\kappa = 4.46\times10^{11}$ for a discretized one-dimensional
   Laplacian at $N=2^{20}$, a completely ordinary matrix, which turns an
   estimate of 14 queries into $3\times10^{12}$.

   *Efficient preparation of $|b\rangle$.* If loading the right-hand side
   costs $\Omega(N)$, the speedup is spent before the algorithm starts.

   *The output is a state, not a vector.* This one changes what problem
   is being solved. The algorithm produces $|x\rangle\propto
   A^{-1}|b\rangle$, and extracting all $N$ components costs
   $\Omega(N)$ measurements. Only a limited class of global properties,
   expectation values $\langle x|M|x\rangle$ for efficiently measurable
   $M$, come out cheaply.

   **With all four in place, Question 1 is answered yes,** and better
   than for most speedup claims. There is a proof of the runtime, and,
   unusually, a matching hardness result: the problem in this restricted
   form is BQP-complete, so a classical algorithm running in
   $\mathrm{poly}(\log N)$ would imply $\mathsf{BQP}=\mathsf{BPP}$. That
   puts the classical half of the claim on a named complexity assumption
   rather than on "nobody has found one," which is a genuinely stronger
   footing than factoring's.

   **Unqualified, the claim fails, and not for lack of evidence.** Drop
   well-conditioning and there is no speedup, demonstrably, on matrices
   people actually solve. Drop the state-output caveat and the speedup is
   provably gone, since the readout alone is linear in $N$. "Solving
   linear systems of equations," as an unrestricted English sentence, is
   a problem HHL does not solve. So the unqualified version is not a Tier
   2 or Tier 3 claim awaiting evidence. It is a false statement, and the
   checklist's first job here is to notice that the restrictions are part
   of the *claim* rather than caveats attached to it.

   **One further narrowing worth knowing, because it moved after the
   original paper.** Dequantization results starting with Tang's work
   showed that for *low-rank* matrices with sample-and-query access,
   classical algorithms also achieve polylogarithmic scaling, removing
   the exponential advantage in that regime. So the surviving territory
   is narrower than "sparse and well-conditioned" suggested in 2009: it
   is sparse-but-not-low-rank, well-conditioned, cheaply loadable, and
   read out only through global properties. That is a real set of
   problems and it is a much smaller room than the abstract implies. It
   is also a clean example of the field narrowing its own claim, which is
   what the Evaluating Quantum Advantage lesson argues is healthy rather
   than embarrassing.

   </details>

3. Classify the claim "BB84 quantum key distribution is unconditionally
   secure against any eavesdropping strategy, including future ones."
   Then state the specific additional assumption (about the physical
   implementation, not the idealized protocol) that would need to be
   violated for a real-world BB84 deployment to fall outside that
   guarantee, in the same spirit as this lesson's barren-plateau example
   separating a proven theorem from the unscoped claim it gets mistaken
   for.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Tier 1, for the idealized protocol.** Complete security proofs
   exist, from Mayers and from Shor and Preskill, later recast in
   composable frameworks. The guarantee is information-theoretic: it
   holds against an adversary limited only by quantum mechanics, with no
   assumption about the eavesdropper's computational power. That is what
   "including future ones" earns, and it is a real and remarkable
   property that no computational cryptosystem has.

   **What "unconditional" actually means, since the word oversells
   itself.** It means "not conditional on a *computational* hardness
   assumption." It does not mean "conditional on nothing." The proofs
   assume the devices behave as modeled, that no information leaks from
   the two labs by any channel outside the model, that the random number
   generators are genuinely random, and that the classical channel used
   for basis reconciliation is authenticated, which itself requires a
   short pre-shared secret. Every one of those is an assumption, and each
   is about the world rather than about mathematics.

   **The implementation assumption to name: single-photon sources.** The
   proof assumes each pulse carries one photon in the chosen state. Real
   deployments use attenuated laser pulses, whose photon number is
   Poisson-distributed, so a fraction of pulses contain two or more
   identical photons. That opens the **photon-number-splitting attack**:
   Eve keeps one photon from every multi-photon pulse, forwards the rest
   untouched, waits for the basis announcement, and measures. She learns
   those bits completely while introducing **no error at all**, so the
   protocol's own eavesdropper test, an elevated error rate, never fires.
   Over a lossy channel Eve can block the single-photon pulses and pass
   only the multi-photon ones, and the security proof's conclusion simply
   does not apply to the system being run.

   That is the exact structure of the barren-plateau case. A proven
   theorem about a stated model gets repeated as an unscoped claim about
   a deployed system, and the sentence stays true right up until someone
   builds the thing.

   **The repair, and it is a good one.** Decoy-state BB84, due to Hwang
   and to Lo, Ma, and Chen, sends pulses at several randomly chosen
   intensities and compares their transmission yields. A
   photon-number-splitting attacker cannot make the different intensities
   behave consistently, so the attack becomes detectable, and a provable
   key rate is restored for weak coherent sources. The claim returns to
   Tier 1 with the model corrected rather than the caveat waived, which
   is the right way for this to be fixed.

   **Why the caveat should be taken seriously rather than noted
   politely.** Commercial QKD systems running provably secure protocols
   have been broken in the laboratory, by detector-blinding attacks that
   drive avalanche photodiodes into a linear regime the security model
   never described, and by timing side channels. Nothing in those attacks
   contradicts any theorem. They go around the model. The research
   response is device-independent QKD, whose proofs assume only an
   observed Bell-inequality violation and nothing about how the boxes
   work, at a heavy cost in rate and distance. The existence of that
   research programme is the clearest evidence available that the
   implementation assumption in ordinary BB84 is load-bearing.

   </details>

## Summary

- The checklist: (1) is there a complete mathematical proof? → Theorem.
  (2) Strong numerical/simulation evidence across many cases, no general
  proof? → Heuristic/empirical finding. (3) A plausible, motivated
  argument with some real evidence, unverified in the general setting?
  → Conjecture. (4) None of the above? → Genuinely open. Always follow
  up by naming the specific result that would move a claim up a tier.
- Four settled examples: Grover's optimality is a Theorem (proved twice,
  by the adversary and polynomial methods); QAOA's real-world Max-Cut
  performance is mostly Heuristic (a narrow $p=1$, restricted-graph-class
  proof exists, but general large-instance performance is evaluated
  empirically); factoring's classical hardness is a Conjecture (decades
  of failed attacks, no proof); barren plateaus are mixed: a fully
  general, unconditional Theorem (zero-mean gradient) alongside a
  Theorem proven only for a restricted, sufficiently-deep/random ansatz
  class (vanishing variance), reinforced by direct numerical
  confirmation.
- A well-supported heuristic is not a lesser or unscientific result. It
  is one of the most common, useful categories in all of computer
  science, and treating "unproven" as "untrustworthy" is as serious a
  misreading as treating "unproven" as "proven."
- This four-tier classification skill generalizes completely beyond
  quantum computing: it is a reusable tool for evaluating any technical
  claim, in any field, that you did not personally derive yourself.
