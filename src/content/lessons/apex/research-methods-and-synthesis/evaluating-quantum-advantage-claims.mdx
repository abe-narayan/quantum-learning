## Motivation

What would it take for you to believe a "quantum advantage" headline?
You already own most of the answer. Distinguishing Theorem from
Heuristic gave you the vocabulary to sort a result by its epistemic
status: proven, conjectured, or merely observed to work. When Classical
Simulation Works gave you something sharper and more structural: two
specific, checkable reasons a quantum circuit might collapse to
something a laptop can simulate efficiently, namely
Gottesman-Knill/stabilizer structure and bounded bond-dimension growth
(low entanglement, simulable by a matrix product state). Put those two
tools together and you have almost everything you need.

This lesson does not introduce new physics. It assembles a checklist,
five questions in a fixed order, that turns a vague headline into a set
of claims you can actually check, and it applies that checklist to the
field's own real history, including the uncomfortable parts. That
history is not a scandal. Read correctly, it is what a healthy research
field looks like from the outside, and knowing how to read it that way
is the point of this lesson.

## The Checklist

Given any claim of the form "quantum computer X achieved quantum advantage
(or supremacy) on task Y," ask these five questions, in this order. Each
one either survives the claim or narrows what the claim is actually saying.

<DefinitionBox title="The five-question quantum advantage checklist">

1. **What specific computational task was actually solved or sampled
   from?** Not the press-release description: the literal input/output
   specification. "Simulating a molecule" and "sampling from a specific
   random circuit's output distribution" are different tasks with
   completely different downstream implications, even if both get called
   "quantum advantage."
2. **What specific classical algorithm and hardware was it compared
   against, and was that the *best known* classical approach at the
   time, or a weaker baseline?** A claim compared against a naive
   brute-force state-vector simulation is a much weaker claim than one
   compared against the best classical algorithm anyone had published,
   run on comparable hardware.
3. **Does the claimed classical hardness rest on an unconditional proof,
   or a plausible-but-unproven complexity-theoretic assumption?** As the
   Complexity Theory capstone established, essentially every real
   classical-hardness argument for a sampling task rests on something
   like the polynomial hierarchy not collapsing: a real, citable, Tier 2
   argument, not a Tier 1 theorem. Naming the assumption is not a
   weakness in the claim; failing to name it is a weakness in how the
   claim is being reported.
4. **Using this course's own two classical-simulability criteria, does the
   task structurally avoid *both* known efficient-simulation loopholes?**
   Concretely: is the circuit provably far from Clifford/stabilizer
   structure (Gottesman-Knill doesn't apply), and does its entanglement
   grow with system size rather than staying bounded (no efficient
   matrix-product-state representation)? A task that quietly falls into
   either loophole is not hard to classically simulate at all. It just
   hasn't been noticed yet.
5. **Is the demonstrated task practically useful, or specifically
   engineered to be hard for classical computers with no other purpose?**
   This is a separate axis from every question above. A claim can pass
   questions 1–4 cleanly and still be a demonstration of raw hardware
   capability with no direct economic or scientific payoff of its own.
   That is worth knowing explicitly, not discovering later.

</DefinitionBox>

Notice what this checklist is not. It is not a way to debunk every
claim, and it is not a way to declare every claim solid. It is a fixed
set of questions that forces a headline to become five separate,
checkable statements. A claim that answers all five precisely is a
strong claim, in the same measure that a claim dodging two or three of
them is a weak one.

## Physical Interpretation: Why This Matters

<InsightBlock>
  "Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two
  independent axes. A claim that answers one tells you nothing about
  the other, and most public confusion about quantum advantage comes
  from collapsing them into one.
</InsightBlock>

Every one of these five questions maps onto a real distinction that gets
collapsed constantly in how quantum computing results reach the public,
and the collapse runs in both directions.

**The pro-quantum collapse.** A headline says "quantum computer solves
in 200 seconds what would take a classical supercomputer 10,000 years,"
without naming the task, the specific classical algorithm the
10,000-year estimate came from, or the assumption behind the
classical-hardness claim. Readers come away believing something like
"quantum computers are now generally faster than classical ones," a
claim questions 1, 2, and 5 above would immediately show is far too
broad. The actual claim is almost always narrower: *for one specific,
often practically-useless task*, *against one specific classical
baseline*, *under one specific unproven assumption*.

**The anti-quantum collapse.** A follow-up headline says "classical
computer matches quantum supremacy claim, quantum advantage debunked,"
treating an improved classical algorithm's narrowed margin as proof the
entire field is overclaiming or worse. This skips question 3 entirely.
The classical-hardness claim was never an unconditional proof to begin
with, so an improved classical algorithm challenging it isn't a
refutation of anything. It is the assumption being tested the way a
conjecture should be tested.

**The most important single distinction: hard-to-simulate versus useful.**
Question 5 deserves to be stated as its own principle, because conflating
it with questions 1–4 is the single most common source of public confusion
about what these results mean:

<Callout type="note">
**"Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two separate
axes.** A task can be hard to simulate and useless (most random circuit
sampling experiments), useful and easy to simulate for most real
instances (many chemistry problems on small molecules), or useful *and*
hard to simulate for the instances that matter (the open goal of
quantum simulation for classically-intractable molecules and
materials), which is the valuable target. Treating "hard to simulate"
as a synonym for "important" or vice versa produces the whiplash
between overclaiming and dismissiveness described above.
</Callout>

Random circuit sampling and boson-sampling-style demonstrations are
**deliberately** chosen because they satisfy question 4 cleanly: they
sit as far as possible from both Gottesman-Knill structure and bounded
bond-dimension growth, which is what makes them a clean test of raw
quantum hardware capability and indirect evidence for a real
quantum-classical complexity separation. That is real scientific value.
But the task itself, sampling from one specific random circuit's output
distribution, has essentially no direct economic or scientific use on
its own. Nobody wants that particular sample for its own sake.

Contrast that with Simulation and Compilation Frontiers' molecular
ground-state energy problem. Finding a molecule's ground-state energy
is independently, practically valuable (to chemistry, to materials
design, to drug discovery) regardless of whether that energy turns out
to be classically easy or classically hard to compute for any specific
molecule. Usefulness there doesn't depend on hardness at all. Some
molecules are classically tractable (weak entanglement growth, question
4 fails to apply) and still worth computing; others are believed
classically intractable and would be worth computing precisely because
a quantum computer could reach them. The task's value and the task's
hardness answer two different questions, and a claim that only answers
one of them has told you half of what you need to know.

## Worked Example: Classifying a Real Pattern

**Apply the checklist to the following, carefully scoped statement: "A
2019 superconducting-processor experiment claimed a large classical-runtime
gap for a random-circuit-sampling task; subsequent classical algorithms
have, more than once, narrowed or contested the classical-hardness margin
for specific instances of this kind of experiment. Does this mean quantum
advantage claims in this line of work are unreliable?"**

The statement is scoped deliberately, but it is not hypothetical, and the
checklist is easier to trust once you can see the actual papers on both
sides of it.

**Question 1, the specific task.** Sampling from the output distribution of one particular family of random quantum circuits on a fixed number of qubits and circuit depth.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="'Best known at the time' is a moving target, not a permanent ceiling. That distinction is what the rest of this reasoning turns on.">
**Question 2, the classical baseline.** The original classical-runtime estimate was produced by the best classical simulation technique known and published *at that time*.
</DerivationStep>

**Question 3, the assumption.** The classical-hardness argument rests on a real, citable complexity-theoretic argument tied to the polynomial hierarchy not collapsing: Tier 2, not Tier 1.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="This is why circuits like this were chosen as the demonstration task in the first place.">
**Question 4, the structural check.** Built from gates outside the Clifford group (no Gottesman-Knill shortcut), run deep enough that entanglement grows across the whole system (no efficient matrix-product-state description).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Its value is entirely as a capability benchmark and as empirical evidence bearing on Question 3's underlying complexity assumption, not as a computation anyone wants for its own sake.">
**Question 5, usefulness.** None, directly. The sampling task has no independent economic or scientific application.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

**Now the actual question: does classical progress against these claims
mean the line of work is unreliable?** No, and the reasoning matters
more than the conclusion:

- Improving classical algorithms *in direct response to* a quantum
  claim is the correct scientific process, provided the original claim
  was careful about what it asserted at the time (question 2's "best
  known at the time," not "best possible ever"). A conjecture that
  invites attempts to break it, and gets tested by them, is a
  conjecture behaving as a conjecture should.
- A narrowed margin is still often a real, if smaller, advantage. Going
  from "10,000 classical years" to "a few classical days" for a
  specific instance is a massive change in the classical-hardness
  margin, but it does not necessarily erase an advantage entirely, and
  each specific case has to be checked on its own terms rather than
  treated as a uniform "debunking."
- Designing new demonstrations specifically harder for the improved
  classical algorithms to catch is itself legitimate, valuable ongoing
  research: an arms-race dynamic that shows up constantly elsewhere in
  algorithm design (cryptography's own history of proposed schemes and
  subsequent attacks is the clearest classical parallel) and is not
  unique to, or discrediting of, quantum computing specifically.

The honest summary: *this pattern (real, documented more than once,
well known within the field's own literature) is the community's
healthy self-correction process working in public, not a sign the
underlying research program is broken.*

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Treating a narrowed quantum-advantage margin after improved classical
algorithms as proof the original claim was fraudulent or the field is
broken.** It means science is working correctly. The original claim
rested on a stated, plausible-but-unproven assumption (question 3) and
the best classical approach known *at the time* (question 2), both
explicitly provisional by their own nature. A later, better classical
algorithm testing that provisional claim, and narrowing or contesting
it for specific instances, is the field checking its own extraordinary
claims the way a healthy field should, provided the original
researchers were careful about what they claimed. The failure mode
would be an original claim asserting unconditional, permanent classical
impossibility. That would be false regardless of what happened later,
and it is not, in the well-documented cases, what careful original
papers actually asserted.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" as the
same axis, or assuming one implies the other.** A task engineered to be
maximally hard for classical computers (question 4 passing cleanly)
tells you nothing about question 5, and a practically important task (a
real molecule, a real optimization problem) tells you nothing about
question 4 on its own. Some useful instances are classically easy, some
are hard, and which is which is often the entire open research
question. Reporting
that treats every "quantum advantage" demonstration as if it were also
economically significant, or dismisses every useful-but-currently-easy
quantum computation for lacking a supremacy claim, is making the same
category error in opposite directions.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A "quantum advantage" claim decomposes into five checkable questions:
  the specific task, the specific classical baseline (and whether it was
  actually the best known one), the specific assumption behind any claimed
  classical hardness, whether the task structurally avoids both of this
  course's classical-simulability loopholes (Gottesman-Knill structure and
  bounded bond-dimension growth), and whether the task is practically
  useful or specifically engineered to be hard.
- The field's real, well-documented pattern of classical algorithms
  subsequently narrowing or contesting specific classical-hardness
  margins is healthy self-correction, not fraud or failure, provided the
  original claims were scoped to "best known classical approach at the
  time" rather than an unconditional, permanent impossibility.
- "Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two separate
  axes. Random circuit sampling occupies hard-to-simulate/low-usefulness by
  design; a molecule's ground-state energy is independently useful
  regardless of where it lands on the hardness axis; conflating the two
  axes is a common, real source of public confusion about what these
  results mean.
- The practical payoff: turning a vague, unevaluable headline into five
  concrete, checkable questions is the single most useful skill for
  assessing any current or future claim about quantum computing's
  progress.
