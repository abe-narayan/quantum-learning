## Motivation

Distinguishing Theorem from Heuristic gave you the vocabulary to sort a
result by its epistemic status — proven, conjectured, or merely observed to
work. When Classical Simulation Works, from this pillar's simulation and
compilation course, gave you something sharper and more structural: two
specific, checkable reasons a quantum circuit might collapse to something a
laptop can simulate efficiently — Gottesman-Knill/stabilizer structure, and
bounded bond-dimension growth (low entanglement, simulable by a matrix
product state). Put those two tools together and you already have almost
everything you need to evaluate a claim of the form "quantum computer
achieves quantum advantage."

This lesson does not introduce new physics. It assembles a checklist,
five questions, in a fixed order, that turns a vague headline into a set
of claims you can actually check, and it applies that checklist honestly to
the field's own real history, including the uncomfortable parts. That
history is not a scandal. Read correctly, it is exactly what a healthy
research field should look like from the outside, and knowing how to read
it that way is the actual point of this lesson.

## The Checklist

Given any claim of the form "quantum computer X achieved quantum advantage
(or supremacy) on task Y," ask these five questions, in this order. Each
one either survives the claim or narrows exactly what it's actually saying.

<DefinitionBox title="The five-question quantum advantage checklist">

1. **What specific computational task was actually solved or sampled
   from?** Not the press-release description — the literal input/output
   specification. "Simulating a molecule" and "sampling from a specific
   random circuit's output distribution" are different tasks with
   completely different downstream implications, even if both get called
   "quantum advantage."
2. **What specific classical algorithm and hardware was it compared
   against — and was that genuinely the *best known* classical approach at
   the time, or a weaker baseline?** A claim compared against a naive
   brute-force state-vector simulation is a much weaker claim than one
   compared against the best classical algorithm anyone had published, run
   on comparable hardware.
3. **Does the claimed classical hardness rest on an unconditional proof,
   or a plausible-but-unproven complexity-theoretic assumption?** As the
   Complexity Theory capstone established, essentially every real
   classical-hardness argument for a sampling task rests on something like
   the polynomial hierarchy not collapsing — a real, citable, Tier 2
   argument, not a Tier 1 theorem. Naming the assumption is not a weakness
   in the claim; failing to name it is a weakness in how the claim is being
   reported.
4. **Using this course's own two classical-simulability criteria, does the
   task structurally avoid *both* known efficient-simulation loopholes?**
   Concretely: is the circuit provably far from Clifford/stabilizer
   structure (Gottesman-Knill doesn't apply), and does its entanglement
   genuinely grow with system size rather than staying bounded (no
   efficient matrix-product-state representation)? A task that secretly
   falls into either loophole is not hard to classically simulate at all —
   it just hasn't been noticed yet.
5. **Is the demonstrated task practically useful, or specifically
   engineered to be hard for classical computers with no other purpose?**
   This is a genuinely separate axis from every question above. A claim
   can pass questions 1–4 cleanly and still be a demonstration of raw
   hardware capability with no direct economic or scientific payoff on its
   own — and that is worth knowing explicitly, not discovering later.

</DefinitionBox>

Read left to right, that is the whole checklist as one pipeline: each
question either survives the claim or narrows exactly what it's actually
saying, and what comes out the other end is five separate, checkable
statements instead of one unevaluable headline.

Notice what this checklist is not: it is not a way to debunk every claim,
and it is not a way to declare every claim solid. It is a fixed set of
questions that forces a headline to become five separate, checkable
statements — and a claim that answers all five precisely is a genuinely
strong claim, exactly as much as a claim that dodges two or three of them
is a weak one.

## Physical Interpretation: Why This Matters

<InsightBlock>
  "Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two
  independent axes — a claim that answers one tells you nothing about
  the other, and most public confusion about quantum advantage comes
  from collapsing them into one.
</InsightBlock>

Every one of these five questions maps onto a real distinction that gets
collapsed constantly in how quantum computing results reach the public,
and the collapse runs in both directions.

**The pro-quantum collapse.** A headline says "quantum computer solves in
200 seconds what would take a classical supercomputer 10,000 years,"
without naming the task, the specific classical algorithm the 10,000-year
estimate came from, or the assumption behind the classical-hardness claim.
Readers come away believing something like "quantum computers are now
generally faster than classical ones" — a claim questions 1, 2, and 5
above would immediately show is far too broad. The actual claim is almost
always narrower: *for one specific, often practically-useless task*,
*against one specific classical baseline*, *under one specific unproven
assumption*.

**The anti-quantum collapse.** A follow-up headline says "classical
computer matches quantum supremacy claim, quantum advantage debunked,"
treating an improved classical algorithm's narrowed margin as proof the
entire field is overclaiming or worse. This skips question 3 entirely: the
classical-hardness claim was never an unconditional proof to begin with,
so an improved classical algorithm challenging it isn't a refutation of
anything — it's the assumption being tested exactly the way an honest
conjecture should be tested.

**The most important single distinction: hard-to-simulate versus useful.**
Question 5 deserves to be stated as its own principle, because conflating
it with questions 1–4 is the single most common source of public confusion
about what these results mean:

<Callout type="note">
**"Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two separate
axes.** A task can be hard to simulate and useless (most random circuit
sampling experiments), useful and easy to simulate for most real instances
(many chemistry problems on small molecules), or — the genuinely valuable
target — useful *and* hard to simulate for the instances that matter (the
open goal of quantum simulation for classically-intractable molecules and
materials). Treating "hard to simulate" as a synonym for "important" or
vice versa produces exactly the whiplash between overclaiming and
dismissiveness described above.
</Callout>

Random circuit sampling and boson-sampling-style demonstrations are
**deliberately** chosen because they satisfy question 4 cleanly — they sit
as far as possible from both Gottesman-Knill structure and bounded
bond-dimension growth, which is exactly what makes them a clean test of
raw quantum hardware capability and indirect evidence for a genuine
quantum-classical complexity separation. That is real scientific value.
But the task itself — sampling from one specific random circuit's output
distribution — has essentially no direct economic or scientific use on its
own; nobody wants that particular sample for its own sake.

Contrast that with Simulation and Compilation Frontiers' molecular ground-
state energy problem. Finding a molecule's ground-state energy is
independently, practically valuable — to chemistry, to materials design, to
drug discovery — regardless of whether that energy turns out to be
classically easy or classically hard to compute for any specific molecule.
Usefulness there doesn't depend on hardness at all; some molecules are
classically tractable (weak entanglement growth, question 4 fails to
apply) and still worth computing, and others are believed classically
intractable and would be worth computing precisely because a quantum
computer could reach them. The task's value and the task's hardness are
answers to two different questions, and a claim that only answers one of
them has told you only half of what you need to know.

## Worked Example: Classifying a Real Pattern Honestly

**Apply the checklist to the following, carefully honest statement: "A
2019 superconducting-processor experiment claimed a large classical-runtime
gap for a random-circuit-sampling task; subsequent classical algorithms
have, more than once, narrowed or contested the classical-hardness margin
for specific instances of this kind of experiment. Does this mean quantum
advantage claims in this line of work are unreliable?"**

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep annotation="Not a useful computation — a deliberately chosen sampling problem, exactly the level of specificity Question 1 demands.">
**Question 1 — the specific task.** Sampling from the output distribution of one particular family of random quantum circuits on a fixed number of qubits and circuit depth.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="'Best known at the time' is a moving target, not a permanent ceiling — that distinction is exactly what the rest of this reasoning turns on.">
**Question 2 — the classical baseline.** The original classical-runtime estimate was produced by the best classical simulation technique known and published *at that time*.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="This was true on day one, not a detail that emerged later — naming the assumption up front is what makes later classical progress a test, not a refutation.">
**Question 3 — the assumption.** The classical-hardness argument rests on a real, citable complexity-theoretic argument tied to the polynomial hierarchy not collapsing — Tier 2, not Tier 1.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Exactly why circuits like this were chosen as the demonstration task in the first place.">
**Question 4 — the structural check.** Built from gates outside the Clifford group (no Gottesman-Knill shortcut), run deep enough that entanglement grows across the whole system (no efficient matrix-product-state description).
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Its value is entirely as a capability benchmark and as empirical evidence bearing on Question 3's underlying complexity assumption — not as a computation anyone wants for its own sake.">
**Question 5 — usefulness.** None, directly. The sampling task has no independent economic or scientific application.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

**Now the actual question: does classical progress against these claims
mean the line of work is unreliable?** No — and the reasoning matters more
than the conclusion:

- Improving classical algorithms *in direct response to* a quantum claim is
  exactly the correct scientific process, provided the original claim was
  appropriately careful about what it asserted at the time (question 2's
  "best known at the time," not "best possible ever"). A conjecture that
  invites attempts to break it, and gets tested by them, is a conjecture
  behaving exactly as a conjecture should.
- A narrowed margin is still often a real, if smaller, advantage — going
  from "10,000 classical years" to "a few classical days" for a specific
  instance is a massive change in the classical-hardness margin, but it
  does not necessarily erase an advantage entirely, and each specific case
  has to be checked on its own terms rather than treated as a uniform
  "debunking."
- Designing new demonstrations specifically harder for the improved
  classical algorithms to catch is itself legitimate, valuable ongoing
  research — an arms race dynamic that shows up constantly elsewhere in
  algorithm design (cryptography's own history of proposed schemes and
  subsequent attacks is the clearest classical parallel) and is not unique
  to, or discrediting of, quantum computing specifically.

The honest summary sentence: *this pattern — real, more than one
documented instance, well known within the field's own literature — is the
community's healthy self-correction process working in public, not a sign
the underlying research program is broken.*

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Treating a narrowed quantum-advantage margin after improved classical
algorithms as proof the original claim was fraudulent or the field is
broken.** It means science is working correctly. The original claim rested
on a stated, plausible-but-unproven assumption (question 3) and the best
classical approach known *at the time* (question 2) — both explicitly
provisional by their own nature. A later, better classical algorithm
testing that provisional claim, and narrowing or contesting it for
specific instances, is the field checking its own extraordinary claims
exactly the way a healthy field should, provided the original researchers
were appropriately careful about what they claimed. The failure mode would
be an original claim asserting unconditional, permanent classical
impossibility — that would be false regardless of what happened later, and
that is not, in the well-documented cases, what careful original papers
actually asserted.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating "hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" as the
same axis, or assuming one implies the other.** A task engineered to be
maximally hard for classical computers (question 4 passing cleanly) tells
you nothing about question 5, and a practically important task (a real
molecule, a real optimization problem) tells you nothing about question 4
on its own — some useful instances are classically easy, some are hard,
and which is which is often the entire open research question. Reporting
that treats every "quantum advantage" demonstration as if it were also
economically significant, or dismisses every useful-but-currently-easy
quantum computation for lacking a supremacy claim, is making the same
category error in opposite directions.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A "quantum advantage" claim decomposes into five checkable questions:
  the specific task, the specific classical baseline (and whether it was
  actually the best known one), the specific assumption behind any claimed
  classical hardness, whether the task structurally avoids both of this
  course's classical-simulability loopholes (Gottesman-Knill structure and
  bounded bond-dimension growth), and whether the task is practically
  useful or specifically engineered to be hard.
- The field's real, well-documented pattern of classical algorithms
  subsequently narrowing or contesting specific classical-hardness margins
  is healthy self-correction, not fraud or failure — provided the original
  claims were honestly scoped to "best known classical approach at the
  time" rather than an unconditional, permanent impossibility.
- "Hard to classically simulate" and "practically useful" are two separate
  axes. Random circuit sampling occupies hard-to-simulate/low-usefulness by
  design; a molecule's ground-state energy is independently useful
  regardless of where it lands on the hardness axis; conflating the two
  axes is a common, real source of public confusion about what these
  results mean.
- The practical payoff: turning a vague, unevaluable headline into five
  concrete, checkable questions is the single most useful skill for
  assessing any current or future claim about quantum computing's
  progress.
