## Precision About a Sentence Everyone Repeats

BQP and Oracle Complexity did two things carefully: it gave BQP a formal
definition, and it proved that Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon's algorithm, and
Grover's algorithm establish *query*-complexity separations relative to a
black-box oracle, not unconditional *time*-complexity separations between
classical and quantum computation. That lesson deliberately stopped there:
it proved what those specific algorithms do and don't establish, but it
never placed BQP within the broader classical complexity landscape most
popular accounts gesture at with a Venn diagram and move on.

This lesson does that placement. Among the three classes $\mathsf{P}$,
$\mathsf{NP}$, and $\mathsf{BQP}$ there are exactly three proven
containments and exactly three open questions, and conflating a "proven
containment" with an "open question believed one way" is the single most
common way popular writing about quantum computing goes wrong. Getting
this right is also the necessary setup for the rest of this course. QMA
(quantum verification), the Local Hamiltonian problem, and
query-complexity lower bounds all live in the open territory this lesson
maps out.

## Three Classes, Three Containments, Two Open Questions

### Recap: the three classes, precisely

<DefinitionBox title="P (deterministic polynomial time)">

$\mathsf{P}$ is the class of decision problems (languages
$L\subseteq\{0,1\}^*$) solvable by a deterministic classical algorithm
running in time polynomial in the input length $n=|x|$: there exists a
polynomial $p$ and an algorithm that, for every input $x$, halts within
$p(|x|)$ steps and outputs $1$ if $x\in L$ and $0$ otherwise, always
correctly, with zero error.

</DefinitionBox>

<DefinitionBox title="NP (nondeterministic polynomial time, verifier form)">

$\mathsf{NP}$ is the class of decision problems $L$ for which every
YES instance $x\in L$ has a **certificate** $w$ of length polynomial in
$|x|$, verifiable in deterministic polynomial time: there exists a
polynomial-time algorithm $V$ and a polynomial $q$ such that $x\in L$
if and only if there exists $w$ with $|w|\leq q(|x|)$ and $V(x,w)=1$.
Verification is cheap and deterministic; *finding* $w$ is what's
believed to be hard in general.

</DefinitionBox>

<DefinitionBox title="BQP (bounded-error quantum polynomial time)">

$\mathsf{BQP}$ is the class of decision problems solvable by a
polynomial-time-uniform family of quantum circuits $\{C_n\}$, built from
a fixed universal gate set, such that for input $x$ of length $n$,
measuring a designated output qubit of $C_n|x\rangle|0\cdots0\rangle$
outputs $1$ with probability $\geq2/3$ if $x\in L$ and $\leq1/3$ if
$x\notin L$. (Full formal treatment, including what
"polynomial-time-uniform" rules out, is in the prerequisite lesson;
this recap states the definition without re-deriving it.)

</DefinitionBox>

As the prerequisite lesson proved in detail, the specific constants
$2/3,1/3$ don't matter: running $k$ independent copies of $C_n$ and
taking the majority vote drives the failure probability down to
$2^{-\Omega(k)}$ by a Chernoff-Hoeffding bound on sums of independent
bounded random variables, so *any* pair of constants bounded away from
$1/2$ defines the identical class $\mathsf{BQP}$, up to polynomial
overhead in circuit size. That amplification fact is why "the error
bound is $1/3$" is a convention rather than a substantive commitment.
The same reasoning applies verbatim to $\mathsf{BPP}$, the classical
bounded-error analogue.

### The three proven containments

| Containment | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ | **Proven** | Any classical poly-time algorithm can be simulated by a reversible (hence unitary) circuit with only polynomial overhead, then run on a quantum computer that never uses superposition. A classical circuit is a special case of a quantum one. |
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ | **Proven** | A poly-time decision algorithm for $L$ needs no certificate at all: take $V(x,w)=$ "run the $\mathsf{P}$ algorithm on $x$ and ignore $w$" with the trivial empty certificate. |
| $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | **Proven** | A classical algorithm can compute any output amplitude of a poly-size quantum circuit to sufficient precision by summing over computational-basis paths, reusing the same polynomial workspace for each term. That is polynomial *space*, even though the simulation generally takes *exponential time*. |

The $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ containment is worth
dwelling on, because it's easy to state carelessly. It does **not** say
quantum computers are only as powerful as classical ones. It says a
classical computer with enough *memory* (polynomial) can eventually
compute what a quantum computer computes, given enough *time*
(potentially exponential). Space and time are different resources, and
this containment is a statement about space only. It's also not the
tightest containment known ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ is a
strictly stronger, separately proven fact, cited here, not derived),
but $\mathsf{PSPACE}$ is the containment this lesson's diagram, and the
open questions below, actually need.

### The genuinely open questions

Three questions about how $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, and
$\mathsf{BQP}$ relate are **not** settled by anything above, or by
anything currently known in complexity theory. Precision matters more
here than anywhere else in this course.

**(1) Is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?** This asks whether a
quantum computer can efficiently solve *every* problem in $\mathsf{NP}$:
whether some quantum analogue of brute-force certificate search could
be made polynomial in general, the way Grover's algorithm makes
unstructured search quadratically faster but not exponentially faster.
This is **not known**, and it is **widely believed to be false**. The
evidence is real but limited: the prerequisite lesson's BBBV theorem
proves that Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is optimal for
*unstructured black-box search*, meaning no black-box quantum algorithm
can turn an $N$-item search into anything better than a quadratic
speedup, for every possible oracle. A later lesson in this course
(Query Complexity and Lower Bounds) develops this technique further.
But this is suggestive, not conclusive. An $\mathsf{NP}$-complete
problem like 3-SAT is not a black box (its clauses are explicit and
structured), so a black-box lower bound says nothing decisive about
whether some structure-exploiting quantum algorithm could still solve
it in polynomial time. $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ is disbelieved
on the strength of this evidence and the field's decades of failure to
find such an algorithm, not because anyone has proven it false.

**(2) Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$?** This asks whether every
quantum algorithm's YES-answer can be checked by an ordinary,
classical, polynomial-length certificate. Can quantum computation's
power always be *verified* the $\mathsf{NP}$ way after the fact, even
when it cannot be *found* that way? This is also **not known**. Unlike
question (1), there isn't even a strong heuristic argument pointing one
way. Problems believed to lie in $\mathsf{BQP}$ have no known
$\mathsf{NP}$-style classical certificate; the natural "certificate,"
the full quantum computational transcript, isn't classically
polynomial-length or obviously checkable in polynomial time. But the
absence of a *known* certificate is not a proof that none exists. This
question is the direct motivation for this course's next lesson, which
builds $\mathsf{QMA}$, the class where the certificate itself is allowed
to be quantum.

One qualification on "not even a strong heuristic argument," because the
relativized picture has moved and the unrelativized one has not. There is
now an oracle relative to which $\mathsf{BQP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$,
and since $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$, that oracle also separates
$\mathsf{BQP}$ from $\mathsf{NP}$. So there is evidence pointing one way
after all, of the kind this course keeps insisting on scoping:
evidence in the black-box model, which by the relativization barrier
cannot on its own settle the unrelativized question. The honest
statement is therefore narrower than "no argument either way" and wider
than "believed false": the question is open, and the one real piece of
evidence bearing on it lives in a model where the answer is known not to
transfer.

  title="The oracle evidence on question (2)"

  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1411.5729"
>
  Aaronson introduced the problem, under the name Fourier Checking, and
  conjectured that it could separate $\mathsf{BQP}$ from the polynomial

  Forrelation and proved the matching bounds that make it easy for a
  quantum algorithm and provably hard for classical ones in the query
  model. Raz and Tal then proved the conjecture, giving the first oracle

  $\mathsf{PH}$. Read the three as a case study in how relativized
  evidence accumulates: a well-chosen problem, a conjecture about it, a
  proof years later, and a conclusion that is real and still
  silent about the unrelativized world.
</ResearchConnection>

**(3) Is $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$?** This asks the most basic question
of all: do quantum computers give *any* advantage over classical ones
for deciding languages, given unlimited polynomial time on both sides?
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ is **not a proven theorem**. It is
essentially universally believed, on the strength of Shor's algorithm
(examined precisely below), but belief, however strong and however
widely shared among experts, is not proof.

$\mathsf{P}$'s circle sits solidly inside the overlap of the two dashed

The real answer could be that one class contains the other, that they're
equal, or that they overlap only partially the way it's drawn. That is
the content of open questions (1) and (2) above.

### Shor's algorithm's actual complexity-theoretic status

<PredictBeforeReveal
  question="Shor's algorithm gives a polynomial-size quantum circuit that factors integers, with no known classical polynomial-time algorithm for the same task. Before reading the precise analysis below, predict: does this prove P ≠ BQP?"

  correctValue="conditional"
  explanation="Shor's algorithm unconditionally proves factoring is in BQP; that part is a real theorem. So Shor's algorithm is strong conditional evidence for P ≠ BQP, not a separation theorem. The next two sections make the distinction precise."

<TheoremBox title="What Shor's algorithm proves" provenance="cited">

Integer factoring, phrased as a decision problem ("does $N$ have a
nontrivial factor less than $k$?"), is in $\mathsf{BQP}$: there is a
polynomial-size quantum circuit family that solves it with bounded
error, via period-finding and the quantum Fourier transform (built and
run on the factor-15 example in Quantum Algorithms II). No known
classical algorithm solves it in polynomial time.

</TheoremBox>

This is a genuine, unconditional theorem: it is a proven fact that
factoring is in $\mathsf{BQP}$. What it is *not* is a proof that
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$, and the reason requires two separate,
precise observations, both commonly elided in popular accounts:

**Factoring is not known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete.** It sits in
$\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ (both a short factor and a short
certificate of *no* small factor are known to exist and be efficiently
checkable), a class widely believed to be strictly easier than the
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems, since an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem
in $\mathsf{coNP}$ would imply $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$, itself
believed false. So even a hypothetical proof that factoring has no
classical polynomial algorithm would say nothing directly about
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems like 3-SAT, and would not resolve
question (1) above.

**"Factoring has no classical polynomial-time algorithm" is itself
unproven.** It is the load-bearing conjecture behind RSA's security:
widely believed, extensively tested against decades of attempted
classical attacks, and not remotely proven. No unconditional lower
bound rules out a fast classical factoring algorithm; nobody has found
one, which is different from nobody being able to find one in
principle.

So Shor's algorithm's precise, honest status is: **strong conditional
evidence**, not a theorem, that quantum computers are more powerful
than classical ones. *Conditional* on the widely-believed-but-unproven
assumption that factoring is classically hard, Shor's algorithm would
witness $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ for the specific case of
factoring. Without that assumption, all that's unconditionally proven
is "factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$, and nobody has found a classical
polynomial algorithm for it." That is real and important, but a
different and weaker claim than a separation theorem. Distinguishing "a
proven theorem" from "strong conditional evidence built on a
widely-believed but unproven assumption" is the habit this platform's
later Research Methods course trains as a general skill; this is its
first, sharpest worked instance.

## What Shor's Algorithm Does and Does Not Settle

<InsightBlock>
  "Quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones" is not one
  claim but several, and which of P⊆BQP, NP⊆BQP, BQP⊆NP, and P=BQP a
  given result bears on is what separates a checkable claim from an
  impressive-sounding one.
</InsightBlock>

Complexity theory can look like classification for its own sake
(sorting problems into labeled boxes), but its actual job is supplying
the only language precise enough to say what "quantum computers are
more powerful than classical ones" does and doesn't mean, and to name
which open questions are the deep ones. Without that language,
"quantum advantage" claims are unfalsifiable by construction: any
speedup on any problem sounds equally impressive, whether it resolves a
decades-old open question or exploits an oracle model that hides the
one thing that made the problem hard in the first place.

With this lesson's language, a claim like "a quantum computer solved an
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem exponentially faster than any classical
algorithm" becomes checkable rather than merely impressive-sounding.
If that claim is about a genuinely *worst-case*, *general* instance of
an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem, with a real correctness proof and a
real classical lower bound to compare against, it would be direct
evidence toward $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ (a famous, open,
widely disbelieved question) and a landmark result by any measure.
That combination, huge implication plus decades of failed attempts by
everyone else, is the situation that calls for maximal scrutiny
of the fine print: is the instance class really general, or secretly
promise-restricted? Is the comparison against every classical algorithm,
or just the ones tried so far? Is there an actual proof, or a numerical
experiment on small instances? This lesson's job was to make it
possible to ask those questions precisely instead of just intuiting
that something sounds too good to be true.

## Taking Apart a Popular Claim

**Claim under evaluation:** "Shor's algorithm proves quantum computers
are more powerful than classical computers."

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep annotation="Shor's algorithm gives a polynomial-size quantum circuit family that decides integer factoring with bounded error. This half is a proven theorem, not a conjecture.">
What does Shor's algorithm actually establish? Factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$, unconditionally.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="A separation P ≠ BQP would additionally require factoring ∉ P, which is the unproven RSA security conjecture, not a theorem.">
Does this alone imply $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$? Only if factoring $\notin\mathsf{P}$, which is not proven.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Factoring sits in NP ∩ coNP, not known to be NP-complete, so any result about it, even an unconditional one, does not transfer to NP-complete problems in general.">
Does it resolve $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ or $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$? Neither.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Separate the conditional claim (real, and strong) from the unconditional one (real, but weaker) instead of collapsing them into one.">
The precise conclusion: *conditional* on factoring's classical hardness, Shor's algorithm separates $\mathsf{P}$ from $\mathsf{BQP}$; *unconditionally*, only factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ plus decades of failed classical attempts is proven.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Longer, and correct: the habit of precision this course's Research Methods material trains as a general skill.">
Restated claim: Shor's algorithm gives strong conditional evidence, resting on an assumption nobody has proven but real cryptographic infrastructure depends on, that quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones for at least this one specific, practically important problem.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Containments Read Backwards

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating Shor's algorithm as a proof that $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$.**
Shor's algorithm proves quantum computers can factor efficiently; it does
NOT prove $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ or resolve any of the classical
open complexity questions above. It shows factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$
with no known classical polynomial algorithm: strong conditional
evidence resting on the unproven assumption that factoring is
classically hard, not an unconditional separation theorem.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Reading BBBV's optimality of Grover's algorithm as a proof that
$\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$.** BBBV rules out a generic,
structure-blind quantum speedup better than quadratic for *unstructured
black-box search specifically*. An $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem given
explicitly (a real 3-SAT formula, say) is not a black box; a smart
algorithm can inspect its structure directly. BBBV is strong evidence
that $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ is unlikely. It is not a proof
that the containment fails in the unrestricted, non-black-box setting.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A colleague claims: "Since $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ and
   $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ are both proven, and factoring is a
   real problem solved by a quantum computer, it follows that
   $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$." Identify every logical gap in
   this argument.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   There are three, and they fail at different points.

   **The two containments point the wrong way.** $\mathsf{P}\subseteq
   \mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ say that both
   classes contain a common core. That places no constraint at all on how
   $\mathsf{NP}$ and $\mathsf{BQP}$ relate to each other. $\{1,2\}$ and
   $\{1,3\}$ both contain $\{1\}$, and neither contains the other. To get
   $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ you would need every problem in
   $\mathsf{NP}$ to sit in $\mathsf{BQP}$, and the two theorems only
   cover the ones that were already in $\mathsf{P}$.

   **One problem is not a class, unless it is complete.** The step from
   "factoring is in $\mathsf{BQP}$" to "all of $\mathsf{NP}$ is in
   $\mathsf{BQP}$" needs factoring to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete, so that
   every other $\mathsf{NP}$ problem reduces to it in polynomial time and
   inherits the quantum algorithm. Factoring is not known to be
   $\mathsf{NP}$-complete, and it is widely believed not to be. The
   decision version sits in $\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ (a prime
   factorization certifies both a yes and a no answer), and an
   $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem in $\mathsf{coNP}$ would force
   $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$, which almost nobody expects. So the one
   piece of evidence the argument leans on is drawn from the corner of
   $\mathsf{NP}$ least likely to be representative of it.

   **The conclusion would not follow even from a much stronger premise.**
   Suppose someone proved outright that factoring has no classical
   polynomial-time algorithm. That would settle $\mathsf{P}\neq
   \mathsf{BQP}$, a genuinely enormous result, and $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq
   \mathsf{BQP}$ would still be exactly as open as it is today. A
   separation between two classes and a containment between two other
   classes are different kinds of statement, and no amount of the first
   produces the second.

   Worth noticing what the argument gets right, because the instinct is
   not silly. Every step in it is the kind of step that *would* work in a
   world where these classes were linearly ordered. Complexity classes are
   not linearly ordered, and expecting them to be is the single most
   common way that arguments of this shape go wrong.

   </details>

2. Explain, precisely, why "no known classical polynomial-time
   algorithm for factoring" and "no classical polynomial-time algorithm
   for factoring exists" are different claims, and which one Shor's
   algorithm's significance actually rests on.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The first is a fact about what people have found. The second is a
   mathematical claim about what can exist, and nobody has any proof of
   it.

   That gap is not a technicality that will close with more effort.
   Complexity theory currently has no superpolynomial lower bound for
   *any* problem in $\mathsf{NP}$ against general classical computation.
   And the specific claim in question is at least as hard as the field's
   most famous open problem, for a reason that takes one line: the
   decision version of factoring is in $\mathsf{NP}$, so proving it is
   not in $\mathsf{P}$ would immediately prove
   $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$. Anyone who could establish "no classical
   polynomial-time factoring algorithm exists" would have settled
   $\mathsf{P}$ versus $\mathsf{NP}$ on the way past.

   Which claim does Shor's significance rest on? Split the question,
   because the honest answer differs by what kind of significance is
   meant.

   **Shor's theorem itself rests on neither.** It is unconditional: there
   is a polynomial-size uniform quantum circuit family that factors, so
   factoring is in $\mathsf{BQP}$. That proof does not mention classical
   algorithms at all and cannot be weakened by anything discovered about
   them.

   **Its status as evidence for a quantum speedup rests entirely on the
   second, unproven claim.** "Quantum computers can do something
   classical ones cannot" is the statement $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$,
   and factoring witnesses that separation only if factoring is genuinely
   outside $\mathsf{P}$. Absent that, Shor's algorithm is an interesting
   quantum algorithm for a problem that might turn out to be classically
   easy, which would be a disappointment rather than a contradiction.

   **Its cryptographic significance rests on the first, plus deployment.**
   RSA is in use because decades of attack have not produced a fast
   classical factoring algorithm, which is an empirical assumption people
   have chosen to stake infrastructure on. Shor's algorithm threatens
   that assumption from a new direction without needing the second claim
   to be true.

   The history is the reason to be careful rather than pedantic here.
   Primality testing was for a long time in the "no known efficient
   deterministic algorithm" category, and in 2002 the AKS algorithm put
   it in $\mathsf{P}$. Nothing broke, because nothing had been built on
   its hardness. Factoring is a different story precisely because a great
   deal has.

   </details>

3. Suppose a future result proved $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$.
   Explain in your own words what this would and would not imply about
   $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, and about $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It would settle neither, but it would not leave them untouched either.
   It is strong evidence *against* $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$,
   and it is completely silent on $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$.

   **What it would say directly.** Every language a quantum computer can
   decide would have a short classical certificate for its yes-instances.
   Any quantum advantage could be checked after the fact by a classical
   verifier, which is a remarkable practical statement and the exact
   opposite of the situation that motivates $\mathsf{QMA}$ in the next
   lesson.

   **Why it bears on $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, via one extra
   fact.** $\mathsf{BQP}$ is closed under complement: flip the output
   qubit and the completeness and soundness bounds swap symmetrically, so
   $\mathsf{BQP}=\mathsf{coBQP}$. Taking complements of both sides of
   $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ then gives
   $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP}$ as well, so in fact

   $$
   \mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}.
   $$

   Now suppose $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ held too. Chaining the
   two gives $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$, hence
   $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{coNP}$, hence
   $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$: a collapse almost nobody expects, since
   it would mean unsatisfiability has short certificates. So the two
   containments cannot comfortably coexist, and proving one would be a
   serious argument against the other. That is a real deduction rather
   than an intuition, and it is the sharpest thing this question has to
   offer.

   **Why it says nothing about $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$.** There is
   nothing contradictory about $\mathsf{P}\subsetneq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq
   \mathsf{NP}$. Factoring is the ready-made illustration: its decision
   version already sits in $\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$, so it is
   perfectly consistent for factoring to be in $\mathsf{BQP}$, outside
   $\mathsf{P}$, and inside $\mathsf{NP}$ all at once. A world where
   quantum computers are strictly more powerful than classical ones and
   every quantum answer is classically checkable is a coherent world, and
   arguably the one most people would guess we live in.

   **One thing the proof itself would have to be like.** There is an
   oracle relative to which $\mathsf{BQP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$, and
   $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{PH}$, so relative to that oracle
   $\mathsf{BQP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$. Any proof of
   $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ therefore has to be
   non-relativizing: it cannot go through by simulating one machine with
   another in a way that survives being handed an arbitrary black box.
   That is not a reason to doubt the statement. It is a constraint on
   what a proof of it could look like, and the same constraint that makes
   all three of this lesson's open questions resistant to the techniques
   that settled the easy containments.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, and $\mathsf{BQP}$ have exactly three
  proven containments: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$,
  $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$, and $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq
  \mathsf{PSPACE}$ (a space, not time, containment).
- Whether $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, whether
  $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$, and whether $\mathsf{P}=
  \mathsf{BQP}$ are all genuinely open. The first is widely believed
  false, on real but black-box-limited BBBV-style evidence; the second
  has no strong heuristic pointing either way; the third is widely
  believed false largely on the strength of Shor's algorithm.
- Shor's algorithm proves factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ (an
  unconditional theorem), but does not prove $\mathsf{P}\neq
  \mathsf{BQP}$, because factoring is not known to be
  $\mathsf{NP}$-complete and its classical hardness is itself an
  unproven (if widely believed) conjecture.
- This distinction, a proven theorem versus strong conditional
  evidence resting on an unproven assumption, is the tool needed to
  evaluate extraordinary quantum-advantage claims without either
  dismissing or over-crediting them.
