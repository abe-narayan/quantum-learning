## Motivation

BQP and Oracle Complexity did two things carefully: it gave BQP a formal
definition, and it proved that Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon's algorithm, and
Grover's algorithm establish *query*-complexity separations relative to a
black-box oracle, not unconditional *time*-complexity separations between
classical and quantum computation. That lesson deliberately stopped there:
it proved what those specific algorithms do and don't establish, but it
never placed BQP within the broader classical complexity landscape most
popular accounts gesture at with a Venn diagram and move on.

This lesson does that placement precisely. Three classes — $\mathsf{P}$,
$\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{BQP}$ — turn out to have exactly three proven
containments between them and exactly three genuinely open questions,
and conflating a "proven containment" with an "open question believed
one way" is the single most common way popular writing about quantum
computing goes wrong. Getting this exactly right is also the necessary
setup for the rest of this course: QMA (quantum verification), the Local
Hamiltonian problem, and query-complexity lower bounds all live in the
open territory this lesson maps out.

## Mathematical Development

### Recap: the three classes, precisely

<DefinitionBox title="P (deterministic polynomial time)">

$\mathsf{P}$ is the class of decision problems (languages
$L\subseteq\{0,1\}^*$) solvable by a deterministic classical algorithm
running in time polynomial in the input length $n=|x|$: there exists a
polynomial $p$ and an algorithm that, for every input $x$, halts within
$p(|x|)$ steps and outputs $1$ if $x\in L$, $0$ otherwise — always
correctly, with zero error.

</DefinitionBox>

<DefinitionBox title="NP (nondeterministic polynomial time, verifier form)">

$\mathsf{NP}$ is the class of decision problems $L$ for which every
YES instance $x\in L$ has a **certificate** $w$ of length polynomial in
$|x|$, verifiable in deterministic polynomial time: there exists a
polynomial-time algorithm $V$ and a polynomial $q$ such that $x\in L$
if and only if there exists $w$ with $|w|\leq q(|x|)$ and $V(x,w)=1$.
Verification is cheap and deterministic; *finding* $w$ is exactly what's
believed to be hard in general.

</DefinitionBox>

<DefinitionBox title="BQP (bounded-error quantum polynomial time)">

$\mathsf{BQP}$ is the class of decision problems solvable by a
polynomial-time-uniform family of quantum circuits $\{C_n\}$, built from
a fixed universal gate set, such that for input $x$ of length $n$,
measuring a designated output qubit of $C_n|x\rangle|0\cdots0\rangle$
outputs $1$ with probability $\geq2/3$ if $x\in L$ and $\leq1/3$ if
$x\notin L$. (Full formal treatment, including exactly what
"polynomial-time-uniform" rules out, is in the prerequisite lesson —
this recap states the definition without re-deriving it.)

</DefinitionBox>

As the prerequisite lesson proved in detail, the specific constants
$2/3,1/3$ don't matter: running $k$ independent copies of $C_n$ and
taking the majority vote drives the failure probability down to
$2^{-\Omega(k)}$ by a Chernoff-Hoeffding bound on sums of independent
bounded random variables, so *any* pair of constants bounded away from
$1/2$ defines the identical class $\mathsf{BQP}$, up to polynomial
overhead in circuit size. That amplification fact is exactly why "the
error bound is $1/3$" is a convention, not a substantive commitment,
the same reasoning applies verbatim to $\mathsf{BPP}$, the classical
bounded-error analogue.

### The three proven containments

| Containment | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ | **Proven** | Any classical poly-time algorithm can be simulated by a reversible (hence unitary) circuit with only polynomial overhead, then run on a quantum computer that simply never uses superposition — a classical circuit is a special case of a quantum one. |
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$ | **Proven** | A poly-time decision algorithm for $L$ needs no certificate at all — take $V(x,w)=$ "run the $\mathsf{P}$ algorithm on $x$ and ignore $w$" with the trivial empty certificate. |
| $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | **Proven** | A classical algorithm can compute any output amplitude of a poly-size quantum circuit to sufficient precision by summing over computational-basis paths, reusing the same polynomial workspace for each term — polynomial *space*, even though this simulation generally takes *exponential time*. |

The $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ containment is worth
dwelling on, because it's easy to state carelessly. It does **not** say
quantum computers are only as powerful as classical ones — it says a
classical computer with enough *memory* (polynomial) can eventually
compute what a quantum computer computes, given enough *time*
(potentially exponential). Space and time are different resources, and
this containment is a statement about space only. It's also not the
tightest containment known ($\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ is a
strictly stronger, separately proven fact, cited here, not derived),
but $\mathsf{PSPACE}$ is the containment this lesson's diagram, and the
open questions below, actually need.

### The genuinely open questions

Three questions about how $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, and
$\mathsf{BQP}$ relate are **not** settled by anything above, or by
anything currently known in complexity theory. Precision matters more
here than anywhere else in this course.

**(1) Is $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?** This asks whether a
quantum computer can efficiently solve *every* problem in $\mathsf{NP}$:
whether some quantum analogue of brute-force certificate search could
be made polynomial in general, the way Grover's algorithm makes
unstructured search quadratically faster but not exponentially faster.
This is **not known**, and it is **widely believed to be false**. The
evidence is real but limited: the prerequisite lesson's BBBV theorem
proves that Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is exactly
optimal for *unstructured black-box search* — no black-box quantum
algorithm can turn an $N$-item search into anything better than a
quadratic speedup, for every possible oracle. A later lesson in this
course (Query Complexity and Lower Bounds) develops this technique
further. But this is suggestive, not conclusive: an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete
problem like 3-SAT is not a black box — its clauses are explicit and
structured — so a black-box lower bound says nothing decisive about
whether some structure-exploiting quantum algorithm could still solve
it in polynomial time. $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ is disbelieved
on the strength of this evidence and the field's decades of failure to
find such an algorithm, not because anyone has proven it false.

**(2) Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$?** This asks whether every
quantum algorithm's YES-answer can be checked by an ordinary,
classical, polynomial-length certificate — whether quantum computation's
power can always be *verified* the $\mathsf{NP}$ way after the fact,
even if it can't be *found* that way. This is also **not known**. Unlike
question (1), there isn't even a strong heuristic argument pointing one
way: specific problems believed to be in $\mathsf{BQP}$ are known for
which no $\mathsf{NP}$-style classical certificate is currently known:
the natural "certificate," the full quantum computational transcript,
isn't classically polynomial-length or obviously checkable in
polynomial time. But the absence of a *known* certificate is not a proof
that none exists. This question is the direct motivation for this
course's next lesson, which builds $\mathsf{QMA}$ — the class where the
certificate itself is allowed to be quantum.

**(3) Is $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$?** This asks the most basic question
of all: do quantum computers give *any* advantage over classical ones
for deciding languages, given unlimited polynomial time on both sides?
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ is **not a proven theorem**. It is
essentially universally believed, on the strength of Shor's algorithm
(examined precisely below), but belief, however strong and however
widely shared among experts, is not proof.

$\mathsf{P}$'s circle sits solidly inside the overlap of the two dashed
ellipses, because $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ and

fact — the real answer could be that one class contains the other, that
they're equal, or that they overlap only partially the way it's drawn.
That is precisely the content of open questions (1) and (2) above.

### Shor's algorithm's actual complexity-theoretic status

  question="Shor's algorithm gives a polynomial-size quantum circuit that factors integers, with no known classical polynomial-time algorithm for the same task. Before reading the precise analysis below, predict: does this prove P ≠ BQP?"

  explanation="Shor's algorithm unconditionally proves factoring is in BQP — that part is a real theorem. <TheoremBox title="What Shor's algorithm proves" provenance="cited">

Integer factoring (as a decision problem — e.g., "does $N$ have a
nontrivial factor less than $k$?") is in $\mathsf{BQP}$: there is a
polynomial-size quantum circuit family that solves it with bounded
error, via period-finding and the quantum Fourier transform (built and
run on the factor-15 example in Quantum Algorithms II). No known
classical algorithm solves it in polynomial time.

</TheoremBox>

This is a genuine, unconditional theorem: it is a proven fact that
factoring is in $\mathsf{BQP}$. What it is *not* is a proof that
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$, and the reason requires two separate,
precise observations, both commonly elided in popular accounts:

**Factoring is not known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete.** It sits in
$\mathsf{NP}\cap\mathsf{coNP}$ (both a short factor and a short
certificate of *no* small factor are known to exist and be efficiently
checkable), a class widely believed to be strictly easier than the
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems, since an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem
in $\mathsf{coNP}$ would imply $\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{coNP}$, itself
believed false. So even a hypothetical proof that factoring has no
classical polynomial algorithm would say nothing directly about
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems like 3-SAT, and would not resolve
question (1) above.

**"Factoring has no classical polynomial-time algorithm" is itself
unproven.** It is the load-bearing conjecture behind RSA's security —
widely believed, extensively tested against decades of attempted
classical attacks, and not remotely proven. No unconditional lower
bound rules out a fast classical factoring algorithm; nobody has found
one, which is different from nobody being able to find one in
principle.

So Shor's algorithm's precise, honest status is: **strong conditional
evidence**, not a theorem, that quantum computers are more powerful
than classical ones. *Conditional* on the widely-believed-but-unproven
assumption that factoring is classically hard, Shor's algorithm would
witness $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ for the specific case of
factoring. Without that assumption, all that's unconditionally proven
is "factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$, and nobody has found a classical
polynomial algorithm for it" — real and important, but a different and
weaker claim than a separation theorem. Distinguishing "a proven
theorem" from "strong conditional evidence built on a widely-believed
but unproven assumption" is exactly the habit this platform's later
Research Methods course trains as a general skill; this is its first,
sharpest worked instance.

## Physical Interpretation: why this precision matters

<InsightBlock>
  "Quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones" is not one
  claim but several, and precisely which of P⊆BQP, NP⊆BQP, BQP⊆NP, and
  P=BQP a given result actually bears on is exactly what separates a
  checkable claim from an impressive-sounding one.
</InsightBlock>

Complexity theory can look like classification for its own sake —
sorting problems into labeled boxes — but its actual job is supplying
the only language precise enough to say what "quantum computers are
more powerful than classical ones" does and doesn't mean, and to name
exactly which open questions are the deep ones. Without that language,
"quantum advantage" claims are unfalsifiable by construction: any
speedup on any problem sounds equally impressive, whether it resolves a
decades-old open question or exploits an oracle model that hides the
one thing that made the problem hard in the first place.

With this lesson's language, a claim like "a quantum computer solved an
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem exponentially faster than any classical
algorithm" becomes checkable rather than merely impressive-sounding.
If that claim is about a genuinely *worst-case*, *general* instance of
an $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem, with a real correctness proof and a
real classical lower bound to compare against, it would be direct
evidence toward $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ — a famous, open,
widely-disbelieved question — and a landmark result by any measure.
That combination (huge implication, decades of failed attempts by
everyone else) is exactly the situation that calls for maximal scrutiny
of the fine print: is the instance class really general, or secretly
promise-restricted? Is the comparison against every classical algorithm,
or just the ones tried so far? Is there an actual proof, or a numerical
experiment on small instances? This lesson's job was to make it
possible to ask those questions precisely instead of just intuiting
that something sounds too good to be true.

## Worked Reasoning Example

**Claim under evaluation:** "Shor's algorithm proves quantum computers
are fundamentally more powerful than classical computers."

<DerivationSteps>
<DerivationStep annotation="Shor's algorithm gives a polynomial-size quantum circuit family that decides integer factoring with bounded error. This half is a proven theorem, not a conjecture.">
What does Shor's algorithm actually establish? Factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ — unconditionally.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="A separation P ≠ BQP would additionally require factoring ∉ P — and that is exactly the unproven RSA security conjecture, not a theorem.">
Does this alone imply $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$? Only if factoring $\notin\mathsf{P}$, which is not proven.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Factoring sits in NP ∩ coNP, not known to be NP-complete, so any result about it — even an unconditional one — does not transfer to NP-complete problems in general.">
Does it resolve $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ or $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$? Neither.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Separate the conditional claim (real, and strong) from the unconditional one (real, but weaker) instead of collapsing them into one.">
The precise conclusion: *conditional* on factoring's classical hardness, Shor's algorithm separates $\mathsf{P}$ from $\mathsf{BQP}$; *unconditionally*, only factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ plus decades of failed classical attempts is proven.
</DerivationStep>
<DerivationStep annotation="Longer, and correct — exactly the habit of precision this course's Research Methods material trains as a general skill.">
Restated claim: Shor's algorithm gives strong conditional evidence — resting on an assumption nobody has proven but real cryptographic infrastructure depends on — that quantum computers are more powerful than classical ones for at least this one specific, practically important problem.
</DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating Shor's algorithm as a proof that $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$.**
Shor's algorithm proves quantum computers can factor efficiently; it does
NOT prove $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ or resolve any of the classical
open complexity questions above. It shows factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$
with no known classical polynomial algorithm — strong conditional
evidence, resting on the unproven assumption that factoring is
classically hard, not an unconditional separation theorem.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Reading BBBV's optimality of Grover's algorithm as a proof that
$\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$.** BBBV rules out a generic,
structure-blind quantum speedup better than quadratic for *unstructured
black-box search specifically*. An $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem given
explicitly (a real 3-SAT formula, say) is not a black box — a smart
algorithm can inspect its structure directly. BBBV is real, strong
evidence that $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ is unlikely, not a
proof that it's false in the unrestricted, non-black-box setting.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\mathsf{P}$, $\mathsf{NP}$, and $\mathsf{BQP}$ have exactly three
  proven containments: $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$,
  $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$, and $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq
  \mathsf{PSPACE}$ (a space, not time, containment).
- Whether $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, whether
  $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$, and whether $\mathsf{P}=
  \mathsf{BQP}$ are all genuinely open. The first is widely believed
  false, on real but black-box-limited BBBV-style evidence; the second
  has no strong heuristic pointing either way; the third is widely
  believed false largely on the strength of Shor's algorithm.
- Shor's algorithm proves factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ (a real,
  unconditional theorem), but does not prove $\mathsf{P}\neq
  \mathsf{BQP}$, because factoring is not known to be
  $\mathsf{NP}$-complete and its classical hardness is itself an
  unproven (if widely believed) conjecture.
- This distinction, a proven theorem versus strong conditional
  evidence resting on an unproven assumption, is precisely the tool
  needed to evaluate extraordinary quantum-advantage claims without
  either dismissing or over-crediting them.
