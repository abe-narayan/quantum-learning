## Four Lessons, Four Kinds of Result

Four lessons, four different kinds of result. Complexity Classes: P, NP,
and BQP placed $\mathsf{BQP}$ inside the classical landscape and proved
exactly three containments while leaving three questions open. QMA and
Quantum Verification built the quantum analogue of $\mathsf{NP}$-verification
and proved $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$. The
Local Hamiltonian Problem showed, via Kitaev's theorem, that the natural
QMA-complete problem is a real physics question: a Hamiltonian's
ground-state energy. Query Complexity and Lower Bounds proved, by two independent
methods, that Grover's algorithm's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity cannot
be beaten in the black-box model.

Every one of those lessons was careful, at the moment it stated a result,
to say whether that result was a theorem, a conjecture, or an open
question, and never to blur the three. This capstone's only new content is
to pull all four lessons' calibration into a single map, applied by name to
every specific result this course built, and then to use that map for the
thing it's actually for: correctly classifying a new claim, including the
most publicly famous kind of quantum-computing claim there is, a
"quantum supremacy" experiment. That classification skill is also the
direct bridge to this track's next and final course, **Research Methods
and Synthesis**, which turns this habit into a repeatable practice
for reading real papers.

## Three Tiers of Epistemic Status

### The three-tier framework

Three tiers, three different epistemic statuses, and conflating any two of
them is the single most common way popular writing about quantum computing
goes wrong:

- **Proven theorems.** Established by a complete mathematical proof. No
  further evidence could ever strengthen a proven theorem, and no future
  experiment could ever falsify one (short of finding an error in the
  proof itself).
- **Strongly-evidenced conjectures.** Believed by essentially the entire
  field, on the strength of specific, namable evidence, but not proven.
  A conjecture can be believed with very high confidence and still be
  false; that is exactly what makes it a conjecture and not a theorem.
- **Genuinely open questions.** Not even a strong consensus belief exists
  either way, or the question is unresolved with no decisive evidence
  pointing one direction.

#### Tier 1: Proven theorems

<TheoremBox title="This course's proven containments and completeness results" provenance="cited">

$\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and
$\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ are proven
containments (Lessons 1–2). The Local Hamiltonian problem is
$\mathsf{QMA}$-complete, by Kitaev's theorem (Lesson 3). Grover's algorithm's
$\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is provably optimal in the black-box
model, established independently by both the adversary method and the
polynomial method (Lesson 4).

</TheoremBox>

| Result | Proved in | Why it's Tier 1 |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | Lesson 1 | A classical circuit is a special case of a quantum one (first containment); a quantum circuit's amplitudes are computable in polynomial space via a reusable path-sum (second) |
| $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | Lesson 2 | A classical certificate is a valid basis-state quantum witness (first containment); the same space-efficient path-sum idea, plus a space-efficient maximization over witnesses (second) |
| Local Hamiltonian is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete | Lesson 3 | Kitaev's theorem. Containment in $\mathsf{QMA}$ was derived in full (the ground state is its own witness); $\mathsf{QMA}$-hardness rests on Kitaev's history-state construction, whose architecture Lesson 3 laid out and verified on a small case while citing the spectral analysis that completes the proof |
| Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ is optimal (black-box) | Lesson 4 | Proved twice, by independent techniques (the adversary method and the polynomial method), that no black-box quantum algorithm beats quadratic speedup for unstructured search |

These four results share the property that makes them Tier 1: nothing
about future algorithmic cleverness, faster hardware, or new experimental
technique could ever overturn them. They are true the way the Pythagorean
theorem is true.

This is the single picture every result in Lessons 1–3 assembles into: two
solid, proven chains up to $\mathsf{PSPACE}$ ($\mathsf{P}\subseteq
\mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}$),

$\mathsf{NP}$ compare, because that one remains open.

#### Tier 2: Strongly-evidenced conjectures

<DefinitionBox title="What makes a conjecture 'strongly evidenced' rather than merely 'popular'">

A conjecture belongs in this tier when the field can point to specific,
namable evidence for it (a proven result in a restricted model, decades of
failed attempts at a counterexample, or a cryptographic assumption that
has stood unbroken) while being explicit that none of that evidence
amounts to a proof in the unrestricted setting the conjecture is actually
about.

Two different lines are being drawn here, and it is worth keeping them
apart. The line between Tier 1 and everything below it is drawn by proof
and nothing else; no quantity of evidence or belief carries a statement
across it. The line between Tier 2 and Tier 3 is drawn *inside* the
unproven territory, and there evidence and the field's expectations are
exactly what is being assessed. So "nobody has a consensus belief either
way" is a reason to file an open question under Tier 3 rather than Tier
2, and never a reason to move anything into or out of Tier 1.

</DefinitionBox>

| Conjecture | Why it's believed (the real evidence) | What the evidence does *not* do |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ | The oldest, most famous open problem in all of computer science; decades of the entire field failing to find a polynomial algorithm for any $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem | Quantum computing does not resolve this at all; it is a purely classical question, unaffected by anything this course proved |
| $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ | Shor's algorithm: factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ with no known classical polynomial algorithm (Lesson 1) | Factoring is not known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete, and "factoring has no classical polynomial algorithm" is itself unproven, so this is conditional evidence, not a separation theorem |
| $\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ | BBBV's proven optimality of Grover's search, reinforced by Lesson 4's adversary- and polynomial-method lower bounds | All of this evidence lives in the *black-box* model; an explicitly given $\mathsf{NP}$-complete instance (e.g. a real 3-SAT formula) is not a black box, so the evidence is suggestive, not a proof for the general, structured setting |
| Factoring has no efficient classical algorithm | The actual cryptographic assumption underlying RSA's security: decades of attempted classical attacks, none successful | No unconditional lower bound rules out a fast classical factoring algorithm; "nobody has found one" and "no such algorithm exists" remain different claims |

Notice the structural pattern: $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$'s evidence
*is* Shor's algorithm, and Shor's own significance rests on factoring's
conjectured classical hardness. The fourth row is the load-bearing
assumption underneath the second. Tier 2 conjectures can depend on each
other this way; that is normal, and does not make either one weaker or
stronger than its own evidence independently supports.

<InsightBlock>
  Whether a result reaches Tier 1 is not about how confident the field
  is; Tier 2 conjectures like $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ are believed
  almost universally and are still not theorems. Confidence does decide
  Tier 2 against Tier 3, since both are unproven and evidence is the only
  thing left to sort them by. It never promotes anything to Tier 1.
</InsightBlock>

#### Tier 3: Genuinely open questions

- **Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$?** Unlike $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq
  \mathsf{BQP}$, there isn't even a strong heuristic argument pointing
  either way (Lesson 1): some problems believed to be in $\mathsf{BQP}$
  have no known classical polynomial-length certificate, but the absence
  of a known certificate is not a proof that none exists.
- **Are there $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems with efficient quantum
  algorithms?** This is essentially the question "is $\mathsf{NP}
  \subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?" restricted to the hardest problems in
  $\mathsf{NP}$, and a genuine YES answer here would be tightly bound up
  with $\mathsf{P}$ vs. $\mathsf{NP}$ itself: an efficient quantum
  algorithm for even one $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem would be one of
  the most important results in the field's history, because
  it bears so directly on Tier 2's most disbelieved conjecture.
- **Can query-complexity lower-bound techniques be extended to
  unconditional circuit-complexity separations?** Lesson 4's adversary and
  polynomial methods are exact, rigorous, and entirely about the
  *black-box* (query) model. Nobody currently knows how to adapt either
  technique to prove an unconditional separation for explicitly given
  circuits. The Baker-Gill-Solovay relativization barrier is the obstacle
  in the way, and no proof strategy is known to get
  around it for the questions that matter most (like $\mathsf{P}$ vs.
  $\mathsf{NP}$ or $\mathsf{P}$ vs. $\mathsf{BQP}$).
- **Exactly how large a quantum advantage is achievable for practically
  useful problems?** As opposed to problems specifically engineered to be
  classically hard and quantumly easy (random circuit sampling, discussed
  next), nobody knows the ceiling on quantum speedup for problems people
  actually want solved: chemistry, optimization, materials science.
  Some (like factoring) have an exponential conjectured speedup; many
  others have, at best, a proven quadratic Grover-style speedup or no
  known speedup at all, and which category a given useful problem falls
  into is frequently itself unresolved.

## Sycamore, Read Through the Tiers

<ResearchConnection
  title="The Sycamore 'quantum supremacy' experiment"
  source="Arute et al., Nature 574, 505 (2019) (arXiv:1910.11333)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.11333"
>
  A 53-qubit superconducting processor sampled from a specific random
  circuit's output distribution in a way estimated, at the time, to take
  a then-best classical supercomputer thousands of years, versus about
  200 seconds on the quantum hardware. Pednault et al.
  ([arXiv:1910.09534](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.09534)) argued within
  weeks that a cleverer classical technique, trading secondary storage
  for the raw memory a brute-force state-vector simulation requires,
  could reproduce the same task in days, and Pan, Chen and Zhang
  (Phys. Rev. Lett. 129, 090502 (2022)) later did so with tensor-network
  contraction. Similar narrowing of specific instances has continued
  since.
</ResearchConnection>

Google's 2019 Sycamore experiment, already pictured elsewhere on this
platform running the exact transmon hardware Quantum Hardware describes and
sitting at the classical-simulation wall Simulating Quantum
Systems computes, is the most publicly visible "quantum supremacy" claim in
the field's history. This course's framework exists to state, with
no more and no less confidence than is warranted, what an experiment like
that establishes.

**What random circuit sampling is.** A specific, carefully chosen
computational task: sample from the output distribution of a particular
random quantum circuit. The task is not chosen because it's useful. It
is chosen because it is believed to be *classically hard* while being
*quantumly easy*, which makes it a clean, if narrow, place to look for a
gap between the two models of computation.

**The real theoretical basis for believing it's classically hard.** This
is not hand-waving: there is a citable complexity-theoretic argument,
built on the conjecture that the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse.
Roughly, if a classical computer *could* efficiently sample from the same
distribution a random quantum circuit produces, a chain of implications
(cited here, not re-derived; it is its own substantial result in the
sampling-complexity literature) would force the polynomial hierarchy down
to a finite level, something almost as widely disbelieved as
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ itself. That is a named theoretical
basis for random circuit sampling's classical hardness, and it *itself*
rests on an unproven, if strongly believed, assumption: the same Tier 2
shape as everything else in this lesson's middle column.

<ResearchConnection
  title="The hardness argument, with its assumptions on the surface"
  source="Bouland, Fefferman, Nirkhe & Vazirani, Nature Physics 15, 159 (2019) (arXiv:1803.04402)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.04402"
>
  The version of the argument a real experiment has to lean on, and it
  needs two assumptions rather than the one just described. A hardware
  device samples *approximately*, not exactly, and the collapse argument
  above is an exact-sampling argument; closing that gap requires
  additionally assuming that computing a random circuit's output
  probability is hard on *average*, not merely in the worst case. This
  paper proves a worst-case-to-average-case reduction that narrows the
  distance between the two, which is a real theorem and is not the same
  as removing the assumption. Tiering the claim honestly means naming
  both dependencies, not just the famous one.
</ResearchConnection>

**What the experiment does establish.** Strong empirical evidence
consistent with $\mathsf{BQP}$ (or more precisely, the class of problems a
quantum computer solves efficiently) being separated from what a classical
computer can efficiently simulate, for this one specific, carefully
engineered sampling task, run on real hardware, under the
plausible-but-unproven polynomial-hierarchy assumption above.

**What it does not establish.** It does not prove any Tier 3 question
resolved. It does not prove $\mathsf{BQP}\neq$(efficiently classically
simulable) unconditionally; that would require proving the
polynomial-hierarchy assumption itself, which nobody has done. And it does
not, by itself, demonstrate a quantum advantage on any *practically useful*
computational problem: random circuit sampling was chosen for its
classical hardness, not for its usefulness, and Tier 3's last question
(how large a useful-problem advantage is achievable) is left exactly as
open by this experiment as it was before.

**The honest, ongoing scientific back-and-forth.** This is not a case
where the story stopped in 2019. Simulating Quantum Systems' own account
of this exact experiment already notes that IBM subsequently argued a
cleverer classical technique, trading disk space for the raw memory
brute-force state-vector simulation requires, could approximate the same
sampling task, narrowing the claimed classical-hardness margin. That
pattern has continued industry-wide since: specific classical algorithms
have, at various points, been shown to simulate specific random-circuit-
sampling instances faster than initially estimated, and some early
supremacy claims have had their margins reduced or contested as classical
methods improved. **This is not a sign that the enterprise is flawed.**
A claim resting on "no known classical algorithm is fast enough yet" is,
by its own honest nature, a claim the field should keep trying to break.
Successfully narrowing or contesting a specific instance is how a healthy
field checks its own extraordinary claims, not evidence that
the entire research program was hollow. Evaluating this kind of
claim evenhandedly, neither dismissing it as meaningless PR
nor overclaiming it as proof of unconditional quantum superiority, is
what this track's next course, **Research Methods and Synthesis**,
formalizes into a repeatable practice, in a lesson titled
"Evaluating Quantum Advantage Claims." This capstone's random-circuit-
sampling discussion is the direct motivating case study for why that
lesson exists.

## Auditing a Supremacy Claim

**Classify this claim using the three-tier framework: "Google's Sycamore
experiment proves quantum computers are more powerful than classical
computers."**

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Establish the bare empirical fact before attaching any theoretical interpretation to it.">
    **What was actually demonstrated?** A quantum processor sampled
    from a specific random circuit's output distribution in a way estimated,
    at the time, to take a then-best classical supercomputer thousands of
    years, versus about 200 seconds on the quantum hardware.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A mathematical proof of a complexity-class separation is the only thing that qualifies for Tier 1; an experiment on specific hardware cannot be one.">
    **Is this a Tier 1 proven theorem?** No. Nothing about it is a
    mathematical proof of a complexity-class separation. It is an empirical,
    experimental result about one specific circuit family run on one specific
    piece of hardware.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Locate the actual theoretical claim the experiment bears on, and identify the unproven assumption it rests on.">
    **What theoretical claim does it provide evidence for, and how
    strong is that evidence?** It provides strong empirical evidence consistent
    with a separation between efficient quantum and efficient classical
    computation, *for this specific sampling task*, resting on the
    plausible-but-unproven conjecture that the polynomial hierarchy does not
    collapse. That places the underlying theoretical claim in Tier 2:
    strongly evidenced, not proven.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Check the claim against every genuinely open question this course identified. None of them are touched by a result engineered for classical hardness rather than usefulness.">
    **Does it resolve any Tier 3 question?** No. It says nothing
    about $\mathsf{BQP}$ vs. $\mathsf{NP}$, nothing about whether any
    $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem has an efficient quantum algorithm, and
    nothing about the achievable quantum advantage on a practically useful
    problem. Random circuit sampling was engineered to be classically hard,
    not to be useful.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="An honest classification has to account for how the evidence has moved since the original claim, not just the state at announcement.">
    **Has the specific classical-hardness claim itself been static
    and unchallenged?** No, and this matters for an honest classification.
    IBM's subsequent disk-based classical simulation argument, and later
    improved classical algorithms industry-wide, have contested or narrowed
    the classical-hardness margin for specific instances. That is normal,
    healthy scientific self-correction, not a retraction of the whole result.
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Fold every prior step back into a single restatement that carries no more and no less confidence than the evidence supports.">
    **Restate the original claim precisely.** "Google's Sycamore
    experiment proves quantum computers are more powerful than classical
    computers" should become: "Google's Sycamore experiment provided strong
    empirical evidence, consistent with (but not a proof of) a quantum
    advantage over classical computation, for one specific, carefully chosen,
    practically-useless sampling task, resting on a plausible but unproven
    theoretical assumption. Some of the specific classical-hardness
    margins claimed have since been legitimately narrowed or contested by
    improved classical methods, which is exactly how this kind of claim is
    supposed to be checked." Longer, and correct.
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

## The Frontier: Where the Map Is Moving

The map above is not static. Two results from the past decade show how new
work enters it, and each is easy to mis-tier if you skim the headlines.
Both slot into the same three columns.

### The quantum PCP conjecture: open, with one necessary step now proven

<DefinitionBox title="The quantum PCP conjecture (open)">

There exists a universal constant $\varepsilon > 0$ such that estimating a
local Hamiltonian's ground-state energy to within $\varepsilon m$, where
$m$ is the number of local terms, is $\mathsf{QMA}$-hard. Lesson 3's
completeness result holds for a promise gap that shrinks like
$1/\mathrm{poly}(n)$; the conjecture asks whether the hardness survives
when the gap is a constant fraction of the total energy scale. It is the
quantum analogue of the classical PCP theorem's jump from exact to
approximate constraint satisfaction, and it is open in both directions:
no proof, and no refutation.

</DefinitionBox>

The conjecture sits in Tier 3, and it comes with a built-in test. If it is
true (and $\mathsf{QMA}\neq\mathsf{NP}$), then there must exist families
of local Hamiltonians whose every low-energy state is hard to prepare: if
some state below energy $\varepsilon m$ could be produced by a
constant-depth circuit, a classical description of that circuit would
serve as an $\mathsf{NP}$ witness for the energy estimate, and the
hardness would collapse. Freedman and Hastings named this necessary
consequence the NLTS ("no low-energy trivial states") conjecture. For
years, nobody could prove even that.

<ResearchConnection
  title="NLTS Hamiltonians from good quantum codes"
  source="Anshu, Breuckmann & Nirkhe, STOC 2023 (arXiv:2206.13228, 2022)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13228"
>
  In 2022, Anshu, Breuckmann, and Nirkhe proved the NLTS conjecture: the
  local Hamiltonians built from constant-rate, linear-distance quantum LDPC
  codes have the property that every state below a constant fraction of
  the total energy requires circuit depth that grows with system size.
</ResearchConnection>

Tier the pair carefully. NLTS is now Tier 1: a proven theorem, and it will
stay proven no matter what happens next. The quantum PCP conjecture stays
Tier 3: proving a necessary consequence removes one way the conjecture
could have died, but it is not a proof, and the field still lacks a
consensus belief either way. A headline reading "major step toward quantum
PCP" is accurate; a headline reading "quantum PCP nearly proven" is not.

### Classical verification: the QMA thread, made practical

Lesson 2's verification story had one apparent limit built in: the
verifier is a quantum circuit. A skeptic with only a classical computer
seemingly cannot check a quantum device's answer without trusting another
quantum device. In 2018, Urmila Mahadev showed that this limit is not
fundamental.

<ResearchConnection
  title="Classical verification of quantum computations"
  source="Mahadev, FOCS 2018 (arXiv:1804.01082)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.01082"
>
  A classical polynomial-time verifier can interactively verify the result
  of any polynomial-time quantum computation. The protocol turns the
  quantum prover into a trusted measurement device, and its soundness
  rests on the assumption that the learning-with-errors (LWE) problem is
  intractable for efficient quantum computers.
</ResearchConnection>

Where does this sit on the map? Split it the way the framework demands.
The protocol and its analysis are Tier 1: proven theorems. The guarantee
it delivers is conditional on LWE's quantum hardness, a cryptographic
assumption of the same shape as "factoring has no efficient classical
algorithm" in the Tier 2 table: unbroken after years of attack, widely
believed, unproven. So the precise claim is: *if* LWE is quantumly hard,
a classical skeptic can verify a quantum computation. That conditional
structure is not a weakness to hide. It is the same honest form every
Tier 2 row in this lesson already takes.

## Overclaiming and Its Mirror Image

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating a "quantum supremacy" experiment as proof of a general
theoretical claim.** Random circuit sampling provides strong empirical
evidence consistent with a Tier 2 conjecture (that efficient classical
simulation and efficient quantum computation are separated), for one
specific, engineered task, under an unproven assumption. It is not a Tier
1 theorem, it does not resolve any Tier 3 question, and it says nothing
about advantage on practically useful problems. The opposite error is
equally imprecise: dismissing the whole result as "meaningless PR"
because it hasn't proven an unconditional separation, or because a later
classical algorithm narrowed its margin. A carefully designed empirical
result consistent with a real theoretical conjecture is neither proof nor
noise.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing "evidenced in a restricted model" with "proven in general."**
$\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$'s evidence (BBBV, the adversary and
polynomial methods) is real and rigorous, entirely within the black-box
query model. An explicitly given $\mathsf{NP}$-complete instance is not a
black box, so this evidence is suggestive of, not a proof of, the
unrestricted claim. The same shape of error appears with random circuit
sampling's polynomial-hierarchy argument: a citable theoretical
basis for believing classical hardness, resting itself on an unproven
assumption, is Tier 2 evidence, not a Tier 1 theorem.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. A conference talk claims: "Our random circuit sampling experiment
   proves BQP is not contained in P." Using this lesson's three-tier
   framework, identify exactly which tier the underlying theoretical claim
   belongs in, and state precisely what would need to be true for the
   experiment's evidence to become a proof instead.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The underlying claim, $\mathsf{BQP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{P}$, is
   **Tier 2**. The talk is asserting Tier 1 status for it, and no
   experiment can supply that.

   Three separate things have gone wrong, and they are worth keeping
   apart because only the third is about tiers at all.

   **An experiment is the wrong kind of object.** A complexity class is a
   statement about a *family* of circuits, quantified over all input
   sizes. A device with a fixed qubit count is a single point. Even a
   flawless run at $n=53$ places no constraint on asymptotic behaviour,
   because asymptotics say nothing about any finite $n$ and any finite
   $n$ says nothing back. This is not a matter of the experiment being
   too small; there is no size at which it would start to count as a
   proof.

   **Random circuit sampling is not in $\mathsf{BQP}$.** $\mathsf{BQP}$
   is a class of *decision* problems. Sampling from a distribution is a
   different kind of task, living in the sampling classes
   $\mathsf{SampBQP}$ and $\mathsf{SampBPP}$. A separation there would be
   a real and interesting result and it would not be the separation the
   talk named. The claim is category-mismatched before its evidence is
   even examined.

   **The theory it rests on is conditional twice over.** The argument
   that random circuit sampling is classically hard assumes the
   polynomial hierarchy does not collapse. And because real hardware
   samples approximately rather than exactly, the argument needs a second
   assumption about average-case hardness of computing output
   probabilities. Both are Tier 2, and a conclusion drawn through two
   Tier 2 assumptions is Tier 2 at best.

   **What would make it a proof.** Nothing about the experiment, at any
   scale, with any fidelity. $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ is a
   mathematical statement and needs a mathematical proof: an
   unconditional lower bound showing some problem in $\mathsf{BQP}$ has
   no polynomial-time classical algorithm. Two things are known about
   what such a proof would have to look like. It cannot relativize, by
   the Baker-Gill-Solovay barrier. And it would settle a famous open
   problem in purely classical complexity on the way past, because
   $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ means that
   $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{PSPACE}$ would force $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{BQP}$,
   so proving $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ proves
   $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ as a corollary. Anyone announcing the
   former has also announced the latter, which is a useful sanity check
   to apply to any such announcement.

   **What the talk could have said and been right.** That the experiment
   is strong empirical evidence, on real hardware, that this particular
   engineered sampling task is beyond current classical methods at this
   scale, consistent with the conjectured separation and conditional on
   the two named assumptions. That is a genuinely significant claim. It
   is a smaller one, and the difference between the two sentences is the
   whole subject of the next course.

   </details>

2. Explain why $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ and "factoring has no
   efficient classical algorithm" are both Tier 2 conjectures that depend
   on each other, and why that dependency doesn't make either one weaker
   than its own evidence supports.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The dependency runs in one direction, and that is what keeps it
   healthy.

   Shor's theorem puts factoring in $\mathsf{BQP}$, unconditionally. So
   if factoring is not in $\mathsf{P}$, then $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$
   follows immediately: one problem in $\mathsf{BQP}$ and outside
   $\mathsf{P}$ is all a separation needs. That single implication is the
   main reason anybody believes $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$, which is why
   the table's second row's evidence is really the fourth row's claim.

   **The reason this is a chain and not a loop** is that the fourth row
   has its own evidence, and none of it is quantum. Factoring's
   conjectured classical hardness rests on centuries of number theory and
   decades of concentrated algorithmic attack: trial division to Pollard
   rho to the quadratic sieve to the general number field sieve, which
   after all that effort is still subexponential rather than polynomial.
   It also rests on the largest adversarial test anyone has ever run,
   which is that the world's public-key infrastructure has been sitting
   on the assumption in the open for decades. Not one line of that
   evidence mentions quantum computation, so
   $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ is inheriting a belief that was formed
   independently of it.

   Circularity would require the evidence for factoring's hardness to be
   "$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$." It is not, and it never was.

   **The real caution the dependency implies,** stated so that "it does
   not weaken either" is not mistaken for "it costs nothing." It means
   $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ has one flagship witness rather than
   several independent ones. Find a classical polynomial-time factoring
   algorithm tomorrow and the conjecture does not become false, but it
   loses its best argument and falls back on thinner support: discrete
   logarithm, other hidden-subgroup problems, and oracle separations that
   the relativization barrier says cannot transfer. So the honest reading
   is that these two beliefs will largely stand or fall together, and
   pretending otherwise would overstate how many independent legs the
   conjecture is standing on.

   **Why the framework handles this correctly.** A Tier 2 claim resting
   on another Tier 2 claim stays Tier 2. Nothing in the tier structure
   lets a stack of conjectures bootstrap itself upward, because the only
   thing that reaches Tier 1 is a proof. That rule looks pedantic right
   up until you meet a chain of four plausible conditionals whose
   conclusion is being reported as a fact, at which point it is the only
   thing standing between the reader and a mistake.

   </details>

3. A later classical algorithm narrows the classical-hardness margin
   originally claimed for a specific random-circuit-sampling experiment.
   Explain why this is a normal, healthy part of how the field checks its
   own claims rather than evidence the original result was wrong or the
   whole research program is unsound.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the claim being narrowed was always a claim about the best
   *known* classical method, and the only way to test a claim of that
   shape is to try to beat it. A margin that nobody attacked would be a
   margin nobody had checked.

   The clean way to see it is to separate the three claims such an
   experiment actually makes, since a later classical algorithm touches
   exactly one of them.

   **The measurement.** The device ran a circuit of a stated size and
   depth and produced samples with a stated fidelity. That is an
   experimental result. A classical algorithm published three years later
   has no bearing on whether the hardware did what the paper says it did.

   **The comparison.** "This would take a classical supercomputer $X$
   years" is a statement about the best classical algorithm anyone had
   written down at the time of publication. It has an expiry date built
   into its own definition. Revising it downward is not falsifying the
   paper; it is updating a quantity the paper could only ever have quoted
   as of a date.

   **The theoretical hardness argument.** That random circuit sampling is
   classically hard rests on the polynomial hierarchy not collapsing, and
   it is an asymptotic statement. A faster simulation at $53$ qubits does
   not touch it, because a constant-factor or memory-hierarchy
   improvement at fixed $n$ is not an asymptotic result. The crossover
   point moves; the conjectured slope does not.

   **What would be genuinely bad news,** because "this is healthy" should
   not become a formula that absorbs every criticism. A classical
   algorithm running in time polynomial in the circuit's size and depth,
   for the general instance family rather than for a specific circuit,
   would attack the hardness conjecture itself and would be a serious
   problem for the entire programme. The narrowings that have actually
   happened are not of that kind: they trade secondary storage against
   memory, or contract the circuit as a tensor network more cleverly, on
   particular instances. Knowing which kind of result you are reading is
   the skill; declaring all of them harmless is not.

   **Where criticism is warranted, and it is not of the later work.** If
   an original claim was phrased as though "$10{,}000$ years" were a
   property of the task rather than of a particular classical program on
   particular hardware in a particular year, then the overclaim is the
   error, and it was an error on the day it was published rather than on
   the day someone improved the simulation. Tiering a claim honestly at
   the moment it is made is what makes later improvements read as
   progress instead of as embarrassment.

   The structural comparison worth carrying: this is exactly how
   cryptography works. RSA is trusted because people have been paid to
   break it for decades and have not. Nobody treats an improved factoring
   record as evidence that public-key cryptography was a hollow research
   programme. Adversarial testing is the mechanism by which a claim of
   the form "nobody can do this efficiently" acquires any credibility at
   all, and a field where such claims went unchallenged would be worth
   far less trust, not more.

   </details>

## Summary

- This course's four lessons sort cleanly into three tiers: **proven**
  ($\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$,
  $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$, Local
  Hamiltonian is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete, Grover's optimality by two
  independent methods), **strongly-evidenced conjectures**
  ($\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$,
  $\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, factoring's classical hardness,
  random circuit sampling's classical hardness), and **genuinely open
  questions** (is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$? any efficiently
  quantum-solvable $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem? unconditional
  circuit-complexity separations from query techniques? the ceiling on
  useful-problem quantum advantage?).
- Random circuit sampling experiments like Sycamore provide strong
  empirical evidence consistent with a real Tier 2 conjecture, for one
  specific, classically-hard-by-design task, resting on the
  polynomial-hierarchy assumption. That is not a Tier 1 proof of anything,
  and not, by itself, a demonstration of advantage on any useful problem.
  Subsequent narrowing or contesting of specific hardness margins by
  improved classical algorithms is healthy scientific self-correction, not
  a flaw in the enterprise.
- The actual payoff of this entire course is a repeatable question: for any
  new claim, is it a proven theorem, a conjecture with stated evidence, or
  a genuinely open question? And if it's experimental, exactly what task
  was it demonstrated on, and under what classical-hardness assumption?
