## Motivation

Four lessons, four different kinds of result. Complexity Classes: P, NP,
and BQP placed $\mathsf{BQP}$ inside the classical landscape and proved
exactly three containments while leaving three questions open. QMA and
Quantum Verification built the quantum analogue of $\mathsf{NP}$-verification
and proved $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$. The
Local Hamiltonian Problem showed that the natural QMA-complete problem is a
real physics question — a Hamiltonian's ground-state energy — via Kitaev's
theorem. Query Complexity and Lower Bounds proved, by two independent
methods, that Grover's algorithm's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity cannot
be beaten in the black-box model.

Every one of those lessons was careful, at the moment it stated a result,
to say whether that result was a theorem, a conjecture, or an open
question — and never to blur the three. This capstone's only new content is
to pull all four lessons' calibration into a single map, applied by name to
every specific result this course built, and then to use that map for the
thing it's actually for: correctly classifying a new claim, including the
most publicly famous kind of quantum-computing claim there is, a
"quantum supremacy" experiment. That classification skill is also the
direct bridge to this pillar's next and final course, **Research Methods
and Synthesis**, which turns exactly this habit into a repeatable practice
for reading real papers.

## Mathematical Development

### The three-tier framework

Three tiers, three different epistemic statuses, and conflating any two of
them is the single most common way popular writing about quantum computing
goes wrong:

- **Proven theorems.** Established by a complete mathematical proof. No
  further evidence could ever strengthen a proven theorem, and no future
  experiment could ever falsify one (short of finding an error in the
  proof itself).
- **Strongly-evidenced conjectures.** Believed by essentially the entire
  field, on the strength of specific, namable evidence — but not proven.
  A conjecture can be believed with very high confidence and still be
  false; that is exactly what makes it a conjecture and not a theorem.
- **Genuinely open questions.** Not even a strong consensus belief exists
  either way, or the question is simply unresolved with no decisive
  evidence pointing one direction.

#### Tier 1 — Proven theorems

<TheoremBox title="This course's proven containments and completeness results" provenance="cited">

$\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ and
$\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ are proven
containments (Lessons 1–2). The Local Hamiltonian problem is
$\mathsf{QMA}$-complete — Kitaev's theorem (Lesson 3). Grover's algorithm's
$\Theta(\sqrt N)$ query complexity is provably optimal in the black-box
model, established independently by both the adversary method and the
polynomial method (Lesson 4).

</TheoremBox>

| Result | Proved in | Why it's Tier 1 |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | Lesson 1 | A classical circuit is a special case of a quantum one (first containment); a quantum circuit's amplitudes are computable in polynomial space via a reusable path-sum (second) |
| $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ | Lesson 2 | A classical certificate is a valid basis-state quantum witness (first containment); the same space-efficient path-sum idea, plus a space-efficient maximization over witnesses (second) |
| Local Hamiltonian is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete | Lesson 3 | Kitaev's theorem: a full reduction from an arbitrary $\mathsf{QMA}$ verifier circuit to a local Hamiltonian's ground-state energy, in both directions |
| Grover's $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ is optimal (black-box) | Lesson 4 | Proved twice, by independent techniques (the adversary method and the polynomial method), that no black-box quantum algorithm beats quadratic speedup for unstructured search |

These four results share the property that makes them Tier 1: nothing
about future algorithmic cleverness, faster hardware, or new experimental
technique could ever overturn them. They are true the way the Pythagorean
theorem is true.

#### Tier 2 — Strongly-evidenced conjectures

<DefinitionBox title="What makes a conjecture 'strongly evidenced' rather than merely 'popular'">

A conjecture belongs in this tier when the field can point to specific,
namable evidence for it — a proven result in a restricted model, decades of
failed attempts at a counterexample, or a real, currently-unbroken
cryptographic assumption — while being explicit that none of that evidence
amounts to a proof in the unrestricted setting the conjecture is actually
about.

</DefinitionBox>

| Conjecture | Why it's believed (the real evidence) | What the evidence does *not* do |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ | The oldest, most famous open problem in all of computer science; decades of the entire field failing to find a polynomial algorithm for any $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem | Quantum computing does not resolve this at all — it is a purely classical question, unaffected by anything this course proved |
| $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$ | Shor's algorithm: factoring $\in\mathsf{BQP}$ with no known classical polynomial algorithm (Lesson 1) | Factoring is not known to be $\mathsf{NP}$-complete, and "factoring has no classical polynomial algorithm" is itself unproven — so this is conditional evidence, not a separation theorem |
| $\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$ | BBBV's proven optimality of Grover's search, reinforced by Lesson 4's adversary- and polynomial-method lower bounds | All of this evidence lives in the *black-box* model; an explicitly-given $\mathsf{NP}$-complete instance (e.g. a real 3-SAT formula) is not a black box, so the evidence is suggestive, not a proof for the general, structured setting |
| Factoring has no efficient classical algorithm | The actual cryptographic assumption underlying RSA's security: decades of attempted classical attacks, none successful | No unconditional lower bound rules out a fast classical factoring algorithm; "nobody has found one" and "no such algorithm exists" remain different claims |

Notice the structural pattern: $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$'s evidence
*is* Shor's algorithm, and Shor's own significance rests on factoring's
conjectured classical hardness — the fourth row is the load-bearing
assumption underneath the second. Tier 2 conjectures can depend on each
other this way; that is normal, and does not make either one weaker or
stronger than its own evidence independently supports.

#### Tier 3 — Genuinely open questions

- **Is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$?** Unlike $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq
  \mathsf{BQP}$, there isn't even a strong heuristic argument pointing
  either way (Lesson 1): some problems believed to be in $\mathsf{BQP}$
  have no known classical polynomial-length certificate, but the absence
  of a known certificate is not a proof that none exists.
- **Are there $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problems with efficient quantum
  algorithms?** This is essentially the question "is $\mathsf{NP}
  \subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$?" restricted to the hardest problems in
  $\mathsf{NP}$, and a genuine YES answer here would be tightly bound up
  with $\mathsf{P}$ vs. $\mathsf{NP}$ itself: an efficient quantum
  algorithm for even one $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem would be one of
  the most important results in the field's history, precisely because
  it bears so directly on Tier 2's most disbelieved conjecture.
- **Can query-complexity lower-bound techniques be extended to
  unconditional circuit-complexity separations?** Lesson 4's adversary and
  polynomial methods are exact, rigorous, and entirely about the
  *black-box* (query) model. Nobody currently knows how to adapt either
  technique to prove an unconditional separation for explicitly-given
  circuits — the Baker-Gill-Solovay relativization barrier is exactly the
  obstacle that stands in the way, and no proof strategy is known to get
  around it for the questions that matter most (like $\mathsf{P}$ vs.
  $\mathsf{NP}$ or $\mathsf{P}$ vs. $\mathsf{BQP}$).
- **Exactly how large a quantum advantage is achievable for practically
  useful problems?** As opposed to problems specifically engineered to be
  classically hard and quantumly easy (random circuit sampling, discussed
  next), nobody knows the ceiling on quantum speedup for problems people
  actually want solved — chemistry, optimization, materials science.
  Some (like factoring) have an exponential conjectured speedup; many
  others have, at best, a proven quadratic Grover-style speedup or no
  known speedup at all, and which category a given useful problem falls
  into is frequently itself unresolved.

## Physical Interpretation: Random Circuit Sampling and What It Actually Shows

Google's 2019 Sycamore experiment, already pictured elsewhere on this
platform running the exact transmon hardware Quantum Hardware describes and
sitting exactly at the classical-simulation wall Simulating Quantum
Systems computes, is the most publicly visible "quantum supremacy" claim in
the field's history. This course's framework exists precisely to state, with
no more and no less confidence than is warranted, what an experiment like
that establishes.

**What random circuit sampling is.** A specific, carefully chosen
computational task: sample from the output distribution of a particular
random quantum circuit. The task is not chosen because it's useful — it
is chosen because it is believed to be *classically hard* while being
*quantumly easy*, which makes it a clean, if narrow, place to look for a
gap between the two models of computation.

**The real theoretical basis for believing it's classically hard.** This
is not hand-waving: there is a citable, real complexity-theoretic argument,
built on the conjecture that the polynomial hierarchy does not collapse.
Roughly, if a classical computer *could* efficiently sample from the same
distribution a random quantum circuit produces, a chain of implications
(cited here, not re-derived — it is its own substantial result in the
sampling-complexity literature) would force the polynomial hierarchy down
to a finite level, something almost as widely disbelieved as
$\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$ itself. That is a genuine, named theoretical
basis for random circuit sampling's classical hardness — and it is
*itself* resting on an unproven, if strongly believed, assumption, exactly
the same Tier 2 shape as everything else in this lesson's middle column.

**What the experiment does establish.** Strong empirical evidence
consistent with $\mathsf{BQP}$ (or more precisely, the class of problems a
quantum computer solves efficiently) being separated from what a classical
computer can efficiently simulate — for this one, specific, carefully
engineered sampling task, run on real hardware, under the
plausible-but-unproven polynomial-hierarchy assumption above.

**What it does not establish.** It does not prove any Tier 3 question
resolved. It does not prove $\mathsf{BQP}\neq$(efficiently classically
simulable) unconditionally — that would require proving the
polynomial-hierarchy assumption itself, which nobody has done. And it does
not, by itself, demonstrate a quantum advantage on any *practically useful*
computational problem: random circuit sampling was chosen for its
classical hardness, not for its usefulness, and Tier 3's last question
(how large a useful-problem advantage is achievable) is left exactly as
open by this experiment as it was before.

**The honest, ongoing scientific back-and-forth.** This is not a case
where the story stopped in 2019. Simulating Quantum Systems' own account
of this exact experiment already notes that IBM subsequently argued a
cleverer classical technique — trading disk space for the raw memory
brute-force state-vector simulation requires — could approximate the same
sampling task, narrowing the claimed classical-hardness margin. That
pattern has continued industry-wide since: specific classical algorithms
have, at various points, been shown to simulate specific random-circuit-
sampling instances faster than initially estimated, and some early
supremacy claims have had their margins reduced or contested as classical
methods improved. **This is not a sign that the enterprise is flawed.**
A claim resting on "no known classical algorithm is fast enough yet" is,
by its own honest nature, a claim the field should keep trying to break —
and successfully narrowing or contesting a specific instance is exactly
how a healthy field checks its own extraordinary claims, not evidence that
the entire research program was hollow. Evaluating exactly this kind of
claim — precisely, evenhandedly, neither dismissing it as meaningless PR
nor overclaiming it as proof of unconditional quantum superiority — is
what this pillar's next course, **Research Methods and Synthesis**,
formalizes into a repeatable practice, in a lesson literally titled
"Evaluating Quantum Advantage Claims." This capstone's random-circuit-
sampling discussion is the direct motivating case study for why that
lesson exists.

## Worked Example

**Classify this claim using the three-tier framework: "Google's Sycamore
experiment proves quantum computers are more powerful than classical
computers."**

**Step 1 — What was actually demonstrated?** A quantum processor sampled
from a specific random circuit's output distribution in a way estimated,
at the time, to take a then-best classical supercomputer thousands of
years, versus about 200 seconds on the quantum hardware.

**Step 2 — Is this a Tier 1 proven theorem?** No. Nothing about it is a
mathematical proof of a complexity-class separation. It is an empirical,
experimental result about one specific circuit family run on one specific
piece of hardware.

**Step 3 — What theoretical claim does it provide evidence for, and how
strong is that evidence?** It provides strong empirical evidence consistent
with a separation between efficient quantum and efficient classical
computation, *for this specific sampling task*, resting on the
plausible-but-unproven conjecture that the polynomial hierarchy does not
collapse. That places the underlying theoretical claim in Tier 2:
strongly evidenced, not proven.

**Step 4 — Does it resolve any Tier 3 question?** No. It says nothing
about $\mathsf{BQP}$ vs. $\mathsf{NP}$, nothing about whether any
$\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem has an efficient quantum algorithm, and
nothing about the achievable quantum advantage on a practically useful
problem — random circuit sampling was engineered to be classically hard,
not to be useful.

**Step 5 — Has the specific classical-hardness claim itself been static
and unchallenged?** No — and this matters for an honest classification.
IBM's subsequent disk-based classical simulation argument, and later
improved classical algorithms industry-wide, have contested or narrowed
the classical-hardness margin for specific instances. That is normal,
healthy scientific self-correction, not a retraction of the whole result.

**Step 6 — Restate the original claim precisely.** "Google's Sycamore
experiment proves quantum computers are more powerful than classical
computers" should become: "Google's Sycamore experiment provided strong
empirical evidence, consistent with (but not a proof of) a quantum
advantage over classical computation, for one specific, carefully chosen,
practically-useless sampling task, resting on a plausible but unproven
theoretical assumption — and some of the specific classical-hardness
margins claimed have since been legitimately narrowed or contested by
improved classical methods, which is exactly how this kind of claim is
supposed to be checked." Longer, and correct.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating a "quantum supremacy" experiment as proof of a general
theoretical claim.** Random circuit sampling provides strong empirical
evidence consistent with a Tier 2 conjecture (that efficient classical
simulation and efficient quantum computation are separated), for one
specific, engineered task, under an unproven assumption. It is not a Tier
1 theorem, it does not resolve any Tier 3 question, and it says nothing
about advantage on practically useful problems. The opposite error —
dismissing the whole result as "meaningless PR" because it hasn't proven
an unconditional separation, or because a later classical algorithm
narrowed its margin — is equally imprecise: a genuine, carefully-designed
empirical result consistent with a real theoretical conjecture is neither
proof nor noise.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Confusing "evidenced in a restricted model" with "proven in general."**
$\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$'s evidence (BBBV, the adversary and
polynomial methods) is real and rigorous — entirely within the black-box
query model. An explicitly-given $\mathsf{NP}$-complete instance is not a
black box, so this evidence is suggestive of, not a proof of, the
unrestricted claim. The same shape of error appears with random circuit
sampling's polynomial-hierarchy argument: a real, citable theoretical
basis for believing classical hardness, resting itself on an unproven
assumption, is Tier 2 evidence, not a Tier 1 theorem.
</Callout>

## Summary

- This course's four lessons sort cleanly into three tiers: **proven**
  ($\mathsf{P}\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$,
  $\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PSPACE}$, Local
  Hamiltonian is $\mathsf{QMA}$-complete, Grover's optimality by two
  independent methods), **strongly-evidenced conjectures**
  ($\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{NP}$, $\mathsf{P}\neq\mathsf{BQP}$,
  $\mathsf{NP}\not\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}$, factoring's classical hardness,
  random circuit sampling's classical hardness), and **genuinely open
  questions** (is $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{NP}$? any efficiently
  quantum-solvable $\mathsf{NP}$-complete problem? unconditional
  circuit-complexity separations from query techniques? the ceiling on
  useful-problem quantum advantage?).
- Random circuit sampling experiments like Sycamore provide genuine, strong
  empirical evidence consistent with a real Tier 2 conjecture, for one
  specific, classically-hard-by-design task, resting on the
  polynomial-hierarchy assumption — not a Tier 1 proof of anything, and
  not, by itself, a demonstration of advantage on any useful problem.
  Subsequent narrowing or contesting of specific hardness margins by
  improved classical algorithms is healthy scientific self-correction, not
  a flaw in the enterprise.
- The actual payoff of this entire course is a repeatable question: for any
  new claim, is it a proven theorem, a conjecture with stated evidence, or
  a genuinely open question — and if it's experimental, exactly what task
  was it demonstrated on and under what classical-hardness assumption?
