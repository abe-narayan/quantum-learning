## The Same Query Scaling, Without the QFT

The previous lesson pinned down exactly what quantum phase estimation
costs: a precision register of $t$ qubits, an $O(t^2)$-gate QFT applied
coherently at the end, and (easy to overlook, but the part that actually
matters for hardware) every one of the $2^t-1$ controlled-Grover-iterate
applications feeding that QFT has to stay coherent *simultaneously*,
because QPE's whole trick is querying a superposition of iteration
counts at once. That is a lot to ask of a noisy, near-term device.

<Question>
  For the specific task of estimating an amplitude, not finding an
  eigenphase in general, is all of that machinery actually
  necessary?
</Question>

The answer is no, and it rests on a nontrivial result that is still in
active use.

## From Monte Carlo to a Grover Schedule

### The estimation problem, and classical Monte Carlo's O(1/ε²)

Suppose a unitary $A$ prepares $A|0\rangle=|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{1-a}\,
|0\rangle|\text{junk}_0\rangle+\sqrt a\,|1\rangle|\text{junk}_1\rangle$,
and the goal is to estimate $a\in[0,1]$, the probability that measuring
the flag qubit returns $1$. Classically (or by simply running $A$ and
measuring, with no quantum trick at all) this is a Bernoulli-sampling
problem: each shot is a coin flip with bias $a$, and the sample-mean
estimator $\hat a$ after $n$ shots has variance $a(1-a)/n$, so its
standard deviation (the estimator's typical error) is
$\sqrt{a(1-a)/n}=O(1/\sqrt n)$. Solving for the sample size needed to
reach a target precision $\epsilon$:

$$
\epsilon \sim \frac1{\sqrt n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = O\!\left(\frac1{\epsilon^2}\right)
$$

the familiar classical Monte Carlo scaling: halving the error requires
*quadrupling* the number of samples. This is the baseline every
amplitude-estimation algorithm below is measured against.

### The original algorithm: Grover iterate + QPE (BHMT 2000)

  caption="Gilles Brassard, co-author (with Høyer, Mosca, and Tapp) of the 2000 amplitude estimation algorithm this section derives: the original Grover-iterate-plus-QPE construction that the rest of this lesson shows how to run without the QPE half."

Brassard, Høyer, Mosca, and Tapp's amplitude estimation (2000) reuses
this platform's own Grover derivation directly, with the search-specific
pieces generalized. In place of $H^{\otimes n}$ preparing the uniform
superposition $|s\rangle$, use the general state-preparation unitary $A$
preparing $|\psi\rangle$; in place of the marked-item oracle, use
$S_\chi$, the reflection that flips the sign of every basis state with
flag qubit $=1$; in place of the search-specific diffusion operator
$D=2|s\rangle\langle s|-I$, use $D=-AS_0A^{-1}$ ($S_0=2|0\rangle\langle
0|-I$), which reflects about $|\psi\rangle=A|0\rangle$ exactly as $D$
reflected about $|s\rangle=H^{\otimes n}|0\rangle$ before. The **Grover
iterate** is $Q=DS_\chi$, the same "compose two reflections in
a 2D subspace" object the earlier lesson analyzed, with
$|\alpha\rangle,|\beta\rangle$ relabeled as the orthogonal
unmarked/marked components of $|\psi\rangle$ and $\theta$ now defined by
$\sin^2\theta=a$ instead of $\sin^2\theta=1/N$. Every step of that
derivation carries over unchanged, so after $k$ applications of $Q$ the
success probability is exactly the closed form below, reusing the
earlier lesson's result with zero new derivation required.

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "P", symbol: "P(flag=1|k)", gloss: "The probability of measuring the flag qubit as 1 after k Grover-iterate applications. This is the quantity the algorithm is built to estimate precisely." },
    { id: "k", symbol: "k", gloss: "The number of times the Grover iterate Q has been applied so far." },
    { id: "theta", symbol: "θ", gloss: "The rotation angle defined by sin²θ = a, where a is the amplitude being estimated." },
    { id: "a", symbol: "a", gloss: "The target amplitude: the probability that measuring the flag qubit of A|0⟩ returns 1." },
  ]}
>
$$
P(\text{flag}=1\mid k) = \sin^2\big((2k+1)\theta\big), \qquad \sin^2\theta=a
$$
</EquationReveal>

The key extra fact BHMT add: restricted to this 2D subspace,
$Q$ is a rotation matrix by angle $2\theta$, whose eigenvalues are
$e^{\pm2i\theta}$. Running **quantum phase estimation on $Q$** (a
superposition of $Q^0,Q^1,Q^2,\dots,Q^{2^t-1}$, controlled by a $t$-qubit
precision register, exactly as the QPE-precision lesson's register
setup) reads out an estimate of $2\theta$ to $t$ bits, and hence of $a$.
BHMT's Theorem 12 gives the precise bound: with $M=2^t$ Grover-iterate
applications total,

$$
\big|\tilde a - a\big| \;\leq\; \frac{2\pi\sqrt{a(1-a)}}M + \frac{\pi^2}{M^2}, \qquad \text{with probability} \geq \frac8{\pi^2}\approx0.81
$$

so $\epsilon=O(1/M)$, i.e. $M=O(1/\epsilon)$ Grover-iterate applications
suffice: the **quadratic speedup for estimation**,
Heisenberg-limited $O(1/\epsilon)$ in place of classical Monte Carlo's
$O(1/\epsilon^2)$. The cost: those $M=2^t$ applications of $Q$ are
queried *in superposition*, so the entire circuit (every controlled-$Q$
application plus the final inverse QFT on $t=O(\log(1/\epsilon))$ qubits)
must run coherently before a single measurement is taken.

  The paper that generalized Grover's algorithm from search to

### QPE-free amplitude estimation: the same iterate, a classical schedule

The important modern development keeps the exact
same building block (the Grover iterate $Q$ above) and the exact same
closed-form probability $\sin^2((2m+1)\theta)$, but discards both the
controlled-$Q$ superposition and the QFT entirely.

<ResearchConnection
  title="Amplitude Estimation without Phase Estimation"
  source="Suzuki, Uno, Raymond, Tanaka, Onodera & Yamamoto, Quantum Information Processing 19, 75 (2020)"
  url="https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.10246"
>
  See also Grinko, Gacon, Zoufal & Woerner, "Iterative Quantum Amplitude
  Estimation," *npj Quantum Information* **7**, 52 (2021). Both results
  show that the Heisenberg-limited $O(1/\epsilon)$ scaling survives
  without a phase-estimation register, using only a classical schedule
  of Grover-iterate depths combined by maximum likelihood.
</ResearchConnection>

Instead:

1. Choose a **classical, increasing sequence** of iteration counts
   $m_1<m_2<\dots<m_K$ in advance (e.g. exponentially increasing,
   $m_k=2^{k-1}$).
2. For each $m_k$, run the circuit "$A$, then $Q$ applied $m_k$ times,
   then measure the flag qubit" some number of times, exactly like an
   ordinary Grover run: no ancilla register, no controlled-$Q$, no QFT.
3. Feed the resulting $\{(m_k, \text{measured 1-counts})\}$ into a
   **classical maximum-likelihood estimator** (or a related
   confidence-interval-combining scheme), using the known closed form
   $P(1\mid m_k)=\sin^2((2m_k+1)\theta)$ as the likelihood model for
   every round simultaneously, to reconstruct $\theta$ (hence $a$).

The precise, citable result: with the right choice of schedule (an
exponentially increasing $\{m_k\}$ combined via maximum likelihood, or
via Grinko et al.'s overlapping-confidence-interval combining rule),
this still achieves $\epsilon=O(1/M_{\max})$ precision using a *total*
Grover-iterate count across all rounds that is $O(1/\epsilon)$ (the same
Heisenberg-limited asymptotic scaling as the QPE-based algorithm), with
only state preparation, the Grover iterate, and measurement as building
blocks, and only classical (not quantum) post-processing. The intuitive
reason a handful of classically-chosen $m_k$ can pin down $\theta$ this
precisely: differentiating the closed form,

$$
\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin^2\big((2m+1)\theta\big) = (2m+1)\sin\big(2(2m+1)\theta\big)
$$

so the *local sensitivity* of a measurement at iteration count $m$ to a
small shift in $\theta$ is amplified by a factor of $(2m+1)$ relative to
a single direct measurement ($m=0$). Larger $m$ rounds are far more
informative per shot, at the cost of also being periodic (multiple
$\theta$ values can produce the same $\sin^2$ reading at large $m$).
That is why the combining step needs the *whole schedule*, not
just the largest $m_k$ alone: the low-$m$ rounds resolve which
period the high-$m$ rounds' precise-but-ambiguous reading falls in.

## Why Circuit Depth, Not Query Count, Decides

<InsightBlock>
  Same asymptotic query scaling, radically different circuit structure:
  QPE-free amplitude estimation buys hardware deployability without
  giving up the quadratic speedup.
</InsightBlock>

Both algorithms above are asymptotically optimal in the same sense:
$O(1/\epsilon)$ total Grover-iterate queries, quadratically better than
classical Monte Carlo. Where they differ is entirely in *circuit
structure*, and that difference is exactly the Quantum Hardware track's
central constraint made concrete. QPE-based amplitude estimation needs a
$t=O(\log(1/\epsilon))$-qubit ancilla register and, critically, every one
of its controlled-$Q$ applications plus the final QFT must survive
*coherently*, back to back, before any measurement collapses the state:
exactly the long, uninterrupted coherence window that decoherence and
gate error accumulate against on real, noisy hardware. The QPE-free
version never asks for that: each round is state-prep, $m_k$ plain
(uncontrolled) Grover iterates, and an immediate measurement: the same
depth budget Grover's algorithm itself already needs, just repeated a
handful of times with classical bookkeeping in between. The price is a
modest, well-quantified constant-factor overhead in total query count
relative to the QPE-based scheme's theoretical optimum (Suzuki et al.
report this overhead explicitly), not a change in scaling. This is an
instance of a pattern that shows up constantly in
present-day quantum algorithm design: "provably query-optimal in
principle" and "deployable on hardware with today's coherence times" are
different questions, and a small, honest trade in the first can buy a
large, practical win in the second.

## Running the Schedule at Theta = Pi/16

**For $\theta=\pi/12$ (so $a=\sin^2(\pi/12)\approx0.0670$), find the
Grover-iterate success probability at $m=2$.**

$\theta=\pi/12=15°$. At $m=2$: $(2\cdot2+1)\theta=5\theta=75°$, so
$P(1\mid m=2)=\sin^2(75°)\approx0.9330$, matching
`groverIterateProb(Math.PI/12, 2)`'s computed value of
{aeWorkedProb.toFixed(4)} exactly. (For reference, $a=\sin^2(15°)
\approx0.0670$, a small amplitude; already at just $m=2$ Grover
iterates the success probability has been amplified to over $93\%$,
the same amplitude-amplification effect the original Grover lesson
derived, now serving estimation rather than search.)

### The illustration schedule: $\theta=\pi/16$

Now the actual amplitude-estimation illustration. Take
$\theta=\pi/16=11.25°$, so $a=\sin^2(\pi/16)\approx$ {aeAmplitude.toFixed(4)},
a small amplitude, deliberately chosen to be
non-trivial to estimate by eye. The closed form
$P(1\mid m)=\sin^2((2m+1)\theta)$ gives, for $m=0,1,2,4$ (a small,
exponentially-spaced schedule, the kind a QPE-free algorithm would
actually query; note $m_0=0$ is just the ordinary, no-amplification
direct measurement):

| $m$ | $(2m+1)\theta$ | $P(1\mid m)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $11.25°$ | $\approx0.0381$ |
| $1$ | $33.75°$ | $\approx0.3087$ |
| $2$ | $56.25°$ | $\approx0.6913$ |
| $4$ | $101.25°$ | $\approx0.9619$ |

Four numbers, four very different measured "1"-frequencies, all
generated by the *same* underlying $\theta$. A single measurement at
$m=0$ alone only tells you $a\approx0.038$, a valid but low-sensitivity
estimate. Looking at the *pattern* across all four (a value near
$0.31$ at $m=1$ is only consistent with $\theta$ in a narrow band near
$11.25°$, and the near-saturated $0.96$ at $m=4$ narrows that band
further still, exactly as the $(2m+1)$-fold sensitivity amplification
above predicts) is what lets a maximum-likelihood combination pin down
$\theta$ far more tightly than any single $m$ could, without ever
needing a superposition over these four values.

## Reading This as a Consolation Prize

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating "QPE-free" as meaning worse scaling, a consolation prize for
giving up the QFT.** It is not: the whole point of Suzuki et al.'s and
Grinko et al.'s results is that the *same* $O(1/\epsilon)$
Heisenberg-limited scaling survives without QPE, using only a classical
schedule of Grover-iterate depths and classical maximum-likelihood
combination. What changes is circuit *structure* (no wide ancilla
register, no coherent controlled-$Q$ superposition, no QFT) and a modest
constant-factor query overhead, not the asymptotic exponent. Confusing
the two erases exactly the result that makes this line of work
interesting.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming a QPE-free schedule could just measure at one large, cleverly
chosen $m$ and skip the smaller ones.** A single large-$m$ measurement is
*more* sensitive to $\theta$ (its slope is amplified by the $(2m+1)$
factor derived above) but also more ambiguous, since $\sin^2((2m+1)
\theta)$ is periodic and many $\theta$ values can match one large-$m$
reading. The smaller-$m$ rounds in the schedule are not redundant padding:
they resolve *which* period the large-$m$ round's precise
reading actually falls in.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $\theta=\pi/16$ (this lesson's illustration amplitude), use the
   closed form to compute $P(1\mid m=3)$ by hand, and check it against
   the value shown for $m=4$ in the worked-example table. Explain, using
   the symmetry of $\sin^2$ about $90°$, why they come out equal.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At $m=3$ the angle is $(2\cdot3+1)\theta=7\times11.25°=78.75°$, so

   $$
   P(1\mid m=3)=\sin^2(78.75°)=0.9619397663 .
   $$

   The table's $m=4$ entry uses $(2\cdot4+1)\theta=9\times11.25°=101.25°$, and $\sin^2(101.25°)=0.9619397663$ as well. Evaluating both with `groverIterateProb(Math.PI/16, 3)` and `groverIterateProb(Math.PI/16, 4)` returns a difference of exactly $0$, not merely a small one: the two are the same double.

   **Why they are equal.** $78.75°+101.25°=180°$, and $\sin(180°-x)=\sin x$, so the two angles have the same sine and therefore the same square. Equivalently, in the form the question suggests, $\sin$ is symmetric about $90°$, since $\sin(90°+u)=\cos u=\sin(90°-u)$, and the two angles sit at $u=11.25°$ on either side of $90°$.

   The structural reason is worth extracting, because it generalizes past this pair. Consecutive scheduled angles differ by $2\theta=22.5°$, and the peak of the curve sits at $90°$. Whenever $90°$ falls exactly halfway between two consecutive $(2m+1)\theta$ values, those two rounds read identically. Here $\theta=\pi/16$ makes $90°$ land precisely at $8\theta$, midway between $7\theta$ and $9\theta$, so $m=3$ and $m=4$ straddle the peak symmetrically. Nothing about this is a coincidence of the numbers chosen; it is what the bar chart's visible symmetry about $m\approx3.5$ is showing.

   **Two consequences worth naming.** First, the equality is a small, concrete demonstration of the ambiguity that the second warning callout is about: a single reading of $0.962$ does not tell you whether you are before or after the peak, and here two different scheduled rounds cannot distinguish those cases from each other at all. Only the lower-$m$ rounds break the tie.

   Second, and less obvious, equal *values* do not mean equal *usefulness*. The two rounds sit at opposite slopes: differentiating gives $dP/d\theta=(2m+1)\sin(2(2m+1)\theta)$, which at $m=3$ is $7\sin(157.5°)=+2.679$ and at $m=4$ is $9\sin(202.5°)=-3.444$. Same probability, opposite sign of response, and different magnitude. A maximum-likelihood combination uses the slope, not just the reading, which is why two rounds that return identical frequencies still contribute different information.

   The wrong turn here is concluding that because $P(1\mid3)=P(1\mid4)$, one of the two rounds is redundant and could be dropped from a schedule. Their readings agree only at this exact $\theta$; the schedule has to be fixed before $\theta$ is known, and at almost any other $\theta$ the two rounds separate.

   </details>

2. Using the classical Monte Carlo scaling $n=O(1/\epsilon^2)$ and the
   quantum $M=O(1/\epsilon)$ scaling, compute the ratio of classical
   samples to quantum Grover-iterate queries needed as $\epsilon$ shrinks
   from $0.01$ to $0.0001$ (a factor-of-$100$ tighter target precision).
   By what factor does each resource requirement grow?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **The asymptotic count.** Tightening $\epsilon$ by a factor of $100$ multiplies $1/\epsilon^2$ by $100^2=10^4$ and $1/\epsilon$ by $100$. So the classical sample count grows by a factor of $\mathbf{10{,}000}$ while the quantum Grover-iterate count grows by a factor of $\mathbf{100}$.

   The ratio of the two requirements therefore also grows by $100$: it scales as

   $$
   \frac{n}{M}\;\sim\;\frac{1/\epsilon^2}{1/\epsilon}\;=\;\frac1\epsilon ,
   $$

   so at $\epsilon=0.01$ the ratio is on the order of $100$ and at $\epsilon=0.0001$ on the order of $10{,}000$. That $1/\epsilon$ is the quadratic speedup itself, written as a ratio rather than as an exponent.

   **Now put the constants back, because at these precisions they decide the answer.** The $O(\cdot)$ notation hides a prefactor on each side, and for this lesson's own amplitude they are not close to each other. Take $a=\sin^2(\pi/16)=0.0380602$, so $a(1-a)=0.0366117$ and $\sqrt{a(1-a)}=0.1913417$.

   The classical sample-mean estimator's one-standard-deviation error is $\sqrt{a(1-a)/n}$, so reaching $\epsilon$ needs $n=a(1-a)/\epsilon^2$. The BHMT bound's leading term is $2\pi\sqrt{a(1-a)}/M$, so reaching $\epsilon$ needs roughly $M=2\pi\sqrt{a(1-a)}/\epsilon$. Dividing,

   $$
   \frac{n}{M}=\frac{\sqrt{a(1-a)}}{2\pi\,\epsilon}=\frac{0.0304530}{\epsilon} .
   $$

   Solving the full BHMT bound (both terms) numerically confirms it: at $\epsilon=0.01$, $n=366$ classical shots against $M=128$ Grover iterates, a ratio of $2.86$; at $\epsilon=0.0001$, $n=3{,}661{,}165$ against $M=12{,}031$, a ratio of $304$.

   **What that says.** The advantage is a factor of about $3$ at $\epsilon=0.01$, not the factor of $100$ the bare asymptotics suggest, because the constant $2\pi/\sqrt{a(1-a)}\approx32.8$ eats most of it. The break-even point, where the two counts are equal, is at

   $$
   \epsilon=\frac{\sqrt{a(1-a)}}{2\pi}=0.0305 ,
   $$

   so for any target precision looser than about $3\%$ on this amplitude, plain classical sampling needs *fewer* shots than BHMT needs Grover iterates. The quadratic speedup is real and it is asymptotic; it does not start paying immediately.

   Three wrong turns are worth separating.

   The first is reporting the ratio as the speedup at a fixed $\epsilon$ without checking the constants, which overstates the advantage here by a factor of $32.8$. Since $\sqrt{a(1-a)}\approx\sqrt a$ for small $a$, the overstatement gets worse the rarer the event: at $a=10^{-4}$ the break-even $\epsilon$ falls to about $0.0016$.

   The second is comparing quantities that are not the same quantity. The classical $n$ above is a one-standard-deviation figure, roughly $68\%$ confidence, while BHMT's bound holds with probability $\geq8/\pi^2\approx81\%$. Matching the confidence levels shifts the classical number upward by a factor of about $1.7$ and moves break-even accordingly. The comparison is indicative, not exact, and saying so is part of getting it right.

   The third is forgetting that $M$ has to be a power of two in the QPE-based scheme, since $M=2^t$ for a $t$-qubit precision register. The $\epsilon=0.01$ requirement of $M=127.9$ rounds to $t=7$ and $M=128$, which happens to be nearly free here; the $\epsilon=0.0001$ requirement of $M=12{,}031$ rounds up to $t=14$ and $M=16{,}384$, paying a further $36\%$ that no asymptotic statement mentions. The QPE-free schedule has no such quantization, which is a small independent advantage in its favour.

   </details>

3. Explain, in your own words, why the QPE-free algorithm's per-round
   circuit (state prep, $m_k$ Grover iterates, one measurement) has the
   *same* depth character as plain Grover's algorithm itself, while the
   QPE-based algorithm's circuit does not. Tie your answer to which
   specific step requires coherence across all $2^t$ iteration counts at
   once.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **The QPE-free round is literally a Grover run.** Prepare $A|0\rangle$, apply the plain iterate $Q$ a fixed, classically chosen $m_k$ times, measure the flag qubit, write down a bit. There is no ancilla register, nothing is controlled on anything, and the measurement happens at the end of the round. Structurally that is the same object as running Grover's algorithm for $m_k$ steps and looking at the answer, which is why nothing new is asked of the hardware beyond what Grover already asks.

   **The QPE-based circuit is a different object.** The step that requires coherence across all $2^t$ iteration counts at once is the **controlled-$Q^{2^j}$ ladder**: a $t$-qubit precision register is put into a uniform superposition, and then $Q^{2^0},Q^{2^1},\dots,Q^{2^{t-1}}$ are applied to the system, each controlled on a different precision qubit. The register is then holding a superposition over every iteration count from $0$ to $2^t-1$ simultaneously, with the system entangled to it. The whole point is that the branch which applied $Q$ zero times and the branch which applied it $2^t-1$ times must still be *in superposition with each other* when the inverse QFT arrives, because the QFT extracts $2\theta$ from the relative phases those branches accumulated. Measure anything before the QFT and the interference that carries the answer is gone.

   So the coherence requirement is qualitatively different, not merely larger. QPE-free needs $m_k$ iterates to survive; QPE needs the entire ladder plus the QFT to survive as one uninterrupted quantum computation, with an entangled ancilla register held live throughout.

   **Where honesty is required, and where the lesson's claim is narrower than it first sounds.** The QPE-free scheme does *not* have shallower maximum depth in the asymptotic sense. Its deepest round uses $m_K=O(1/\epsilon)$ iterates, and the QPE circuit's ladder is also $O(1/\epsilon)$ iterates deep. That is not an accident of either construction: reaching Heisenberg-limited $O(1/\epsilon)$ precision requires a coherent run of order $1/\epsilon$ applications of $Q$ somewhere, whatever the algorithm. Claiming QPE-free wins on depth *scaling* would be claiming a better-than-Heisenberg result, which neither paper does. What it wins is everything else about the structure:

   No wide ancilla. QPE needs $t=O(\log(1/\epsilon))$ extra qubits held coherently for the whole run; QPE-free needs none.

   No controlled iterate. Every reflection inside $Q$ acquires one more control, so a multi-controlled phase of width $n$ becomes width $n+1$, and on hardware that is a real constant-factor cost paid on every one of the $O(1/\epsilon)$ applications, not a bookkeeping detail.

   No QFT, saving the $O(t^2)$ gates and, more importantly, removing a stage that has to run *after* the entire ladder while everything is still coherent.

   And the difference that matters most in a laboratory: the rounds are *independent*. Each ends in a measurement and a classical number. A round that decoheres produces one bad data point which can be discarded and rerun, and it cannot corrupt the rounds already banked. The QPE circuit is all-or-nothing: one decoherence event anywhere in the ladder or the QFT poisons the single measurement the whole computation produces, with no way to tell from the output that it happened.

   The wrong turn worth naming is inferring from "shallower circuit structure" that the *deepest* circuit got shorter. It did not. What got shorter is the interval between measurements, which is the quantity noisy hardware actually charges for.

   </details>

## Summary

- Classical Monte Carlo estimates a Bernoulli bias $a$ to precision
  $\epsilon$ using $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ samples, from the sample-mean
  estimator's $O(1/\sqrt n)$ standard deviation.
- The original (BHMT 2000) amplitude estimation algorithm reuses the
  Grover iterate's exact rotation-by-$2\theta$ structure and success
  formula $\sin^2((2k+1)\theta)$, then runs QPE on the iterate (whose
  eigenvalues are $e^{\pm2i\theta}$) to reach $O(1/\epsilon)$
  Heisenberg-limited scaling, at the cost of a wide ancilla register and
  a fully coherent controlled-$Q$-superposition-plus-QFT circuit.
- Modern QPE-free methods (maximum-likelihood / iterative amplitude
  estimation, Suzuki et al. 2020, Grinko et al. 2021) reuse the *same*
  Grover iterate and closed form, but query only a classical, increasing
  schedule of iteration counts $m_k$ (e.g. $m_k=2^{k-1}$), each measured
  directly, and combine the results via classical maximum likelihood.
  The total query count is still $O(1/\epsilon)$, now with no QFT and no wide
  coherent ancilla register.
- Verified directly on $\theta=\pi/16$: the schedule $m=0,1,2,4$ gives
  measured probabilities $\approx0.038,0.309,0.691,0.962$. The pattern
  across these four points, not any single one of them, pins down
  $\theta$ precisely; that is the mechanism the maximum-likelihood
  combination step formalizes.
- This is a "provably optimal in theory" vs. "practically
  deployable on noisy, near-term hardware" tradeoff: same asymptotic
  query scaling, dramatically shallower and shorter-coherence circuit
  structure.
