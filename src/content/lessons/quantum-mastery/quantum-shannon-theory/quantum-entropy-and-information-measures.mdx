  for (let i = 1; i < points.length; i++) {
    const prevPoint = points[i - 1];
    const currPoint = points[i];
    if (Math.sign(prevPoint.condEntropy) !== Math.sign(currPoint.condEntropy)) {
      const t = prevPoint.condEntropy / (prevPoint.condEntropy - currPoint.condEntropy);
      return prevPoint.p + t * (currPoint.p - prevPoint.p);
    }
  }
  return null;
}

## Motivation

Purity, von Neumann Entropy, and Information built $S(\rho)$ as the
quantum generalization of Shannon entropy; Relative Entropy and
Mixed-State Entanglement proved $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq0$. Both lessons ask
"how mixed/how distinguishable is *one* state?" This lesson asks the
question classical Shannon theory answers with mutual information and
conditional entropy: given a joint system $AB$, how much does knowing
$B$ tell you about $A$, and how much *uncertainty about $A$ remains*
once $B$ is known? Every classical fact you'd expect to carry over does
— except one, and that one exception is the entire reason this course
exists as "Quantum" Shannon theory rather than a relabeled copy of the
classical version.

## Mathematical Development

### Quantum mutual information

$$
I(A:B) \equiv S(\rho_A) + S(\rho_B) - S(\rho_{AB})
$$

the direct quantum analogue of the classical $I(A:B)=H(A)+H(B)-H(AB)$,
with each Shannon entropy replaced by von Neumann entropy of the
corresponding (reduced) density matrix. It measures the *total*
correlation between $A$ and $B$—classical and quantum combined —
packed into a single number.

**Worked example 1: the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$.** Built exactly as
Entanglement Entropy for Pure Bipartite States built it, via
`pureStateDensityMatrix` then `reducedDensityMatrixQubit0`/`1`, then
this platform's own `vonNeumannEntropy`—reused directly here, not
reimplemented:

$$
S(\rho_{AB}) = 0 \text{ (pure global state)}, \qquad S(\rho_A) = S(\rho_B) = 1 \text{ bit (maximally mixed reduced states)}
$$

$$
I(A:B) = 1 + 1 - 0 = 2 \text{ bits}
$$

This is the *maximum possible* mutual information for two qubits (each
qubit can carry at most 1 bit of entropy, so $I(A:B)\leq S(\rho_A)+S(\rho_B)\leq2$):
a Bell pair saturates the bound completely.

**Worked example 2: a classically correlated mixed state.** Not every
correlated state is entangled. Take
$\rho_{AB}=\tfrac12|00\rangle\langle00|+\tfrac12|11\rangle\langle11|$ —
a genuine classical mixture (via `convexCombination`, built the same
way Convex Combinations and Physical Mixtures established) of two
*unentangled* basis states, with no coherence between them at all:

$$
S(\rho_{AB}) = H(0.5,0,0,0.5) = 1 \text{ bit}, \qquad S(\rho_A)=S(\rho_B)=H(0.5,0.5)=1 \text{ bit each}
$$

$$
I(A:B) = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1 \text{ bit}
$$

Exactly the classical mutual information of two perfectly correlated
coin flips—unsurprising, since this state *is* two perfectly
correlated classical bits with a density-matrix label. $I(A:B)=1$ sits
strictly between the classically-correlated-but-unentangled case here
and the maximally entangled Bell state's $I(A:B)=2$: mutual information
alone cannot distinguish "correlated" from "entangled" (both give a
nonzero number), which is exactly the gap the next quantity closes.

### Conditional quantum entropy

$$
S(A|B) \equiv S(\rho_{AB}) - S(\rho_B)
$$

again the direct analogue of classical $H(A|B)=H(AB)-H(B)$, interpreted
the same way classically: the *remaining* uncertainty about $A$ once $B$
is fully known. Classically, $H(A|B)\geq0$ always—you cannot know
*less than nothing*. Applied to the Bell state:

$$
S(A|B) = S(\rho_{AB}) - S(\rho_B) = 0 - 1 = -1 \text{ bit}
$$

**Negative.** Not a typo, not a sign error, and not a different
quantity that happens to share the classical name. This is computed
from the exact same `vonNeumannEntropy` calls as every other number in
this lesson, on a genuinely valid density matrix. For the classically
correlated state above, by contrast, $S(A|B)=1-1=0$—non-negative, as
the classical formula demands, because that state carries no
entanglement at all. The sign of $S(A|B)$ is doing real physical work.

### Coherent information

$$
I(A{>}B) \equiv -S(A|B) = S(\rho_B) - S(\rho_{AB})
$$

defined for a channel $\mathcal N$ that takes some input system to
output $B$ (with $A$ standing in for a reference system purifying the
input, exactly the purification construction Schmidt Decomposition and
Purification built). $I(A{>}B)>0$ exactly when $S(A|B)<0$: coherent
information is positive precisely when the channel preserves enough
quantum correlation with the reference to make quantum information
recoverable on the other side. Its regularized version,
$Q(\mathcal N)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\tfrac1n\max I(A{>}B)$ over $n$ uses of
the channel, *is* the channel's quantum capacity (the LSD theorem,
Lloyd-Shor-Devetak)—stated here, not proved, and picked back up in
this course's capstone, which asks exactly how much quantum information
a noisy channel can carry.

## Why This Matters

Classical conditional entropy $H(A|B)\geq0$ is not an incidental
property of Shannon's formula—it is a statement about what
information *is*: you cannot be more uncertain about $A$ after learning
$B$ than knowing nothing at all, because in the worst case $B$ is simply
useless and $H(A|B)=H(A)$. Every classical intuition about
"information" is built on that floor.

Quantum mechanics breaks it, and $S(A|B)=-1$ bit for the Bell state is
the single most quoted, most surprising result in quantum information
theory precisely because of what it does *not* mean. It does not mean
you know more than everything about $A$. It means the question "what is
$A$'s state, on its own?" has stopped making sense: $\rho_A$ is
maximally mixed and carries zero information by itself, while $\rho_{AB}$
carries *more* information (zero entropy, i.e. complete information)
than $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ separately would suggest. All of that
"missing" information is entanglement, not ignorance, and it has a
precise operational price tag: the magnitude $|S(A|B)|$ is exactly the
number of qubits of quantum communication needed to transfer $A$ to
whoever already holds $B$, so that they can reconstruct the full joint
state (the **quantum state merging** protocol, Horodecki-Oppenheim-Winter
2005). When $S(A|B)<0$, state merging *generates* $|S(A|B)|$ EPR pairs
as a byproduct instead of consuming qubits—negative conditional
entropy is a resource, not a paradox, and it is exactly why "how much
can be learned/sent" needs $S(A|B)$ and $I(A{>}B)$ as precise vocabulary
rather than loose analogy to the classical case.

## Worked Example: watching entanglement dilute

Take the Werner state this course's prerequisite lesson already built,
$\rho(p)=p|\Psi^-\rangle\langle\Psi^-|+(1-p)\,I/4$, and sweep $p$ from 0
(maximally mixed, no correlation at all) to 1 (the singlet, maximally
entangled). Two facts, both checked directly rather than assumed:

**$S(\rho_A)=S(\rho_B)=1$ bit for every $p$.** Computed via
`reducedDensityMatrixQubit0`/`1` then `vonNeumannEntropy` at five sample
points:

$$
p=0,\ 0.25,\ 0.5,\ 0.75,\ 1 \ \Longrightarrow\ S(\rho_A)=S(\rho_B)=1.000000 \text{ bit, every time}
$$

This holds because both the singlet and $I/4$ individually reduce to
$I/2$ on either qubit, so *any* convex mixture of them does too —
diluting the singlet with noise never changes either marginal, only the
correlation between them.

**$S(\rho_{AB})$ varies smoothly, computed exactly via the Bell basis.**
$I/4$ is diagonal in *every* basis, and $|\Psi^-\rangle\langle\Psi^-|$ is
already diagonal in the Bell basis (eigenvalue 1 on $|\Psi^-\rangle$, 0
on the other three Bell vectors)—so $\rho(p)$ is diagonal in the Bell
basis too, with eigenvalues $\lambda_{\Psi^-}=\tfrac{1+3p}{4}$ and
$\lambda_{\text{other}}=\tfrac{1-p}{4}$ (each of the other three Bell
vectors), verified directly by applying $\rho(p)$ to all four Bell
vectors and checking each is a genuine eigenvector. This is the same
"diagonalize via symmetry, not a general eigensolver" move Relative
Entropy and Mixed-State Entanglement used on this exact state family.
Feeding those eigenvalues through `shannonEntropyBits` (the same
$-\sum\lambda\log_2\lambda$ formula `vonNeumannEntropy` uses internally,
written out explicitly here since the joint state is 4-dimensional and
this platform's closed-form eigensolver is 2x2-only) gives $S(\rho_{AB})$
at every $p$, hence

$$
I(A:B) = 2 - S(\rho_{AB}), \qquad S(A|B) = S(\rho_{AB}) - 1
$$

At $p=1$: $S(\rho_{AB})=0\Rightarrow I(A:B)=2,\ S(A|B)=-1$ (the Bell-state
numbers above, since $|\Psi^-\rangle$ is a Bell state too). At $p=0$:
$S(\rho_{AB})=2\Rightarrow I(A:B)=0,\ S(A|B)=1$ (no correlation, and the
Bell-state number's classical floor). Between the two, both quantities
move continuously.

Scanning the same computed grid this curve is drawn from, $S(A|B)$
changes sign between $p=0.725$ ($S(A|B)\approx+0.061$) and $p=0.750$
($S(A|B)\approx-0.007$); linear interpolation between those two
computed points puts the crossing at $p\approx0.7476$. Compare that to
the *entanglement* threshold Relative Entropy and Mixed-State
Entanglement's Werner-state concurrence found: $C(p)=\max(0,(3p-1)/2)$
is already positive for any $p>1/3\approx0.333$. **The two thresholds
are not the same.** Every Werner state with $p>1/3$ is genuinely
entangled, but $S(A|B)$ only turns negative much later, past
$p\approx0.7476$. Negative conditional entropy is a *sufficient*
signature of entanglement, never a *necessary* one—there is a wide
entangled-but-$S(A|B)\geq0$ regime ($1/3<p\lesssim0.7476$) sitting
between the separable region and the region where the quantum-state-merging
"bonus qubits" effect kicks in.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking $S(A|B)<0$ is a paradox, a sign error, or evidence that
someone knows "more than everything" about $A$.** It is none of these.
$\rho_A$ on its own is still exactly as mixed as its entropy says (1 bit,
maximally uncertain, for the Bell state)—what's negative is a
*difference*, $S(\rho_{AB})-S(\rho_B)$, and that difference is negative
precisely because $\rho_{AB}$ is *more* pure (lower entropy) than
$\rho_B$ alone, which can only happen through entanglement. Classical
joint distributions can never do this ($H(AB)\geq H(B)$ always, since
classical joint uncertainty can't be less than either marginal's), which
is exactly why this has no classical counterpart to sanity-check it
against.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming negative $S(A|B)$ and "entangled" are the same threshold.**
This lesson's own sweep disproves it directly: the Werner state is
entangled for every $p>1/3$ (via concurrence) but $S(A|B)$ stays
non-negative until $p\approx0.7476$. Mutual information, conditional
entropy, and concurrence are three genuinely different quantities that
agree only at the extremes ($p=0$ and $p=1$), not everywhere in between.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Quantum mutual information $I(A:B)=S(\rho_A)+S(\rho_B)-S(\rho_{AB})$
  measures total (classical + quantum) correlation; computed here as
  exactly 2 bits for the Bell state (the two-qubit maximum) and exactly
  1 bit for a purely classical correlated mixture, using this platform's
  own `vonNeumannEntropy` throughout.
- Conditional quantum entropy $S(A|B)=S(\rho_{AB})-S(\rho_B)$ can be
  **negative**—$-1$ bit for the Bell state, computed directly, not
  asserted—unlike its classical counterpart $H(A|B)\geq0$. The
  magnitude of a negative $S(A|B)$ is the exact quantum-state-merging
  communication cost (here, a *savings*: EPR pairs generated, not spent).
- Coherent information $I(A{>}B)=-S(A|B)$ controls a channel's ability
  to carry quantum information; its regularization is the channel's
  quantum capacity (LSD theorem), picked up again in this course's
  capstone.
- Swept across the Werner-state family, $S(A|B)$ crosses zero near
  $p\approx0.7476$—strictly later than the $p=1/3$ entanglement
  threshold from concurrence, proving negative conditional entropy is a
  sufficient, not necessary, signature of entanglement.
