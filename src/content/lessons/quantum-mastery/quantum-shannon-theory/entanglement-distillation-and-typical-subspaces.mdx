export const binomialCoefficient = (n, k) => {
  let result = 1;
  for (let i = 0; i < k; i += 1) {
    result = (result * (n - i)) / (i + 1);
  }
  return result;
};

export const schmidtTypeDistribution = (n, p) => {
  const q = 1 - p;
  return Array.from({ length: n + 1 }, (_, k) => {
    const count = binomialCoefficient(n, k);
    const probability = count * Math.pow(p, k) * Math.pow(q, n - k);
    return { k, count, probability };
  });
};

## Motivation

The Data-Processing Inequality treated von Neumann entropy and mutual
information as abstract bookkeeping quantities: numbers a channel or a
state carries, useful for proving inequalities. This lesson asks a much
more physical question about entanglement specifically: if two parties,
Alice and Bob, share $n$ copies of a *partially* entangled state, not a
perfect Bell pair, can they use only **local operations and classical
communication (LOCC)** — no direct interaction between them, exactly the
restriction that makes entanglement a genuine resource rather than
something either party could produce alone — to convert those $n$
imperfect copies into a smaller number of *nearly perfect* Bell pairs?
The answer is yes, the protocol is called **entanglement distillation**,
and the reason it works at all is a single sharp idea, adapted directly
from Schumacher's proof that quantum data can be compressed: for many
identical copies of a state, almost all of the probability mass
concentrates on a comparatively tiny **typical subspace**. This lesson
derives that idea from scratch and checks it against real, computed
numbers, not just the clean asymptotic formula.

## Mathematical Development

### Setup: $n$ copies and their joint Schmidt coefficients

Take the partially entangled 2-qubit state

$$
|\psi\rangle = \sqrt{p}\,|00\rangle + \sqrt{1-p}\,|11\rangle, \qquad p\neq \tfrac12,
$$

already in Schmidt form (Schmidt Decomposition and Purification), with
Schmidt coefficients $p$ and $1-p$ and entanglement entropy
$S(\rho_A)=H(p)\equiv -p\log_2 p-(1-p)\log_2(1-p)$, the **binary Shannon
entropy** — exactly the classical quantity this course's entropy lesson
built, now reappearing because $\rho_A=\mathrm{diag}(p,1-p)$ is already
diagonal, so its von Neumann entropy is *literally* a classical Shannon
entropy with no further work.

Now take $n$ independent copies, $|\psi\rangle^{\otimes n}$, still a
single bipartite pure state (system $A$ = all $n$ of Alice's qubits,
system $B$ = all $n$ of Bob's). Expanding the tensor power term by term,
every one of the $2^n$ terms is fixed by choosing, independently for
each of the $n$ copies, whether that copy landed in the $|00\rangle$
branch (weight $\sqrt p$) or the $|11\rangle$ branch (weight $\sqrt{1-p}$).
A term with exactly $k$ of its $n$ copies in the $|00\rangle$ branch
(and $n-k$ in the $|11\rangle$ branch) contributes Schmidt coefficient

$$
\lambda_k = p^{\,k}(1-p)^{\,n-k}
$$

and $|\psi\rangle^{\otimes n}$ is *already* in Schmidt form over the
$2^n$-dimensional computational basis on each side (every term is a
distinct, orthogonal product basis state on each side, paired index for
index, exactly the Schmidt structure Schmidt Decomposition and
Purification proved holds for any bipartite pure state). There are
$\binom{n}{k}$ terms of type $k$ (choosing which $k$ of the $n$ copies
are the $|00\rangle$ ones), each carrying the same coefficient $\lambda_k$,
so the *total* Schmidt weight at type $k$ is

$$
P(k) = \binom{n}{k}\,p^{k}(1-p)^{n-k}
$$

This is exactly the **binomial distribution** $\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p)$
evaluated at $k$, with mean $np$: since $p$ is the *larger* of the two
branch weights whenever $p>\tfrac12$, the typical types cluster near
$k\approx np$, i.e. near "almost all copies in the $|00\rangle$ branch,"
exactly matching intuition.

### The typical subspace

<TheoremBox title="Typical subspace concentration (Schumacher)" provenance="cited">

By the law of large numbers, as $n\to\infty$ the binomial distribution
above concentrates its probability mass on types whose fraction of
$|00\rangle$-branch copies, $k/n$, lies within any fixed $\epsilon>0$ of
$p$ (equivalently, whose exponential rate $-\tfrac1n\log_2\lambda_k$ lies
within $\epsilon$ of $H(p)$). Call this the **typical set** $T_\epsilon$. Its probability
mass $\to 1$ as $n\to\infty$, while its *size* is only
$|T_\epsilon|\approx 2^{nH(p)}$, exponentially smaller than the full
space of dimension $2^n$ whenever $H(p)<1$, i.e. whenever $|\psi\rangle$
is not already maximally entangled.

</TheoremBox>

This is the same theorem, applied to Schmidt coefficients instead of
classical letter probabilities, that Shannon's source coding theorem and
its quantum analogue (Schumacher compression) both rest on: a source
that looks like it needs $n$ bits (or qubits) to describe can actually
be compressed into only $\approx nH$ of them, because only the typical
outcomes ever really occur. Here the same counting argument says
something about *entanglement* rather than compression: the typical
subspace, restricted to just its $\approx 2^{nH(p)}$ dimensions, carries
almost all of $|\psi\rangle^{\otimes n}$'s probability weight, and,
crucially, its Schmidt coefficients are all *nearly equal* to
$2^{-nH(p)}$ within that subspace (every typical type $k$ has
$-\tfrac1n\log_2\lambda_k\approx H(p)$ by definition, so
$\lambda_k\approx 2^{-nH(p)}$ for every one of them). A state whose
nonzero Schmidt coefficients are all nearly equal is, by definition,
nearly **maximally entangled** within that subspace.

### Entanglement concentration: distilling Bell pairs from the typical subspace

<DefinitionBox title="Entanglement concentration">

Alice performs a local projective measurement onto the typical subspace
(a real quantum operation: project her $n$ qubits onto the subspace
spanned by typical strings, and discard the rare failure outcome). With
probability $\to 1$ as $n\to\infty$, this succeeds, and the post-projection
state is nearly maximally entangled across a space of dimension
$\approx 2^{nH(p)}$. Relabeling that space's basis (a local unitary on
each side, no communication needed beyond agreeing on the labeling in
advance) turns it into $m\approx nH(p)$ ordinary qubits per side in a
state extremely close to $m$ perfect Bell pairs $|\Phi^+\rangle^{\otimes m}$.

</DefinitionBox>

This protocol, due to Bennett, Bernstein, Popescu, and Schumacher (1996),
is called **entanglement concentration**: it is the special, cleanest
case of general distillation, restricted to *pure* partially-entangled
states, and it is worth deriving explicitly because every step above is
a real, checkable claim (a projection, a counting argument, a
relabeling), not a black box. The achievable rate,
$m/n\to H(p)=S(\rho_A)$ Bell pairs per copy, is also *optimal*: no LOCC
protocol on pure states can do better, because entanglement entropy is
exactly conserved on average under LOCC for pure bipartite states (a
fact proved elsewhere in this course's entropy lesson via concavity, not
re-derived here).

### Beyond pure states: mixed states and coherent information

Real shared entanglement is never a perfect, isolated pure state:
transmission through fiber or free space adds noise, so Alice and Bob
actually share $n$ copies of some *mixed* state $\rho_{AB}$. The typical
subspace idea still applies (via the state's purification: distilling
$\rho_{AB}^{\otimes n}$ is equivalent to distilling entanglement from a
purification $|\psi\rangle_{ABE}$ against an inaccessible environment
$E$), but the clean projective argument above is no longer literally
enough by itself, because $E$ carries information that could be
correlated with $A$ or $B$ in ways a single projection doesn't remove.
General mixed-state distillation protocols — **entanglement pumping**
(distill a smaller number of higher-fidelity pairs from many noisy ones,
iterate) and **hashing** (use many copies' worth of local Clifford
operations and classical syndrome measurements, structurally close to a
quantum error-correcting code) — handle this at the cost of more
involved LOCC. The achievable rate is bounded above by, and for
important state classes (e.g. states arising from degradable channels)
asymptotically equals, the **coherent information**
$I_c(A\rangle B)=S(\rho_B)-S(\rho_{AB})$ from The Data-Processing
Inequality: exactly the same quantity that lesson used to bound how much
quantum information a noisy channel can carry, now reappearing as the
bound on how much entanglement noisy channel *use* can generate. That
coincidence is not an accident: it is the technical heart of this
course's own capstone on channel capacities, which this lesson's typical
subspace argument is the concrete, fully-worked special case of.

## Physical Interpretation — Why This Matters

Distillation is not a mathematical curiosity: it is the specific reason
long-distance quantum communication is possible at all. A pair of
entangled photons sent through real optical fiber picks up loss and
decoherence over distance, so the entanglement Alice and Bob actually
end up sharing is never a perfect Bell pair: it degrades roughly
exponentially with distance. **Quantum repeaters** solve this by
chaining shorter, higher-fidelity entangled links and periodically
*distilling*: sacrificing some pairs' worth of noisy entanglement to
purify the rest, exactly the entanglement-pumping idea above, before
that entanglement is used for teleportation or quantum key distribution
(QKD). Without distillation, entanglement-based QKD's security
guarantees degrade along with the fidelity of the shared state; with it,
a noisy channel can still support secure key generation, provided the
noise is below distillation's threshold.

Distillation also answers a question the Quantum Computing pillar's own
[Entanglement Entropy for Pure Bipartite States](/lessons/quantum-computing/entanglement-and-measurement/entanglement-entropy-for-pure-states)
lesson only ever *asserted*: why should entanglement entropy, a number
computed from an abstract density matrix's eigenvalues, be *the*
measure of "how entangled" a state is, rather than just one convenient
choice among many? This lesson supplies the missing operational answer.
**Distillable entanglement** (the asymptotic rate of perfect Bell pairs
extractable per copy by LOCC) is a measure defined purely in terms of
what a physical protocol can actually *do* with the state, with no
reference to entropy at all. The fact that this operational quantity
turns out to equal $S(\rho_A)$ exactly, for pure states, is what makes
entanglement entropy more than an abstract formula: it is the honest
exchange rate between raw partially-entangled resource and usable,
near-perfect entanglement, provable rather than assumed.

## Worked Example

**Take $p=0.9$, so $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.9}\,|00\rangle+\sqrt{0.1}\,|11\rangle$.
Compute $H(0.9)$, then take $n=6$ copies (small enough to enumerate all
$2^6=64$ Schmidt coefficients by type) and check how much of the
probability mass the typical set actually captures.**

`vonNeumannEntropy` applied to $\rho_A=\mathrm{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ (already
diagonal, since $|\psi\rangle$'s off-diagonal amplitudes are zero) gives

$$
H(0.9) = -0.9\log_2(0.9) - 0.1\log_2(0.1) \approx 0.468996 \text{ bits}
$$

(`entropyWorked` above, built by feeding `psiWorked` through the same
`pureStateDensityMatrix` $\to$ `reducedDensityMatrixQubit0` $\to$
`vonNeumannEntropy` pipeline the Schmidt lesson used, agrees with this
hand calculation to six decimal places, as it must, since both routes
diagonalize the same $2\times2$ matrix).

For $n=6$, recall $k$ counts how many of the six copies land in the
$|00\rangle$ branch (weight $\sqrt{0.9}$ each), so the joint Schmidt
coefficient at type $k$ is $\lambda_k=0.9^{k}\cdot0.1^{6-k}$, occurring
with multiplicity $\binom{6}{k}$, giving type-probability
$P(k)=\binom{6}{k}\,0.9^{k}\,0.1^{6-k}$:

| $k$ | $\binom{6}{k}$ | $P(k)$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 0.000001 |
| 1 | 6 | 0.000054 |
| 2 | 15 | 0.001215 |
| 3 | 20 | 0.014580 |
| 4 | 15 | 0.098415 |
| 5 | 6 | 0.354294 |
| 6 | 1 | 0.531441 |

(`dist6` above, computed directly from `schmidtTypeDistribution`, not
asserted; the seven values sum to exactly $1.000000$.) The mean type is
$np=6\times0.9=5.4$, with standard deviation
$\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\approx0.7348$. Defining the typical set here as
types within one standard deviation of the mean, $k\in[4.665,\,6.135]$,
gives exactly $k\in\{5,6\}$ — the two largest-$k$ types, since $p=0.9$
means most copies are expected in the $|00\rangle$ branch.

combined probability mass is

$$
P(k=5)+P(k=6) = 0.354294+0.531441 = 0.885735 \approx 88.57\%

leaving only $\approx 11.43\%$ of the weight on the remaining, atypical
types $k=0,\ldots,4$ — already, at $n=6$, the great majority of the
probability sits on just two of the seven possible types. In terms of
raw *dimension* (individual basis strings, not types): those two typical
types together account for $\binom{6}{5}+\binom{6}{6}=6+1=7$ of the full
$2^6=64$-dimensional space — only $10.9\%$ of the dimension, yet
$88.6\%$ of the probability. The asymptotic prediction
$2^{nH(p)}=2^{6\times0.468996}\approx7.03$ is already close
to the exact count of $7$, even at this small, by-hand-checkable $n$.
Entanglement concentration's target rate here is
$m\approx nH(p)=6\times0.468996\approx2.81$ Bell pairs from six copies
(`distillableBellPairsWorked` above) — consistent with the typical
subspace's actual dimension of $7$ sitting between $2^2=4$ and $2^3=8$,
i.e. embeddable in $3$ qubits per side with some residual imperfection,
not a clean integer number of pairs at this small $n$. Only as
$n\to\infty$ does $nH(p)$ round cleanly to a whole number of
near-perfect Bell pairs per copy.

<Callout type="note">
This is a genuine, checkable trend toward the $n\to\infty$ theorem, not
the theorem itself. The clean statements "$|T_\epsilon|\approx 2^{nH(p)}$"
and "the typical subspace is nearly maximally entangled" are *limiting*
claims. At $n=6$ they already point the right direction, as the numbers
above show, but they are not yet sharp — see Common Mistakes below for
exactly where this small-$n$ example still falls short of the limit.
</Callout>

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating "individually most probable" and "typical" as the same
thing.** At $n=6$, the individual Schmidt coefficient for the
all-$|00\rangle$ string ($k=6$, weight $0.9^6=0.531441$) is exactly
$9\times$ larger than any single $k=5$ string's coefficient
($0.9^5\times0.1=0.059049$, the ratio computed directly as
`flatnessRatio6` above). In this worked example $k=6$ happens to be both
the single most probable individual string *and* inside the typical
set — but that agreement is a small-$n$, large-$p$ coincidence, not a
general fact. The typical-subspace theorem is a statement about
concentration of the *empirical frequency* $k/n$ around $p$, not about
which single string is most likely; for large $n$, the probability of
the single all-$|00\rangle$ string, $p^n$, actually vanishes, even
though it always remains the single most probable individual string.
Only in the $n\to\infty$ limit do "typical" and "individually most
probable" cleanly separate into visibly different sets.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the typical subspace's Schmidt coefficients are already
"nearly equal" at small $n$.** The theorem's claim that typical
coefficients are all $\approx 2^{-nH(p)}$ is an $n\to\infty$ statement.
Here at $n=6$, the two typical coefficients are $0.9^6\approx0.531$ and
$0.9^5\times0.1\approx0.059$ (per string) — a factor of $9$ apart, not
remotely flat. Reporting "the typical subspace is nearly maximally
entangled" from this $n=6$ example alone, without the asymptotic
caveat, overstates what six copies actually demonstrate; the honest
claim is that the *trend* toward flatness (relative to the exponential
scale $2^{-nH(p)}$) is visible, not that flatness has been reached.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Entanglement distillation** converts many copies of a partially
  entangled or noisy shared state into fewer, nearly-perfect Bell pairs,
  using only LOCC.
- For $n$ copies of a pure partially-entangled state
  $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt p|00\rangle+\sqrt{1-p}|11\rangle$, the joint
  Schmidt coefficients are binomially weighted by type $k$; the **typical
  subspace** of types near $k\approx pn$ has dimension
  $\approx2^{nH(p)}$ and captures almost all the probability weight as
  $n\to\infty$ — a real, verified trend even at the small, by-hand
  $n=6,p=0.9$ example above, where two of seven types already carry
  $\approx88.6\%$ of the weight.
- Projecting onto the typical subspace and relabeling
  (**entanglement concentration**, Bennett–Bernstein–Popescu–Schumacher)
  distills $m\approx nH(p)=nS(\rho_A)$ near-perfect Bell pairs, the
  optimal LOCC rate for pure states.
- For mixed, noisy shared states, more involved LOCC protocols
  (entanglement pumping, hashing) still work, with achievable rate
  bounded by — and for some state classes asymptotically equal to — the
  coherent information $I_c(A\rangle B)$, directly foreshadowing this
  course's capstone on channel capacities.
- Distillation is what makes quantum repeaters, and therefore
  long-distance QKD and teleportation, physically possible over noisy
  channels, and it is the operational justification for treating
  entanglement entropy as *the* entanglement measure in the first place.
