## Motivation

Every measurement this platform has used so far, from The Measurement
Postulate, Generalized through Quantum Channels: Kraus and Choi's own
Kraus-operator formalism, has been built from **orthogonal projectors**
$\{P_i\}$: $P_iP_j=\delta_{ij}P_i$, $\sum_iP_i=I$, outcome probability
$\text{Tr}(P_i\rho)$. That restriction is quietly limiting in two ways
that turn out to matter a great deal: an orthogonal projective
measurement on a $d$-dimensional system can have **at most $d$
outcomes** (a qubit gets at most 2), and it can never be optimal for
telling apart two *non-orthogonal* quantum states, a problem that shows
up the instant a real device (or an eavesdropper) has to guess which of
several possible signal states it was handed. This lesson introduces the
strictly more general object that removes both restrictions, and proves,
not just states, why it is the right generalization.

## Mathematical Development

### The definition

<DefinitionBox title="Positive operator-valued measure (POVM)">

A **POVM** is a finite set of operators $\{E_i\}$ on a Hilbert space,
each **positive semi-definite** ($E_i\geq0$, i.e. $\langle\phi|E_i|\phi\rangle\geq0$
for every $|\phi\rangle$) but *not* required to be a projector
($E_i^2\neq E_i$ in general, and the $E_i$ need not be mutually
orthogonal), satisfying the **completeness relation**

$$
\sum_i E_i = I
$$

The outcome probabilities are given by the same Born rule this platform
has used throughout, just with $E_i$ (an "effect") standing in for a
projector: $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$.

</DefinitionBox>

Nothing here says the $E_i$ are orthogonal, and nothing caps the number
of outcomes at the Hilbert space dimension — a qubit can have a
3-outcome, 4-outcome, or arbitrarily-many-outcome POVM, which is exactly
the extra room the worked example below needs.

### Why $\sum_iE_i=I$ is exactly the right condition

This isn't an arbitrary normalization convention; it's forced by
requiring $\{P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)\}$ to be an honest probability
distribution for *every* physical state $\rho$, in two separate pieces.

**Non-negativity.** $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)\geq0$ must hold for every
density matrix $\rho$. Taking $\rho=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$ for an
arbitrary $|\phi\rangle$ gives $\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)=\langle\phi|E_i|\phi\rangle$,
so this condition holding for *every* $|\phi\rangle$ is, by definition,
exactly $E_i\geq0$.

**Normalization.** $\sum_iP(i)=1$ must hold for every $\rho$, i.e.
$\text{Tr}\big((\sum_iE_i)\rho\big)=\text{Tr}(\rho)=1$ for every density
matrix. Exactly the argument Quantum Channels: Kraus and Choi used to
pin down trace-preservation from the Choi matrix: test this identity on
the spanning set of basis matrices $\rho=|j\rangle\langle k|$ (extending
by linearity past just density matrices). Writing $A\equiv\sum_iE_i$,
$\text{Tr}(A|j\rangle\langle k|)=A_{kj}$ must equal
$\text{Tr}(|j\rangle\langle k|)=\delta_{jk}$ for every $j,k$, which
forces $A=I$ entry by entry. So $\sum_iE_i=I$ is not one *sufficient*
way to get a valid distribution — it is the unique *necessary* condition,
by the same reasoning this course already trusted for a different
completeness relation.

### Naimark's dilation theorem

<TheoremBox title="Naimark's dilation theorem" provenance="derived">

Every POVM $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^n$ on a $d$-dimensional system $A$ can be
realized as an ordinary projective (von Neumann) measurement on a larger
system: adjoin an ancilla of dimension $d_{\text{anc}}$ with
$n\leq d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}$, apply a fixed unitary $U$ to the joint
$(d\cdot d_{\text{anc}})$-dimensional space, and measure the joint system
in a fixed orthonormal basis. The resulting outcome statistics reproduce
$P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ exactly.

</TheoremBox>

**Proof.** Since each $E_i\geq0$, it has a positive semi-definite square
root; more generally pick *any* $M_i$ with $M_i^\dagger M_i=E_i$ (the
canonical choice $M_i=\sqrt{E_i}$ always works, by the spectral theorem
already used throughout this course). If $A$ is $d$-dimensional, each
$M_i$ can be taken $1\times d$ (a bra) when $E_i$ has rank 1, or more
generally $\text{rank}(E_i)\times d$; stack all the $M_i$ row-by-row
into a single $n\times d$ matrix $V$ (padding with extra rows of $M_i$
in the rank-$>1$ case, or zero rows to reach a convenient total
dimension). Then

$$
V^\dagger V = \sum_i M_i^\dagger M_i = \sum_i E_i = I_d
$$

so $V$ is an **isometry**: its $d$ columns are orthonormal vectors in
$\mathbb C^n$. Any isometry extends to a full unitary $U$ on
$\mathbb C^n$ by completing those $d$ orthonormal columns to a full
orthonormal basis of $\mathbb C^n$ (Gram-Schmidt on the orthogonal
complement of $V$'s range) — the same "extend to a full basis" move the
SVD-based Schmidt decomposition proof already used, run here on an
isometry instead of a single vector. If $n\leq d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}$,
embed $\mathbb C^n$ into $\mathbb C^d\otimes\mathbb C^{d_{\text{anc}}}$
(padding with zero rows for the unused basis vectors) so $U$ is a
genuine unitary on system-plus-ancilla. For any state $|\psi\rangle$ of
$A$,

$$
P(\text{basis outcome }i) = \big|\langle e_i|U|\psi\rangle\big|^2 = \big|(V\psi)_i\big|^2 = \langle\psi|M_i^\dagger M_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|E_i|\psi\rangle = \text{Tr}(E_i\rho)
$$

reproducing the POVM's statistics from an ordinary projective
measurement on the enlarged space. $\blacksquare$

A qubit ($d=2$) with $n=3$ outcomes needs $d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}\geq3$;
a trivial "ancilla" of dimension 1 gives only $2<3$, so *some* ancilla is
genuinely required, and a single **qubit** ancilla ($d_{\text{anc}}=2$,
joint dimension 4) is already enough room. The worked example below
builds this $n=3$, $d=2$, $d_{\text{anc}}=2$ case explicitly, with every
matrix multiplied out and checked.

### Quantum instruments: what a POVM leaves out

A POVM fixes $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$, but says nothing about what
state the system is left in *given* outcome $i$ occurred — and that
post-measurement state is not determined by $E_i$ alone.

<DefinitionBox title="Quantum instrument">

An **instrument** is a collection of completely positive maps
$\{\mathcal I_i\}$, each with a Kraus form
$\mathcal I_i(\rho)=\sum_kK_{i,k}\rho K_{i,k}^\dagger$ (Quantum
Channels: Kraus and Choi's own Kraus operators, one Kraus family per
outcome), such that $\sum_i\mathcal I_i$ is a full CPTP channel. The
associated POVM element is $E_i=\sum_kK_{i,k}^\dagger K_{i,k}$, and the
(unnormalized) post-measurement state on outcome $i$ is
$\mathcal I_i(\rho)$, with $\text{Tr}(\mathcal I_i(\rho))=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)=P(i)$
recovering the POVM as the marginal that forgets everything except
outcome statistics.

</DefinitionBox>

A single POVM element $E_i$ is consistent with *infinitely many*
different instruments. Take any rank-1 $E=|v\rangle\langle v|$ (true of
every $E_i$ in this lesson's worked example): for **any** unit vector
$|w\rangle$, $K=|w\rangle\langle v|$ satisfies
$K^\dagger K=|v\rangle\langle w|w\rangle\langle v|=|v\rangle\langle v|=E$,
so *every* choice of $|w\rangle$ gives a valid Kraus operator for the
same $E$, yet each leaves the post-measurement state deterministically
at $|w\rangle$ — a different physical state for each choice, all
completely invisible to the POVM. The concrete version of exactly this,
with real numbers, appears in the worked example.

## Physical Interpretation

A POVM element does not have to correspond to "a question the system
can definitely answer" the way an orthogonal projector does. It only has
to be a legitimate *piece* of a valid probability assignment. That
relaxation is what buys the extra outcomes: a POVM can dedicate an
entire outcome to "I don't know," provided that outcome's operator is
still positive semi-definite and the whole set still sums to $I$ — a
concession an orthogonal projective measurement, capped at $d$ mutually
exclusive outcomes with no slack, cannot make.

## Why This Matters

Without POVMs, several questions this platform will need cannot even be
*stated* rigorously, let alone answered:

- **Optimal state discrimination.** Quantum cryptography's whole
  security analysis (including the eavesdropping arguments behind BB84
  Quantum Key Distribution) hinges on exactly how well an adversary can
  distinguish non-orthogonal signal states. "Optimal" here means
  optimal over *all* valid measurements, and the true optimum, as the
  worked example below shows concretely, is a POVM that no projective
  measurement can match.
- **Weak and continuous measurement.** Real hardware readout is rarely a
  single crisp projection. Circuit QED's dispersive readout (Quantum
  Hardware's Qubit Readout Techniques) extracts partial information
  continuously by coupling a qubit to a resonator; the right mathematical
  object for "a measurement that only partially collapses the state, and
  can be run continuously in time," is a continuous family of POVM
  elements, not a projector.
- **QKD security proofs.** Proving a key-distribution protocol secure
  means bounding what *any* POVM an eavesdropper could build reveals
  about the key — a claim about *all* physically valid measurements,
  which requires the POVM formalism to even quantify over.

### Setting up the three effects

Outcome 1 ("it must be $\psi_1$") must **never** fire on $\psi_2$:
$\langle\psi_2|E_1|\psi_2\rangle=0$ forces
$E_1=\kappa\,|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_2^\perp|$ for
$|\psi_2^\perp\rangle=(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)=(0.8,-0.6)$, the state
orthogonal to $\psi_2$. Symmetrically, outcome 2 must never fire on
$\psi_1$: $E_2=\kappa\,|\psi_1^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_1^\perp|=\kappa|1\rangle\langle1|$.
The inconclusive outcome takes whatever is left: $E_?=I-E_1-E_2$.
Requiring $E_?\geq0$ (the completeness-relation argument above, applied
in reverse) bounds $\kappa$; solving $\det(E_?)=0$ for the boundary case
(the largest $\kappa$ compatible with positivity, hence the smallest
possible inconclusive probability) gives the closed form

$$
\kappa = \frac{1}{1+s} = \frac{1}{1.6} = 0.625
$$

### Outcome probabilities

Given the actual state is $|\psi_1\rangle$:
$P(E_1)=\text{Tr}(E_1\rho_1)={povmProbsPsi1[0].toFixed(3)}$,
$P(E_2)=\text{Tr}(E_2\rho_1)={povmProbsPsi1[1].toFixed(3)}$ (exactly zero
— unambiguous, as designed), and
$P(E_?)=\text{Tr}(E_?\rho_1)={povmProbsPsi1[2].toFixed(3)}$. Given the
actual state is $|\psi_2\rangle$, by the mirrored construction:
$P(E_1)={povmProbsPsi2[0].toFixed(3)}$ (exactly zero),
$P(E_2)={povmProbsPsi2[1].toFixed(3)}$,
$P(E_?)={povmProbsPsi2[2].toFixed(3)}$. In general,
$P(\text{correct})=1-s$ and $P(\text{inconclusive})=s$ — this is the
optimal (Ivanovic-Dieks-Peres) unambiguous discrimination bound: **no**
valid measurement of any kind can unambiguously discriminate
$|\psi_1\rangle,|\psi_2\rangle$ with a smaller inconclusive probability
than $s=|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|$.

### Why no 2-outcome projective measurement can do this at all

A projective measurement on a qubit has at most 2 outcomes, both of
which must fire with certainty (there is no third "inconclusive" bucket
to route probability into: $P_1+P_2=1$ always). For it to be
*unambiguous*, $P_1$ would need $\langle\psi_2|P_1|\psi_2\rangle=0$
(giving $P_1=|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_2^\perp|$, the *only* rank-1
projector annihilating $\psi_2$) and $P_2$ would need
$\langle\psi_1|P_2|\psi_1\rangle=0$ (giving
$P_2=|\psi_1^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_1^\perp|=|1\rangle\langle1|$). But
$P_1+P_2=I$ requires $|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\perp|\psi_1^\perp\rangle$,
i.e. $\langle\psi_2^\perp|\psi_1^\perp\rangle=-\cos\theta=0$ — true only
when $\theta=90°$, i.e. when $\psi_1,\psi_2$ were already orthogonal to
begin with. For this lesson's genuinely non-orthogonal pair
($s=0.6\neq0$), **no** 2-outcome projective measurement can achieve zero
misidentification probability, period; the POVM above is not merely
*better* than projective measurement here, it is doing something
projective measurement cannot do at all. The best a forced-decision
(2-outcome) measurement can do is *minimize error instead of eliminating
it*: Helstrom's formula (Trace Distance and Fidelity) gives minimum error
probability $\tfrac12(1-\sin\theta)={helstromErrorMin.toFixed(3)}$ for
equal priors — a real, nonzero error rate on every single trial, traded
here for this POVM's zero error rate at the cost of a 60% inconclusive
rate.

### Naimark dilation of this exact POVM

Since $E_1,E_2,E_3$ are each rank 1, take $M_i=\sqrt{E_i}$ in row form:
$M_1=\sqrt\kappa\langle\psi_2^\perp|$,
$M_2=\sqrt\kappa\langle\psi_1^\perp|$, $M_3=\sqrt{\kappa_3}\langle\phi|$,
each a $1\times2$ bra with $M_i^\dagger M_i=E_i$ (verified below).
Stacking them gives the $3\times2$ isometry $V$:

<MatrixGrid matrix={isometryV} label="V (stacked M1; M2; M3)" compareTo={isometryCheck} compareLabel="V-dagger V" ariaLabel="The 3 by 2 isometry V built from the three POVM square-root operators, alongside V-dagger V verified equal to the 2x2 identity" />

$V^\dagger V=I_2$ exactly, confirming $V$ is a genuine isometry — this is
the completeness relation $\sum E_i=I$, re-expressed. Padding $V$ with a
fourth all-zero row (the unused, never-occurring fourth outcome) and
completing its two orthonormal columns to a full orthonormal basis of
$\mathbb C^4$ gives an explicit unitary $U$ on system-plus-qubit-ancilla:

<MatrixGrid matrix={naimarkUnitary} label="U (4x4 unitary completion of V)" compareTo={unitarityCheck} compareLabel="U-dagger U" ariaLabel="The 4 by 4 unitary completion of the padded isometry, alongside U-dagger U verified equal to the 4x4 identity" digits={3} />

Applying $U$ to $|\psi\rangle\oplus(0,0)$ (the system state embedded in
the first two of four joint coordinates) and reading off
$|(U\psi)_i|^2$ for $i=1,2,3$ reproduces
$\big({naimarkProbsPsi1[0].toFixed(3)},\ {naimarkProbsPsi1[1].toFixed(3)},\ {naimarkProbsPsi1[2].toFixed(3)}\big)$
for $|\psi_1\rangle$ and
$\big({naimarkProbsPsi2[0].toFixed(3)},\ {naimarkProbsPsi2[1].toFixed(3)},\ {naimarkProbsPsi2[2].toFixed(3)}\big)$
for $|\psi_2\rangle$ — identical, to numerical precision, to the direct
Born-rule numbers $\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ computed above from two completely
independent calculations. The fourth basis outcome never fires for
either state, consistent with this POVM genuinely needing only 3
outcomes. **A qubit-dimensional POVM measurement really is nothing more
than a projective measurement on a system plus one extra qubit.**

$E_1=0.625\,|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_2^\perp|$ fixes

Two different Kraus operators both square to $E_1$:

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming every POVM element must be a projector.** $E_1$ above has
eigenvalues $\{0.625,\,0\}$ — it is $0.625$ times a rank-1 projector, not
a projector itself ($E_1^2=0.625\,E_1\neq E_1$). The only requirements
are $E_i\geq0$ and $\sum_iE_i=I$; idempotence ($E_i^2=E_i$) and mutual
orthogonality are extra properties that happen to hold for *projective*
measurements specifically, not for POVMs in general.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the post-measurement state after outcome $i$ is always
$\sqrt{E_i}\,\rho\,\sqrt{E_i}/\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ or similarly derived
from $E_i$ alone.** The instrument section above builds two different, equally
valid Kraus realizations of the identical $E_1$ that leave the qubit in
two different states. A POVM genuinely underdetermines the
post-measurement state; only a full instrument (a specific choice of
Kraus operators per outcome) fixes it. The "square-root" or Lüders
choice is a common *default*, not a fact forced by the POVM itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **POVM** $\{E_i\}$ generalizes projective measurement to any set of
  positive semi-definite operators summing to the identity; $E_i$ need
  not be orthogonal, and there can be more outcomes than the Hilbert
  space's dimension.
- $\sum_iE_i=I$ is not a convention — it is the exact condition forced
  by requiring $\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ to be a valid probability
  distribution for every physical state, proved the same way Quantum
  Channels: Kraus and Choi proved trace-preservation on the Choi matrix.
- **Naimark's theorem**: every POVM is a projective measurement on a
  larger (system + ancilla) space, constructively proved and verified
  numerically here for a real 3-outcome qubit POVM using only a single
  qubit ancilla.
- The worked unambiguous-discrimination example showed a POVM achieving
  something a projective measurement provably cannot do at all for
  non-orthogonal states: zero misidentification probability.
- A **quantum instrument** (Kraus operators per outcome) is strictly
  more information than the POVM it induces; the POVM is the marginal
  that keeps outcome statistics and forgets the post-measurement state.
