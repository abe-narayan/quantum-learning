## Every Measurement So Far Was a Projector

Every measurement this platform has used so far, from The Measurement
Postulate, Generalized through Quantum Channels: Kraus and Choi's own
Kraus-operator formalism, has been built from **orthogonal projectors**
$\{P_i\}$: $P_iP_j=\delta_{ij}P_i$, $\sum_iP_i=I$, outcome probability
$\text{Tr}(P_i\rho)$. That restriction is quietly limiting in two ways
that turn out to matter a great deal: an orthogonal projective
measurement on a $d$-dimensional system can have **at most $d$
outcomes** (a qubit gets at most 2), and it can never be optimal for
telling apart two *non-orthogonal* quantum states, a problem that shows
up the instant a real device (or an eavesdropper) has to guess which of
several possible signal states it was handed. The strictly more general
object that removes both restrictions is the <Term id="povm">POVM</Term>,
and this lesson proves rather than states why it is the right
generalization.

## Completeness, Naimark, and Instruments

### The definition

<DefinitionBox title="Positive operator-valued measure (POVM)">

A **POVM** is a finite set of operators $\{E_i\}$ on a Hilbert space,
each **positive semi-definite** ($E_i\geq0$, i.e. $\langle\phi|E_i|\phi\rangle\geq0$
for every $|\phi\rangle$) but *not* required to be a projector
($E_i^2\neq E_i$ in general, and the $E_i$ need not be mutually
orthogonal), satisfying the **completeness relation**

$$
\sum_i E_i = I
$$

The outcome probabilities are given by the same Born rule this platform
has used throughout, just with $E_i$ (an "effect") standing in for a
projector: $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$.

</DefinitionBox>

Nothing here says the $E_i$ are orthogonal, and nothing caps the number
of outcomes at the Hilbert space dimension: a qubit can have a
3-outcome, 4-outcome, or arbitrarily-many-outcome POVM, which is the
extra room the worked example below needs.

### Why $\sum_iE_i=I$ is exactly the right condition

This is forced, not an arbitrary normalization convention, by
requiring $\{P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)\}$ to be an honest probability
distribution for *every* physical state $\rho$, in two separate pieces.

**Non-negativity.** $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)\geq0$ must hold for every
density matrix $\rho$. Taking $\rho=|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$ for an
arbitrary $|\phi\rangle$ gives $\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)=\langle\phi|E_i|\phi\rangle$,
so this condition holding for *every* $|\phi\rangle$ is, by definition,
exactly $E_i\geq0$.

**Normalization.** $\sum_iP(i)=1$ must hold for every $\rho$, i.e.
$\text{Tr}\big((\sum_iE_i)\rho\big)=\text{Tr}(\rho)=1$ for every density
matrix. This is the argument Quantum Channels: Kraus and Choi used to
pin down trace-preservation from the Choi matrix: test this identity on
the spanning set of basis matrices $\rho=|j\rangle\langle k|$ (extending
by linearity past just density matrices). Writing $A\equiv\sum_iE_i$,
$\text{Tr}(A|j\rangle\langle k|)=A_{kj}$ must equal
$\text{Tr}(|j\rangle\langle k|)=\delta_{jk}$ for every $j,k$, which
forces $A=I$ entry by entry. So $\sum_iE_i=I$ is not merely one
*sufficient* way to get a valid distribution. It is the unique
*necessary* condition, by the same reasoning this course already trusted
for a different completeness relation.

### Naimark's dilation theorem

Every POVM turns out to be a projective measurement in disguise, provided
you are willing to enlarge the system. That is
<Term id="naimark-dilation-theorem">Naimark's theorem</Term>, and it is
what keeps the POVM formalism from being a separate postulate: it is the
old postulate, read on a bigger space.

<TheoremBox title="Naimark's dilation theorem" provenance="derived">

**Hypotheses.** $A$ is a $d$-dimensional Hilbert space with $d$ finite,
and $\{E_i\}_{i=1}^n$ is a POVM on it with **finitely many** outcomes.
Nothing is assumed about the $E_i$ beyond positivity and completeness;
in particular they need not be projectors, need not commute, and $n$ may
exceed $d$.

**Conclusion.** Adjoin an ancilla of dimension $d_{\text{anc}}$ with
$n\leq d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}$. There is a unitary $U$ on the joint
$(d\cdot d_{\text{anc}})$-dimensional space and a fixed orthonormal basis
of it such that projectively measuring $U(|\psi\rangle\otimes|0\rangle)$
in that basis reproduces $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ exactly, for every
input state.

Both finiteness hypotheses matter. The proof extends an isometry by
completing an orthonormal basis, which is a finite-dimensional move; the
general statement for continuous-outcome POVMs on infinite-dimensional
spaces is still true but is a theorem about operator-valued measures and
needs different machinery. Naimark (1940); see also Paulsen,
*Completely Bounded Maps and Operator Algebras*, ch. 4.

</TheoremBox>

**Proof.** Since each $E_i\geq0$, it has a positive semi-definite square
root; more generally pick *any* $M_i$ with $M_i^\dagger M_i=E_i$ (the
canonical choice $M_i=\sqrt{E_i}$ always works, by the spectral theorem
already used throughout this course). If $A$ is $d$-dimensional, each
$M_i$ can be taken $1\times d$ (a bra) when $E_i$ has rank 1, or more
generally $\text{rank}(E_i)\times d$; stack all the $M_i$ row-by-row
into a single $n\times d$ matrix $V$ (padding with extra rows of $M_i$
in the rank-$>1$ case, or zero rows to reach a convenient total
dimension). Then

$$
V^\dagger V = \sum_i M_i^\dagger M_i = \sum_i E_i = I_d
$$

so $V$ is an **isometry**: its $d$ columns are orthonormal vectors in
$\mathbb C^n$. Any isometry extends to a full unitary $U$ on
$\mathbb C^n$ by completing those $d$ orthonormal columns to a full
orthonormal basis of $\mathbb C^n$ (Gram-Schmidt on the orthogonal
complement of $V$'s range). This is the same "extend to a full basis"
move the SVD-based Schmidt decomposition proof already used, run here on
an isometry instead of a single vector. If $n\leq d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}$,
embed $\mathbb C^n$ into $\mathbb C^d\otimes\mathbb C^{d_{\text{anc}}}$
(padding with zero rows for the unused basis vectors) so $U$ is a
unitary on system-plus-ancilla. For any state $|\psi\rangle$ of
$A$,

$$
P(\text{basis outcome }i) = \big|\langle e_i|U|\psi\rangle\big|^2 = \big|(V\psi)_i\big|^2 = \langle\psi|M_i^\dagger M_i|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|E_i|\psi\rangle = \text{Tr}(E_i\rho)
$$

reproducing the POVM's statistics from an ordinary projective
measurement on the enlarged space. $\blacksquare$

A qubit ($d=2$) with $n=3$ outcomes needs $d\cdot d_{\text{anc}}\geq3$;
a trivial "ancilla" of dimension 1 gives only $2<3$, so *some* ancilla is
required, and a single **qubit** ancilla ($d_{\text{anc}}=2$,
joint dimension 4) is already enough room. The worked example below
builds this $n=3$, $d=2$, $d_{\text{anc}}=2$ case explicitly, with every
matrix multiplied out and checked.

### Quantum instruments: what a POVM leaves out

A POVM fixes $P(i)=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$, but says nothing about what
state the system is left in *given* outcome $i$ occurred, and that
post-measurement state is not determined by $E_i$ alone.

<DefinitionBox title="Quantum instrument">

An **instrument** is a collection of completely positive maps
$\{\mathcal I_i\}$, each with a Kraus form
$\mathcal I_i(\rho)=\sum_kK_{i,k}\rho K_{i,k}^\dagger$ (Quantum
Channels: Kraus and Choi's own Kraus operators, one Kraus family per
outcome), such that $\sum_i\mathcal I_i$ is a full CPTP channel. The
associated POVM element is $E_i=\sum_kK_{i,k}^\dagger K_{i,k}$, and the
(unnormalized) post-measurement state on outcome $i$ is
$\mathcal I_i(\rho)$, with $\text{Tr}(\mathcal I_i(\rho))=\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)=P(i)$
recovering the POVM as the marginal that forgets everything except
outcome statistics.

</DefinitionBox>

A single POVM element $E_i$ is consistent with *infinitely many*
different <Term id="quantum-instrument">instruments</Term>. Take any rank-1 $E=|v\rangle\langle v|$ (true of
every $E_i$ in this lesson's worked example): for **any** unit vector
$|w\rangle$, $K=|w\rangle\langle v|$ satisfies
$K^\dagger K=|v\rangle\langle w|w\rangle\langle v|=|v\rangle\langle v|=E$,
so *every* choice of $|w\rangle$ gives a valid Kraus operator for the
same $E$, yet each leaves the post-measurement state deterministically
at $|w\rangle$, a different physical state for each choice, all
completely invisible to the POVM. A concrete version of this, with real
numbers, appears in the worked example.

## An Effect Need Not Answer a Question

A POVM element does not have to correspond to "a question the system
can definitely answer" the way an orthogonal projector does. It only has
to be a legitimate *piece* of a valid probability assignment. That
relaxation is what buys the extra outcomes: a POVM can dedicate an
entire outcome to "I don't know," provided that outcome's operator is
still positive semi-definite and the whole set still sums to $I$, a
concession an orthogonal projective measurement, capped at $d$ mutually
exclusive outcomes with no slack, cannot make.

## Questions That Cannot Otherwise Be Posed

Without POVMs, several questions this platform will need cannot even be
*stated* rigorously, let alone answered:

- **Optimal state discrimination.** Quantum cryptography's whole
  security analysis (including the eavesdropping arguments behind BB84
  Quantum Key Distribution) hinges on exactly how well an adversary can
  distinguish non-orthogonal signal states. "Optimal" here means
  optimal over *all* valid measurements, and the true optimum, as the
  worked example below shows concretely, is a POVM that no projective
  measurement can match.
- **Weak and continuous measurement.** Real hardware readout is rarely a
  single crisp projection. Circuit QED's dispersive readout (Quantum
  Hardware's Qubit Readout Techniques) extracts partial information
  continuously by coupling a qubit to a resonator; the right mathematical
  object for "a measurement that only partially collapses the state, and
  can be run continuously in time," is a continuous family of POVM
  elements, not a projector.
- **QKD security proofs.** Proving a key-distribution protocol secure
  means bounding what *any* POVM an eavesdropper could build reveals
  about the key. That is a claim about *all* physically valid
  measurements, and the POVM formalism is what makes quantifying over
  them expressible at all.

## Unambiguous Discrimination, Built Explicitly

$|\psi_2\rangle=0.6|0\rangle+0.8|1\rangle$ have overlap
$s=\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle=0.6$ (relative angle
$\theta=\arccos(0.6)\approx53.13°$). Find the 3-outcome POVM that
unambiguously discriminates them (zero probability of ever
*misidentifying* one as the other) with the smallest possible
inconclusive rate, then settle what a 2-outcome projective measurement

### Setting up the three effects

Outcome 1 ("it must be $\psi_1$") must **never** fire on $\psi_2$:
$\langle\psi_2|E_1|\psi_2\rangle=0$ forces
$E_1=\kappa\,|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_2^\perp|$ for
$|\psi_2^\perp\rangle=(\sin\theta,-\cos\theta)=(0.8,-0.6)$, the state
orthogonal to $\psi_2$. Symmetrically, outcome 2 must never fire on
$\psi_1$: $E_2=\kappa\,|\psi_1^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_1^\perp|=\kappa|1\rangle\langle1|$.
The inconclusive outcome takes whatever is left: $E_?=I-E_1-E_2$.
Requiring $E_?\geq0$ (the completeness-relation argument above, applied
in reverse) bounds $\kappa$; solving $\det(E_?)=0$ for the boundary case
(the largest $\kappa$ compatible with positivity, hence the smallest
possible inconclusive probability) gives the closed form

$$
\kappa = \frac{1}{1+s} = \frac{1}{1.6} = 0.625
$$

### Outcome probabilities

Given the actual state is $|\psi_1\rangle$:
$P(E_1)=\text{Tr}(E_1\rho_1)=$ {povmProbsPsi1[0].toFixed(3)},
$P(E_2)=\text{Tr}(E_2\rho_1)=$ {povmProbsPsi1[1].toFixed(3)} (exactly zero,
unambiguous as designed), and
$P(E_?)=\text{Tr}(E_?\rho_1)=$ {povmProbsPsi1[2].toFixed(3)}. Given the
actual state is $|\psi_2\rangle$, by the mirrored construction:
$P(E_1)=$ {povmProbsPsi2[0].toFixed(3)} (exactly zero),
$P(E_2)=$ {povmProbsPsi2[1].toFixed(3)},
$P(E_?)=$ {povmProbsPsi2[2].toFixed(3)}. In general,
$P(\text{correct})=1-s$ and $P(\text{inconclusive})=s$, which is the
optimal **Ivanovic–Dieks–Peres** unambiguous-discrimination bound: for
two *pure* states supplied with *equal* prior probabilities, no valid
measurement of any kind can unambiguously discriminate
$|\psi_1\rangle,|\psi_2\rangle$ with inconclusive probability below
$s=|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|$.

Both of those hypotheses carry weight. With unequal priors
$\eta_1,\eta_2$ the floor drops to $2\sqrt{\eta_1\eta_2}\,s$, so an
adversary who knows the two signals are unevenly likely does strictly
better than this bound suggests. And for two *mixed* states whose
supports overlap, unambiguous discrimination is impossible at any
inconclusive rate whatsoever: no effect can annihilate one state without
annihilating part of the other. Purity is what makes a zero-error
guarantee available in the first place.

### Why no 2-outcome projective measurement can do this at all

A projective measurement on a qubit has at most 2 outcomes, both of
which must fire with certainty (there is no third "inconclusive" bucket
to route probability into: $P_1+P_2=1$ always). For it to be
*unambiguous*, $P_1$ would need $\langle\psi_2|P_1|\psi_2\rangle=0$
(giving $P_1=|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_2^\perp|$, the *only* rank-1
projector annihilating $\psi_2$) and $P_2$ would need
$\langle\psi_1|P_2|\psi_1\rangle=0$ (giving
$P_2=|\psi_1^\perp\rangle\langle\psi_1^\perp|=|1\rangle\langle1|$). But
$P_1+P_2=I$ requires $|\psi_2^\perp\rangle\perp|\psi_1^\perp\rangle$,
i.e. $\langle\psi_2^\perp|\psi_1^\perp\rangle=-\cos\theta=0$, true only
when $\theta=90°$, i.e. when $\psi_1,\psi_2$ were already orthogonal to
begin with. For this lesson's non-orthogonal pair
($s=0.6\neq0$), **no** 2-outcome projective measurement can achieve zero
misidentification probability, period. The POVM above is not merely
*better* than projective measurement here; it is doing something
projective measurement cannot do at all. The best a forced-decision
(2-outcome) measurement can do is *minimize error instead of eliminating
it*: Helstrom's formula (Trace Distance and Fidelity) gives minimum error
probability $\tfrac12(1-\sin\theta)=$ {helstromErrorMin.toFixed(3)} for
equal priors, a nonzero error rate on every single trial, traded
here for this POVM's zero error rate at the cost of a 60% inconclusive
rate.

$M_1=\sqrt\kappa\langle\psi_2^\perp|$,
$M_2=\sqrt\kappa\langle\psi_1^\perp|$, $M_3=\sqrt{\kappa_3}\langle\phi|$,
each a $1\times2$ bra with $M_i^\dagger M_i=E_i$ (verified below).
Stacking them gives the $3\times2$ isometry $V$:

## Mistaking an Effect for a Projector

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming every POVM element must be a projector.** $E_1$ above has
eigenvalues $\{0.625,\,0\}$: it is $0.625$ times a rank-1 projector, not
a projector itself ($E_1^2=0.625\,E_1\neq E_1$). The only requirements
are $E_i\geq0$ and $\sum_iE_i=I$; idempotence ($E_i^2=E_i$) and mutual
orthogonality are extra properties that happen to hold for *projective*
measurements specifically, not for POVMs in general.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the post-measurement state after outcome $i$ is always
$\sqrt{E_i}\,\rho\,\sqrt{E_i}/\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ or similarly derived
from $E_i$ alone.** The instrument section above builds two different, equally
valid Kraus realizations of the identical $E_1$ that leave the qubit in
two different states. A POVM underdetermines the post-measurement state;
only a full instrument (a specific choice of Kraus operators per outcome)
fixes it. The "square-root" or Lüders
choice is a common *default*, not a fact forced by the POVM itself.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Why does $\sum_iE_i=I$ have to hold for *every* density matrix
   $\rho$ simultaneously, rather than just for one particular state of
   interest?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a measurement device is a fixed piece of apparatus and the
   state is not its input parameter. The $E_i$ are chosen once; whatever
   arrives at the detector has to come out with a valid probability
   distribution.

   The mathematical consequence of dropping the quantifier is severe. With
   $A\equiv\sum_iE_i$, demanding $\mathrm{Tr}(A\rho_0)=1$ for a single
   $\rho_0$ is *one scalar equation* on a matrix with $d^2$ real degrees of
   freedom, and it is satisfied by a huge family of wrong answers. Take
   $A=I+X$ and $\rho_0=|0\rangle\langle0|$:

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}\big((I+X)|0\rangle\langle0|\big)=1+\langle0|X|0\rangle=1+0=1,
   $$

   which passes. Now hand the same device $|{+}\rangle$:

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}\big((I+X)|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|\big)=1+\langle{+}|X|{+}\rangle=1+1=2.
   $$

   The outcome probabilities sum to two. Nothing was wrong with the
   algebra; the calibration was simply done against one state and the
   device was then used on another.

   Quantifying over every $\rho$ is what promotes that one equation to the
   $d^2$ equations $A=I$, and the lesson's proof is exactly that
   promotion: test on the spanning set $\rho=|j\rangle\langle k|$ and read
   off $A_{kj}=\delta_{jk}$ entry by entry. Note that "every density
   matrix" already suffices even though the argument uses non-positive
   basis matrices, because density matrices span the whole space of
   Hermitian operators and $A$ is determined by linearity from any
   spanning set.

   The physical reasons the quantifier is not paranoia are worth listing,
   since a reader can reasonably ask who would ever feed the device an
   unexpected state. A drifting source prepares something slightly off
   target. A channel between preparation and detector alters what arrives.
   And most sharply: if the measured qubit is entangled with anything at
   all, its reduced state is not the one you prepared and can be any state
   in the ball. A completeness relation that holds only on one state is a
   device that reports nonsense the first time it is used inside a larger
   experiment.

   </details>

2. If $|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|=0$ (already orthogonal), what does
   the optimal unambiguous-discrimination POVM above reduce to, and why
   does that match ordinary projective measurement?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It collapses to the ordinary projective measurement in the
   $\{|\psi_1\rangle,|\psi_2\rangle\}$ basis, with the inconclusive
   outcome carrying exactly zero weight.

   Set $s=0$ in the closed form. The scaling becomes
   $\kappa=1/(1+s)=1$, and orthogonality means
   $|\psi_2^\perp\rangle=|\psi_1\rangle$ and
   $|\psi_1^\perp\rangle=|\psi_2\rangle$, so

   $$
   E_1=|\psi_1\rangle\langle\psi_1|,\qquad E_2=|\psi_2\rangle\langle\psi_2|,
   $$

   $$
   E_?=I-|\psi_1\rangle\langle\psi_1|-|\psi_2\rangle\langle\psi_2|=0,
   $$

   the last step because two orthonormal vectors in a two-dimensional space
   are a complete basis, so their projectors already sum to the identity.
   An outcome with $E_?=0$ has probability $\mathrm{Tr}(0\cdot\rho)=0$ on
   every state: it exists on paper and never fires.

   What remains is two rank-1 orthogonal projectors summing to $I$, which
   is the definition of a projective measurement. Its behaviour matches the
   general formulas at $s=0$ on every count: $P(\text{correct})=1-s=1$ and
   $P(\text{inconclusive})=s=0$, so the measurement identifies the state
   with certainty and never hedges.

   Three other results in this course agree at the same point, which is
   the check worth doing. The Helstrom minimum error
   $\tfrac12(1-\sin\theta)$ is zero at $\theta=90°$. The trace distance
   between two orthogonal pure states is $\sqrt{1-s^2}=1$, its maximum, so
   the optimal single-shot success probability $\tfrac12(1+D)$ is $1$. And
   the impossibility argument in the lesson, that unambiguous projective
   discrimination requires $\langle\psi_2^\perp|\psi_1^\perp\rangle=0$,
   holds precisely at $\theta=90°$, which is this case.

   The useful way to read all of that: the POVM is not a different
   *kind* of thing from a projective measurement, it is a one-parameter
   family that contains projective measurement as its $s=0$ member. The
   third outcome's weight $\mathrm{Tr}(E_?)=2s/(1+s)$ grows from zero
   exactly as the two states start to overlap. Non-orthogonality is what
   creates the need for "I cannot tell", and the amount of it is set by
   how much overlap there is.

   </details>

3. A proposed 2-outcome measurement uses $E_1=0.7|0\rangle\langle0|$,
   $E_2=I-E_1$. Is $\{E_1,E_2\}$ a valid POVM? Check both required
   conditions explicitly.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Yes, valid, and both conditions have to be checked because the second
   one is not implied by the first.

   **Completeness.** $E_1+E_2=E_1+(I-E_1)=I$. This holds by construction
   and is the easy half; defining the last effect as "whatever is left"
   always satisfies it.

   **Positivity.** In the computational basis,

   $$
   E_1=\begin{pmatrix}0.7&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
   E_2=I-E_1=\begin{pmatrix}0.3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
   $$

   Both are diagonal, so their eigenvalues are their diagonal entries:
   $\{0.7,0\}$ and $\{0.3,1\}$. All are non-negative, so
   $E_1,E_2\geq0$ and the POVM is valid.

   The reason this is a real check and not a formality is that
   completeness alone guarantees nothing about the leftover. Replace the
   coefficient $0.7$ with $1.3$ and completeness still holds by
   construction, but $E_2=\mathrm{diag}(-0.3,1)$ has a negative eigenvalue,
   so $\mathrm{Tr}(E_2\rho)<0$ for $\rho=|0\rangle\langle0|$: a negative
   probability. That is exactly the failure mode positivity exists to
   exclude, and it is invisible if you only sum the effects.

   It is worth naming what this POVM *is*, since it is a clean example of
   something projective measurement cannot express. $E_1^2=0.49|0\rangle\langle0|\neq E_1$,
   so $E_1$ is not a projector and this is not a projective measurement in
   disguise. It models a detector that fires on $|0\rangle$ only $70\%$ of
   the time and never on $|1\rangle$: outcome $1$ means "definitely was
   $|0\rangle$", while outcome $2$ is inconclusive, since it occurs with
   probability $0.3$ on $|0\rangle$ and $1$ on $|1\rangle$. Explicitly,
   $P(1)=0.7\rho_{00}$ and $P(2)=1-0.7\rho_{00}$. An inefficient detector
   is a POVM, not a projective measurement with noise bolted on, which is
   one of the main practical reasons the formalism is needed at all.

   </details>

4. Explain, in one or two sentences, why a POVM's outcome statistics
   alone can never tell you whether your hardware is implementing the
   Lüders instrument or some other instrument consistent with the same
   POVM.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the outcome distribution depends on the instrument only through
   $E_i=\sum_kK_{i,k}^\dagger K_{i,k}$, and that map from instruments to
   POVMs is many-to-one: replacing every $K_{i,k}$ by $W_iK_{i,k}$ for any
   unitary $W_i$ leaves $E_i$ untouched, since
   $K^\dagger W^\dagger WK=K^\dagger K$, while sending the post-measurement
   state $\mathcal I_i(\rho)$ to $W_i\mathcal I_i(\rho)W_i^\dagger$.

   So the difference is real and simply not recorded in the number the
   detector prints. The lesson's rank-1 case is the extreme version: with
   $E=|v\rangle\langle v|$, every $K=|w\rangle\langle v|$ reproduces the
   same $E$ while leaving the system deterministically in $|w\rangle$, so
   two devices with identical statistics can be leaving the qubit in
   orthogonal states.

   The constructive half is the part to act on. The only way to tell them
   apart is to *use* the post-measurement state: measure again in a basis
   sensitive to $W_i$, or run the output through more circuit and look at
   what comes out. Process tomography on the instrument does exactly this,
   and it is strictly more work than tomography on the POVM, because it has
   to probe a larger object.

   </details>

## Summary

- A **POVM** $\{E_i\}$ generalizes projective measurement to any set of
  positive semi-definite operators summing to the identity; $E_i$ need
  not be orthogonal, and there can be more outcomes than the Hilbert
  space's dimension.
- $\sum_iE_i=I$ is not a convention. It is the exact condition forced
  by requiring $\text{Tr}(E_i\rho)$ to be a valid probability
  distribution for every physical state, proved the same way Quantum
  Channels: Kraus and Choi proved trace-preservation on the Choi matrix.
- **Naimark's theorem**: every POVM is a projective measurement on a
  larger (system + ancilla) space, constructively proved and verified
  numerically here for a real 3-outcome qubit POVM using only a single
  qubit ancilla.
- The worked unambiguous-discrimination example showed a POVM achieving
  something a projective measurement provably cannot do at all for
  non-orthogonal states: zero misidentification probability.
- A **quantum instrument** (Kraus operators per outcome) is strictly
  more information than the POVM it induces; the POVM is the marginal
  that keeps outcome statistics and forgets the post-measurement state.
