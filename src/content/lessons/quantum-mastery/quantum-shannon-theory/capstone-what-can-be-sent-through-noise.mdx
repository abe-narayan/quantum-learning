export const rho1 = pureStateDensityMatrix(oneState);

export const depolarizingChannel = (rho, p) =>
  convexCombination([
    { probability: 1 - p, density: rho },
    { probability: p, density: maximallyMixedState(2) },
  ]);

export const holevoChi = (rhoA, rhoB, p) => {
  const outA = depolarizingChannel(rhoA, p);
  const outB = depolarizingChannel(rhoB, p);
  const avg = convexCombination([
    { probability: 0.5, density: outA },
    { probability: 0.5, density: outB },
  ]);
  return vonNeumannEntropy(avg) - 0.5 * vonNeumannEntropy(outA) - 0.5 * vonNeumannEntropy(outB);
};

  const q = (3 * p) / 4;

  let lo = 0;
  let hi = 1;
  for (let i = 0; i < 60; i++) {
    const mid = (lo + hi) / 2;
    if (hashingBoundQ(mid) > 0) {
      lo = mid;
    } else {
      hi = mid;
    }
  }
  return lo;
})();

## Six Modules, One Vocabulary

Six modules in this course built one continuous vocabulary. POVMs and
Naimark's theorem showed every generalized measurement is a projective
one on a larger space. Stinespring dilation showed every physical
channel is secretly a unitary on system-plus-environment. Von Neumann
entropy, quantum mutual information, and conditional entropy (which,
unlike its classical counterpart, can go *negative*) gave the
vocabulary to quantify uncertainty and correlation. The data-processing
inequality supplied the real content behind "a channel can't manufacture
information," bounding what any physical process can do to correlations
it didn't have a hand in creating. Entanglement distillation gave
entanglement itself an operational price tag: how many near-perfect
Bell pairs a noisy supply of them is worth. Every one of those
results was a statement about a single quantum system or a single use
of a resource. This capstone asks the question that was always waiting
underneath all of them, and names the two resulting numbers the
channel's <Term id="quantum-channel-capacity">**classical capacity** and **quantum capacity**</Term>.

## Holevo, HSW, and LSD

### The Holevo quantity and Holevo's theorem

<DefinitionBox title="Holevo quantity">

For an ensemble $\{p_i,\rho_i\}$ of quantum states prepared with
probabilities $p_i$, the **Holevo quantity** is

$$
\chi(\{p_i,\rho_i\}) \;=\; S\!\left(\sum_i p_i\rho_i\right) - \sum_i p_i\,S(\rho_i)
$$

the entropy of the average state minus the average entropy of the
individual states.

</DefinitionBox>

<TheoremBox title="Holevo's theorem" provenance="cited">

For any measurement (any POVM) performed on an ensemble
$\{p_i,\rho_i\}$, the classical mutual information between the
preparation label $i$ and the measurement outcome is at most
$\chi(\{p_i,\rho_i\})$. This predates and stands independently of the
full channel-capacity theory below: it is a statement about *one*
ensemble and *any* single measurement on it, not yet about repeated
channel uses at all.

</TheoremBox>

$\chi\geq 0$ always (a direct consequence of the concavity of von
Neumann entropy, a standard property cited rather than proved anywhere in
this track), and $\chi=0$ exactly when every $\rho_i$ is identical, the
case in which no label-dependent signal survives at all.

### The classical capacity: HSW

Given a channel $\mathcal N$, define $\chi^*(\mathcal N)=\max_{\{p_i,\rho_i\}}\chi(\{p_i,\mathcal N(\rho_i)\})$,
the largest Holevo quantity achievable by any input ensemble sent
through $\mathcal N$ once.

<TheoremBox title="Holevo-Schumacher-Westmoreland theorem" provenance="cited">

**Hypotheses.** $\mathcal N$ is CPTP on a finite-dimensional system. The
encoder may prepare *any* state of $n$ inputs, entangled across channel
uses included, and the decoder may perform *any* joint POVM on all $n$
outputs at once. Those two freedoms are why the limit below
cannot be dropped: restricting to product inputs and per-use
measurements gives a strictly smaller quantity for some channels.

The classical capacity of $\mathcal N$, the number of classical bits
per channel use reliably transmittable in the limit of arbitrarily many
uses with vanishing error, is

$$
C(\mathcal N) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\,\chi^*(\mathcal N^{\otimes n})
$$

</TheoremBox>

This is subtle: $\chi^*$ is **not** always additive
($\chi^*(\mathcal N\otimes\mathcal M)$ is not always
$\chi^*(\mathcal N)+\chi^*(\mathcal M)$ for arbitrary channels), so in
general the formula above needs this *regularization*, optimizing over
encodings that entangle inputs across many channel uses, and no general
closed form for $C(\mathcal N)$ is known. Whether $\chi^*$ is additive
for *every* channel was a famous open conjecture until Hastings
constructed an explicit counterexample in 2009 disproving it in general.
For specific, highly symmetric channels, including the depolarizing and
dephasing channels this lesson uses, $\chi^*$ **is** known to be
additive (a real theorem, not an assumption), so $C(\mathcal N)=\chi^*(\mathcal N)$
single-letter, with no regularization needed. That special-case
cleanliness is what makes the worked example below an honest,
checkable computation rather than a hand-wave.

### The quantum capacity: LSD

The quantum analogue replaces the Holevo quantity with the **coherent
information**, first defined in Quantum Entropy and Information Measures
and extended here to channels:

$$
I_c(\rho,\mathcal N) \;=\; S(\mathcal N(\rho)) - S_e(\rho,\mathcal N)
$$

where $S_e$ is the entropy of the channel's *environment* after the
interaction (the entropy exchange). This matches that earlier formula
exactly, not just in spirit: purify the channel's action by coupling
input to environment via a reference system $A$, so the joint
reference-output-environment state is pure. In a pure global state, a
subsystem and its complement always share the same entropy, so
$S(\rho_{AB}) = S_e$. That gives $I_c(\rho,\mathcal N) = S(\rho_B) -
S(\rho_{AB}) = -S(A|B)$, that lesson's
coherent information, just with $S_e$ standing in for $S(\rho_{AB})$
because the environment is easier to reason about for a channel than an
explicit reference system. Unlike $\chi$, $I_c$ can be negative, itself
a real and meaningful fact from that earlier lesson.

<TheoremBox title="Lloyd-Shor-Devetak (LSD) theorem" provenance="cited">

The quantum capacity of $\mathcal N$, the number of qubits per channel
use reliably transmittable (equivalently, the rate at which Bell pairs
can be generated) in the many-uses limit, is

$$
Q(\mathcal N) \;=\; \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\max_{\rho}I_c\!\left(\rho,\mathcal N^{\otimes n}\right)
$$

</TheoremBox>

This regularization is not a technicality that specific theorems quietly
resolve the way HSW's does for depolarizing and dephasing channels: for
most channels, including the depolarizing channel used throughout this
lesson, whether the single-letter coherent information already equals
$Q(\mathcal N)$ is an open question. Some special channel
classes (**degradable** channels, where the environment's state is
recoverable from the output) are proven single-letter; the depolarizing
channel is not known to be degradable, so its exact quantum capacity is
not known in closed form at all, only bounded. Computing exact channel
capacities in general is an actively researched, unresolved problem in
quantum Shannon theory, not a solved textbook exercise, and this
capstone is an honest place to say so plainly rather than paper over it.

## The Question the Whole Course Was Building Toward

This is, in exact technical language, the question "how much can I
trust this noisy qubit, fiber link, or gate to carry information
reliably." $Q(\mathcal N)=0$ is not "error correction for this channel
is hard": it is a proof that **no quantum error-correcting code, at any
rate, run for any number of uses, can protect quantum information sent
through $\mathcal N$**. The quantum analogue of the classical
noisy-channel coding theorem says so directly. $Q(\mathcal N)>0$ is the
converse promise: a good enough code exists in principle, even if
nobody has built it yet. Every real hardware platform's fight against
decoherence, and every quantum-repeater design for long-distance
entanglement distribution, is a fight to keep some real physical
channel's $Q$ strictly positive, phrased in this vocabulary
rather than only in a $T_1$/$T_2$ number. The worked example below shows
why that fight is so much harder for quantum information than for
classical information sent down the identical physical wire.

## Both Capacities, Computed on One Channel

**Send the BB84-style ensemble $\{\tfrac12,|0\rangle\langle0|;\ \tfrac12,|+\rangle\langle+|\}$
(the same two conjugate-basis states BB84: Quantum Key Distribution
encodes bit $0$ in, one per basis) through the depolarizing channel
$\mathcal N_p(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\,I/2$, compute the exact Holevo quantity
$\chi(p)$, then find where the channel's quantum capacity hits
zero.**

### The classical side: $\chi(p)$ for a non-orthogonal ensemble

`vonNeumannEntropy`, `convexCombination`, and `maximallyMixedState`, all
this platform's real density-matrix engine, give directly:

$\chi(0) =$ {chiAt0.toFixed(5)} bits.

Notice this is **not** $1$ bit, even at zero noise. An *orthogonal*
ensemble like $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ does reach exactly the
noiseless maximum, $\chi(0)=$ {orthogonalChiAt0.toFixed(0)} bit, computed
the identical way; BB84's whole security argument rests on using
$|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$ because they are **not**
orthogonal ($|\langle0|+\rangle|^2=\tfrac12$), which is why
$\chi(0)\approx0.601$ here, strictly less than $1$. Holevo's bound is
already doing real work on this ensemble before any channel noise is
even added.

Turning on the channel:

$\chi(0.2)=$ {chiAt02.toFixed(5)}, $\chi(0.5)=$ {chiAt05.toFixed(5)},
$\chi(1)=$ {chiAt1.toFixed(5)}.

$\chi(p)\to0$ exactly at $p=1$: full depolarization erases every trace
of which state was sent, so no measurement can recover any information,
matching Holevo's theorem at its own boundary case.

This $\chi(p)$ is one specific ensemble's number, an honest, real
computation, but by HSW's proven additivity for the depolarizing
channel, the *actual* classical capacity $C(p)=\chi^*(p)$ is achieved
by an **orthogonal** ensemble instead (a cited symmetry result, not
re-derived here): $C(p)=1-S(\mathcal N_p(|0\rangle\langle0|))$, giving
$C(0.2)=$ {capacityAt02.toFixed(5)}, $C(0.5)=$ {capacityAt05.toFixed(5)},
$C(0.8)=$ {capacityAt08.toFixed(5)}, strictly positive for every
$p<1$, hitting exactly zero only at full depolarization $p=1$.

### The quantum side: where $Q(p)$ dies

Two different, both rigorous, facts about this same channel's
quantum capacity:

**A certified lower bound.** Feeding the maximally mixed state into
$\mathcal N_p$ and computing its coherent information gives the
**hashing bound**, the rate a one-way entanglement-distillation
protocol (this course's own previous module) can certify:

$$
Q(p)\;\geq\;1-H_2\!\left(\tfrac{3p}{4}\right)-\tfrac{3p}{4}\log_2 3
$$

a closed-form quantity (not a fit or an approximation),
using $\mathcal N_p(I/2)=I/2$ exactly and the fact that, for a Pauli
channel acting on the maximally mixed input, the environment ends up in
a classical mixture over "which error happened," diagonal with exactly
the Kraus probabilities. This bound is positive only for
$p\lesssim$ {hashingZeroCrossing.toFixed(4)}, and strictly negative (hence
uninformative, clamped to $0$ in the figure below) beyond that.

**An exact zero.** Separately, and completely rigorously: a channel is
called **entanglement-breaking** if sending half of a maximally
entangled pair through it always leaves a *separable* (unentangled)
output, and every entanglement-breaking channel has $Q(\mathcal N)=0$
exactly, since no entanglement, hence no quantum information, survives
even in principle. Building the Choi-like state
$(1-p)|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|+p\,I_4/4$ with this platform's real
`convexCombination` and `maximallyMixedState(4)`, then taking the
partial transpose over the channel's output qubit, gives (by the
Peres-Horodecki criterion, exact for two qubits) eigenvalues
$\tfrac12-\tfrac p4$ (three-fold) and $\tfrac{3p}4-\tfrac12$ (the
singlet direction). The singlet eigenvalue is the only candidate for
negativity, and it is computed directly here via
`densityMatrixExpectationValue` against the exact $|\Psi^-\rangle$
projector.

At $p=0.5$ the singlet eigenvalue is
{singletEigenvalueBelowThreshold.toFixed(5)}: negative, so the state is
still entangled and the channel is not yet entanglement-breaking. At
$p=\tfrac23$ it is {singletEigenvalueAtThreshold.toFixed(5)}, exactly
zero, which is the threshold.

Past that threshold the eigenvalue turns positive, the Choi state is PPT
and therefore (for two qubits) separable, so $Q(p)=0$ **exactly and
provably** for every
$p\geq\tfrac23\approx$ {entanglementBreakingThreshold.toFixed(4)}.

Put the two facts side by side: the certified achievable rate already
hits $0$ by $p\approx0.25$, while classical capacity $C(p)$ computed
above is still comfortably positive there ($C(0.2)\approx0.531$ bits,
nowhere near zero). Between $p\approx0.25$ and $p=\tfrac23$, the *exact*
value of $Q(p)$ is an open computation: known to be $\geq0$
trivially and proven exactly $0$ only once entanglement-breaking sets
in at $p=\tfrac23$. Nobody currently knows the exact depolarizing-channel
quantum capacity curve in that range. That is an honest, unresolved
research question, not a hole in this lesson's derivation.

## What a Capacity Bound Does Not Say

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating $\chi(\{p_i,\rho_i\})$ itself as "the capacity."** $\chi$ is
an upper bound on what *any single measurement* on *one* copy of the
ensemble can extract (Holevo's theorem); the *channel's* classical
capacity is the maximum of $\chi$ over input ensembles, in the
many-uses limit, and HSW is the (nontrivial) theorem that this
maximum is achievable, not merely an upper bound on it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming every channel's capacity formula is single-letter, since the
depolarizing and dephasing examples in this lesson are.** Both HSW's
$\chi^*$ and LSD's coherent information can require
regularization for general channels; additivity failing in general
(proven by Hastings for $\chi^*$) is why this lesson's clean
closed forms are special-case facts about *these particular* channels'
symmetry, not evidence that every channel behaves this simply.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Reading "the hashing bound is negative here" as "quantum capacity is
zero here."** A negative or clamped-to-zero lower bound only means
*that specific protocol* certifies nothing; it does not prove the true
$Q(p)$ is zero. Only the separate entanglement-breaking argument above
proves an exact zero, and only from $p=\tfrac23$ onward.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using this lesson's exact numbers, explain in your own words why
   $\chi(0)\approx0.601$ bits for the BB84 ensemble $\{|0\rangle,|+\rangle\}$
   rather than the full $1$ bit an orthogonal ensemble achieves, tying
   the answer directly to $|\langle0|+\rangle|^2=\tfrac12$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\chi$ measures how much the *average* state
   $\bar\rho=\tfrac12(|0\rangle\langle0|+|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|)$ knows
   about which member was sent, and two states that overlap produce an
   average that is less mixed than two that do not.

   Both members are pure, so $\sum_ip_iS(\rho_i)=0$ and
   $\chi=S(\bar\rho)$ outright. In Bloch language,
   $\vec r_0=(0,0,1)$ and $\vec r_+=(1,0,0)$ average to
   $\vec{\bar r}=(0.5,0,0.5)$ with $|\vec{\bar r}|=1/\sqrt2$, giving
   eigenvalues $(1\pm1/\sqrt2)/2=0.853553$ and $0.146447$, hence

   $$
   \chi(0)=S(\bar\rho)=H_2(0.853553)=0.600876\ \text{bits}.
   $$

   The overlap enters through that Bloch length, and the connection is
   exact rather than approximate. Two pure qubit states with overlap
   $s=|\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle|$ sit at Bloch angle $2\theta$ with
   $\cos\theta=s$, so their average vector has length exactly
   $\cos\theta=s$. Therefore for any equal mixture of two pure qubit
   states,

   $$
   \chi=H_2\!\left(\frac{1+s}{2}\right).
   $$

   Here $s=|\langle0|{+}\rangle|=1/\sqrt2$, matching
   $|\langle0|{+}\rangle|^2=\tfrac12$, and the formula returns $0.6009$.
   The two limits check out: $s=0$ gives $H_2(\tfrac12)=1$ bit, the
   orthogonal ensemble's full bit, and $s=1$ gives $H_2(1)=0$, since two
   identical states carry no information about which was sent. The BB84
   ensemble sits between, and $0.601$ is not a rounding artifact of any
   kind but the exact value of a binary entropy at $(1+1/\sqrt2)/2$.

   The physical reading is that Alice put a full bit of randomness in and
   the channel is noiseless, so nothing was lost in transit. What limits
   the receiver is that the two signals are not perfectly
   distinguishable: a measurement cannot separate states that are not
   orthogonal, and Holevo's bound quantifies the resulting shortfall at
   $1-0.601=0.399$ bits.

   For BB84 that shortfall is the entire point rather than a defect. The
   protocol's legitimate parties recover the bit by publicly reconciling
   bases and keeping only the rounds where Alice and Bob used the same one,
   in which the two possible states *are* orthogonal. An eavesdropper who
   does not know the basis faces exactly the $\{|0\rangle,|{+}\rangle\}$
   ensemble computed here, and $\chi=0.601$ bits is a hard ceiling on what
   she can learn per qubit.

   A number worth having beside $\chi$, because it shows Holevo's bound is
   not tight: the best *actual* measurement on this ensemble is the
   Helstrom one, with error probability
   $\tfrac12(1-\sqrt{1-s^2})=0.1464$, conveying
   $1-H_2(0.1464)=0.3991$ bits. The accessible information is well below
   $\chi=0.6009$. Holevo bounds what a single measurement on a single copy
   can extract; reaching $\chi$ requires block coding over many uses, which
   is what HSW's theorem supplies. (That $0.3991$ happens to
   equal $1-\chi$ here is a coincidence of $s=1/\sqrt2$, where the trace
   distance equals the overlap, and not a general identity.)

   </details>

2. The hashing bound goes negative for $p\gtrsim0.25$, but the channel
   is only proven entanglement-breaking (hence $Q=0$ exactly) at
   $p=\tfrac23$. In your own words, what would it take to close that
   gap, either way, and why is doing so a hard problem rather
   than a routine calculation?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Closing it from below means exhibiting a protocol that beats the hashing
   bound; closing it from above means proving an upper bound on a
   *regularized* quantity. Both are hard, and they are hard for different
   reasons.

   **From below.** The hashing bound is the coherent information of a
   single channel use with the maximally mixed input. Beating it requires
   either a better input state or, more importantly, codes that entangle
   inputs across many uses, since coherent information is superadditive and
   the capacity is the regularized limit. This direction has genuinely
   moved: DiVincenzo, Shor and Smolin showed in 1998 that random
   *degenerate* codes achieve positive rates for the depolarizing channel
   past the hashing bound's zero crossing, so $Q(p)>0$ is known for some
   $p$ above $0.2524$. Degenerate codes work by mapping distinct errors
   onto the same syndrome, so the code does not have to identify which
   error occurred, only undo it. That trick has no classical counterpart,
   which is part of why the classical intuition of "hashing is
   essentially optimal" fails here.

   **From above.** Every upper bound must hold for all $n$ at once, which
   rules out simply computing coherent information at $n=1$ and calling it
   a ceiling. What is needed is a quantity that is (i) additive by
   construction, (ii) provably above $Q$, and (iii) computable. The
   available families are entanglement-breaking and PPT arguments (which is
   what gives $p=\tfrac23$ here), antidegradability plus no-cloning, and
   Rains-type and approximate-degradability bounds, and the best known
   upper bounds for the depolarizing channel do sit strictly below the
   entanglement-breaking threshold. But none of them close on the lower
   bound, so a genuine interval remains where the value is unknown.

   **Why it is not routine.** The obstruction is structural rather than
   computational. $Q$ is defined by a limit over $n$, and the quantity
   being maximized is neither additive nor concave in the input state, so
   no finite computation is known to certify the limit. Worse, zero
   capacity is not even a local property: Smith and Yard exhibited two
   channels each with $Q=0$ whose joint use has $Q>0$, so "this channel
   cannot carry quantum information" does not compose the way intuition
   demands. A method that settled the depolarizing channel would have to
   handle all of that, which is why the exact quantum capacity of the
   single most-studied qubit channel is still open.

   Worth keeping in view: this is the one place in the course where the
   answer is genuinely unknown rather than merely uncomputed here. The
   classical capacity of the same channel is known in closed form. The
   quantum capacity is not, and the gap between $0.2524$ and $0.6667$ is
   the honest width of that ignorance.

   </details>

3. Explain why $Q(\mathcal N)=0$ is a much stronger, more final
   statement about a channel than "error correction for this channel
   would need a very high-rate code": connect your answer to the
   quantum noisy-channel coding theorem's converse direction.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the two statements quantify differently. "Would need a very
   high-rate code" is about the codes we currently have, and might be
   overturned tomorrow. $Q(\mathcal N)=0$ quantifies over *every* code, at
   every rate, at every block length, forever.

   The converse direction of the coding theorem is what supplies the
   "forever". A coding theorem has two halves: achievability, that any rate
   below the capacity is attainable with vanishing error, and the converse,
   that any rate strictly above it is not, no matter what the code is. The
   converse is a proof about all possible encoders and decoders, obtained
   from monotonicity of coherent information under the channel rather than
   from any property of a particular construction. So when the capacity is
   zero, the converse applies to every positive rate at once: the set of
   achievable rates is empty, and no sequence of codes has error going to
   zero.

   That is a different kind of claim from "expensive". For a channel with
   $Q>0$ but small, a low rate is a resource problem: more physical qubits
   per logical qubit, longer blocks, more syndrome extraction, but the
   threshold theorem says the fight is winnable by spending. For $Q=0$
   there is nothing to spend. The channel is on the far side of a boundary,
   and adding qubits moves you along a line that never crosses it.

   The mechanism at this lesson's own threshold makes the finality
   tangible. At $p\geq\tfrac23$ the channel is entanglement-breaking, which
   means it is equivalent to measuring the input, sending a classical
   description of the outcome, and preparing a fresh state from that
   description. Written that way, the impossibility needs no capacity
   theory at all: a classical description can be copied, so a channel that
   factors through one could clone quantum states, and no-cloning forbids
   it. The channel is a classical channel wearing a quantum costume, and
   the costume is the only quantum thing about it.

   One qualification keeps "final" honest, and it is a genuinely surprising
   one. $Q(\mathcal N)=0$ is final for $\mathcal N$ used *alone*. Smith and
   Yard showed that two channels each of zero quantum capacity can, used
   together, have strictly positive capacity, a phenomenon with no
   classical analogue at all. So the correct reading of $Q=0$ is "this
   channel transmits no quantum information by itself", which is exactly as
   strong as the theorem says and no stronger.

   </details>

## Summary

- The **Holevo quantity** $\chi(\{p_i,\rho_i\})=S(\sum_ip_i\rho_i)-\sum_ip_iS(\rho_i)$
  bounds what any single measurement extracts from an ensemble
  (Holevo's theorem); the channel's **classical capacity** is HSW's
  theorem that $\max_{\text{ensembles}}\chi$, regularized in general,
  is exactly the many-uses-limit achievable rate, with depolarizing and
  dephasing channels among the special cases proven single-letter.
- The BB84 ensemble's exact computed $\chi(p)$ falls from
  $\approx0.601$ bits at $p=0$ to exactly $0$ at $p=1$, using this
  platform's real `vonNeumannEntropy` and `convexCombination`; the
  orthogonal-basis classical capacity $C(p)$ stays strictly positive
  for every $p<1$.
- The **quantum capacity**, via LSD's regularized coherent information,
  is far more fragile: a computed hashing-bound lower bound goes
  to zero by $p\approx0.25$, and the depolarizing channel is proven
  **exactly** zero-capacity from $p=\tfrac23$ onward via the
  entanglement-breaking criterion, derived and numerically verified
  above from this platform's own Choi-state and partial-transpose
  machinery.
- Exactly where $Q(p)$ truly reaches zero, versus merely where a known
  bound stops certifying it, is an open computation for the depolarizing
  channel; computing exact channel capacities in general is an active
  research area in quantum Shannon theory, not a closed textbook
  question. That is an honest note to end the course on.
