## Motivation

Solving the Finite Well Numerically needed bisection because
$k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ has no closed-form solution. That's true for a
single well, but it hides something more useful: every bound-state
energy of *any* Hamiltonian, solvable or not, is simultaneously encoded
in one analytic function of a complex variable $E$, its **resolvent**.
Bound states show up as poles, scattering states as a branch cut: one
object, derived once here, replacing "solve this specific transcendental
equation" with "find where this specific function blows up."

## Intuition

A driven classical oscillator's response amplitude goes like
$1/(\omega^2-\omega_0^2)$: it diverges exactly at resonance,
$\omega=\omega_0$. The resolvent $R(E)=(E-H)^{-1}$ is the exact quantum
analogue of that response function, with $H$'s eigenvalues playing the
role of resonant frequencies: $R(E)$ "blows up" precisely when $E$ hits
an eigenvalue of $H$, for exactly the same algebraic reason a
denominator vanishes at resonance.

## Mathematical Development

### The resolvent, derived from the spectral theorem

For a self-adjoint $H$ with discrete spectrum $H=\sum_nE_nP_n$ (the last
two lessons' spectral theorem), define $R(E)\equiv(E-H)^{-1}$ for $E$
not an eigenvalue. **Claim:** $R(E)=\sum_n\dfrac{P_n}{E-E_n}$. Verify
directly by multiplying:

$$
(E-H)\sum_n\frac{P_n}{E-E_n}=\sum_n(E-E_n)\frac{P_n}{E-E_n}=\sum_nP_n=I
$$

(using $HP_n=E_nP_n$ and completeness $\sum_nP_n=I$), a genuine, direct
verification that this formula really is the inverse, not just a
plausible guess.

### Poles $\leftrightarrow$ bound states

$R(E)=\sum_n P_n/(E-E_n)$ has an isolated pole at every discrete
eigenvalue $E=E_n$, with residue exactly $P_n$, the projector onto that
eigenspace. This is the precise sense in which "the resolvent's poles
are the bound-state energies": not an analogy, but a direct algebraic
consequence of the spectral decomposition, readable off the formula
above by inspection.

### Branch cuts $\leftrightarrow$ continuous spectrum, via Sokhotski–Plemelji

For a continuous spectrum, the sum becomes the spectral-theorem integral
$R(E)=\int dE'\,dE(E')/(E-E')$ (schematically, using the PVM from two
lessons ago), and the integrand's singularity is no longer isolated: it
sweeps continuously along the whole continuous spectrum. Approaching a
point $E'$ *on* that continuum from just above versus just below the
real axis gives *different* limits, which is exactly what a branch cut
means. The identity behind that statement is worth deriving in full
rather than just naming:

$$
\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{1}{x-i\varepsilon}\right]=\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{x+i\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}
$$

Integrate this over all $x$, using the antiderivative $\frac{d}{dx}
\arctan(x/\varepsilon)=\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)$ (a direct check
by differentiating):

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}\,dx=\Big[\arctan(x/\varepsilon)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=\frac\pi2-\left(-\frac\pi2\right)=\pi
$$

**exactly $\pi$, for every $\varepsilon>0$**, while the function itself
concentrates ever more sharply at $x=0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$ (its peak
height $1/\varepsilon\to\infty$, its width $\sim\varepsilon\to0$),
exactly the same delta-sequence structure as the last lesson's Dirichlet
kernel, now for a Lorentzian instead of a sinc:

Exactly this delta-sequence behavior is visible directly: as $\varepsilon$
shrinks from $1$ down to $0.03$, the curve's peak at $x=0$ grows toward
$1/\varepsilon$ while its width collapses toward $x=0$, even though the
total area underneath stays fixed at exactly $\pi$ for every one of
these $\varepsilon$ values (the exact antiderivative computation just
above, not a numerical approximation that merely gets close). So
$\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)\to\pi\delta(x)$, giving the
**Sokhotski–Plemelji relation**:

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\frac{1}{x-i\varepsilon}=\mathcal P\!\left(\frac1x\right)+i\pi\delta(x)
$$

(the real-part statement, that the principal value $\mathcal P(1/x)$
is the correct real limit, is cited, a standard but separate
distributional argument). The resolvent's imaginary part, right at the
continuous spectrum, is therefore proportional to a genuine density of
states (a $\delta$-function's worth of weight at every point of the
continuum), which is precisely why the continuous spectrum reads as a
branch cut on the real axis rather than a single isolated pole: the
function's value actually differs depending on which side of the cut
you approach from.

### The free-particle Green's function, derived from a jump condition

$G_0(x,x';E)\equiv\langle x|R(E)|x'\rangle$ satisfies $(E-H)G_0=\delta(x-x')$
in position space. For $H=-\tfrac12\,d^2/dx^2$ (natural units, $V=0$),
this reads $G_0''+k^2G_0=2\delta(x-x')$, $k\equiv\sqrt{2E}$. Away from
$x=x'$, $G_0'' +k^2G_0=0$: the outgoing-wave solution respecting
$x\to\pm\infty$ boundary conditions is $G_0(x,x';E)=Ae^{ik|x-x'|}$.
Integrating the ODE across the kink at $x=x'$ (the second derivative of
$|x-x'|$ contributes exactly the delta function's coefficient) gives the
jump condition $G_0'(x'^+)-G_0'(x'^-)=2$. Direct differentiation of
$Ae^{ik|x-x'|}$ gives $G_0'(x'^+)=iAk$, $G_0'(x'^-)=-iAk$, so
$2iAk=2\Rightarrow A=1/(ik)=-i/k$:

$$
\boxed{\;G_0(x,x';E)=-\frac{i}{k}\,e^{ik|x-x'|}, \qquad k=\sqrt{2E}\;}
$$

a fully derived closed form, not looked up.

### The infinite-well Green's function, derived and verified against the known spectrum

Same method, Dirichlet boundary conditions $G(\pm a,x')=0$ instead of
outgoing waves at infinity. Build $G(x,x';E)=B\sin\big(k(x_<+a)\big)
\sin\big(k(a-x_>)\big)$ ($x_<=\min(x,x')$, $x_>=\max(x,x')$; each factor
individually satisfies the boundary condition at its own end, and the
product is automatically continuous at $x=x'$). The constant Wronskian
of the two building-block solutions works out to $-k\sin(2ka)$
(computed directly, evaluating both solutions and their derivatives at
$x=0$), giving the jump condition $-Bk\sin(2ka)=2$, so:

$$
\boxed{\;G_{\text{well}}(x,x';E)=-\frac{2}{k\sin(2ka)}\sin\big(k(x_<+a)\big)\sin\big(k(a-x_>)\big), \qquad k=\sqrt{2E}\;}
$$

**This has poles exactly where $\sin(2ka)=0$, i.e. $2ka=n\pi$, i.e.
$E=n^2\pi^2/(8a^2)$**: compare directly to the infinite well's already-known
closed-form spectrum, `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(n, 2a)` $=n^2\pi^2/(2(2a)^2)=n^2\pi^2/(8a^2)$
**exactly the same formula**, an independent derivation reproducing a
result this platform already had, not a new assumption. For $a=1$,
computed directly by the platform's engine: $E_1\approx1.2337$,
$E_2\approx4.9348$.

## Physical Interpretation

The resolvent turns "find the bound states" (a hard problem in general;
Solving the Finite Well Numerically needed bisection precisely because
no closed form exists) into "find the poles of one specific function of
a complex variable", the *same* kind of question regardless of how
complicated the potential is, even when the eigenvalues themselves have
no closed form. This is why resolvent and Green's-function methods
dominate more advanced treatments (scattering theory, quantum field
theory): they reformulate spectral problems as complex-analysis
problems, trading "solve a transcendental equation" for "locate a
singularity," a different and often more tractable kind of
question.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the resolvent to have poles anywhere in the continuum, the
same way it does for bound states.** It doesn't: the continuous
spectrum shows up as a *branch cut* (a discontinuity in the limiting
value approached from above vs. below the real axis, via
Sokhotski–Plemelji), never as an isolated pole. Only actual point
eigenvalues, isolated from the rest of the spectrum, produce poles.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting that $G(x,x';E)$'s pole locations don't depend on $x,x'$ at
all.** The poles come entirely from where the *denominator*
($k\sin(2ka)$, or $(E-E_n)$ in the spectral sum) vanishes, a property
of the operator $H$ alone. Changing $x,x'$ changes the *residue*
(effectively, how strongly that pole shows up at those particular
points) but never *where* the poles are.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $R(E)=(E-H)^{-1}=\sum_nP_n/(E-E_n)$, derived directly from the
  spectral theorem; its poles are exactly the discrete eigenvalues, with
  residue equal to that eigenvalue's projector.
- The continuous spectrum shows up as a branch cut, via the
  Sokhotski–Plemelji identity $\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}1/(x-i\varepsilon)=
  \mathcal P(1/x)+i\pi\delta(x)$, whose imaginary part is derived here in
  full from an exact, $\varepsilon$-independent integral.
- $G_0(x,x';E)=-\frac{i}{k}e^{ik|x-x'|}$ (free particle) and
  $G_{\text{well}}(x,x';E)=-\frac{2}{k\sin(2ka)}\sin(k(x_<+a))\sin(k(a-x_>))$
  (infinite well), both derived from a jump condition across $x=x'$.
- The well's Green's function poles land exactly on
  `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel`'s already-known spectrum, and its
  closed form agrees with an independent eigenfunction-expansion sum to
  $5$-$6$ significant figures using 200 terms: two different derivations, same number.
