## Bisection, and Why It Was Necessary

Solving the Finite Well Numerically needed bisection because
$k\tan(ka)=\kappa$ has no closed-form solution. That's true for a
single well, but it hides something more useful: every bound-state
energy of *any* Hamiltonian, solvable or not, is simultaneously encoded
in one analytic function of a complex variable $E$, its
<Term id="greens-functions-resolvents">resolvent</Term>.
Bound states show up as poles, scattering states as a branch cut: one
object, derived once here, replacing "solve this specific transcendental
equation" with "find where this specific function blows up."

## Resonance as a Pole

A driven classical oscillator's response amplitude goes like
$1/(\omega^2-\omega_0^2)$: it diverges exactly at resonance,
$\omega=\omega_0$. The resolvent $R(E)=(E-H)^{-1}$ is the exact quantum
analogue of that response function, with $H$'s eigenvalues playing the
role of resonant frequencies: $R(E)$ "blows up" precisely when $E$ hits
the spectrum of $H$, for the same algebraic reason a denominator vanishes
at resonance. The analogy has a limit worth flagging early: for a
discrete eigenvalue the blow-up is a pole, and for a continuous spectral
value it is not, which is what the branch-cut section below is about.

## Poles, Branch Cuts, and Sokhotski-Plemelj

### The resolvent, derived from the spectral theorem

For a self-adjoint $H$ with discrete spectrum $H=\sum_nE_nP_n$ (the last
two lessons' spectral theorem), define $R(E)\equiv(E-H)^{-1}$ for $E$
not an eigenvalue. **Claim:** $R(E)=\sum_n\dfrac{P_n}{E-E_n}$. Verify
directly by multiplying:

$$
(E-H)\sum_n\frac{P_n}{E-E_n}=\sum_n(E-E_n)\frac{P_n}{E-E_n}=\sum_nP_n=I
$$

(using $HP_n=E_nP_n$ and completeness $\sum_nP_n=I$), which verifies that
the formula really is the inverse. One hypothesis
rides along silently: $E$ must lie in the resolvent set. For $E$ on the
spectrum a denominator vanishes and $R(E)$ fails to be a bounded
operator, which is why the resolvent set is defined by boundedness of the
inverse rather than by invertibility alone.

### Poles $\leftrightarrow$ bound states

$R(E)=\sum_n P_n/(E-E_n)$ has an isolated pole at every discrete
eigenvalue $E=E_n$, with residue exactly $P_n$, the projector onto that
eigenspace. This is the precise sense in which "the resolvent's poles
are the bound-state energies": not an analogy, but a direct algebraic
consequence of the spectral decomposition, readable off the formula
above by inspection.

For a matrix this is the end of the story. In finite dimensions
$R(E)=\mathrm{adj}(E-H)/\det(E-H)$ is a rational function of $E$ whose
only singularities are the finitely many roots of the characteristic
polynomial, all of them poles. A matrix resolvent can never have a branch
cut, because a rational function has none. Everything in the next section
is a phenomenon that exists only once the spectrum stops being a finite
list.

### Branch cuts $\leftrightarrow$ continuous spectrum, via Sokhotski–Plemelj

For a continuous spectrum, the sum becomes the spectral-theorem integral
$R(E)=\int dE'\,dE(E')/(E-E')$ (schematically, using the PVM from two
lessons ago), and the integrand's singularity is no longer isolated: it
sweeps continuously along the whole continuous spectrum. Approaching a
point $E'$ *on* that continuum from just above versus just below the
real axis gives *different* limits, which is exactly what a branch cut
means. The identity behind that statement is worth deriving in full:

$$
\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{1}{x-i\varepsilon}\right]=\mathrm{Im}\left[\frac{x+i\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}\right]=\frac{\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}
$$

As $\varepsilon\to0$ this Lorentzian concentrates ever more sharply at
$x=0$: its peak height $1/\varepsilon\to\infty$ and its width
$\sim\varepsilon\to0$. Whether that concentration is the same
delta-sequence structure as the last lesson's Dirichlet kernel turns
entirely on what happens to the area underneath:

Integrating over all $x$, using the antiderivative $\frac{d}{dx}
\arctan(x/\varepsilon)=\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)$ (a direct check
by differentiating), settles it:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\varepsilon}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}\,dx=\Big[\arctan(x/\varepsilon)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty}=\frac\pi2-\left(-\frac\pi2\right)=\pi
$$

**exactly $\pi$, for every $\varepsilon>0$**: fixed area, rising peak,
narrowing width, the same delta-sequence structure as the Dirichlet
kernel, now for a Lorentzian instead of a sinc.

<TheoremBox title="Sokhotski–Plemelj relation" provenance="derived">

For every test function $\varphi\in\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$,

$$
\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\int\frac{\varphi(x)}{x-i\varepsilon}\,dx = \mathcal P\!\int\frac{\varphi(x)}{x}\,dx + i\pi\varphi(0),
$$

written compactly as
$\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}(x-i\varepsilon)^{-1}=\mathcal P(1/x)+i\pi\delta(x)$.

The **sense of the limit** is the part worth pinning down: this is an
equality of *distributions*, in the $\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing of the previous
lesson, not a pointwise limit of functions. Pointwise, the left side has
no limit at $x=0$ at all. The imaginary half is derived above from an
integral that equals $\pi$ at every finite $\varepsilon$; the principal-value
real half is cited (a separate distributional argument, standard in any
treatment of tempered distributions).

</TheoremBox>

The consequence for the resolvent: its imaginary part, evaluated right on
the continuous spectrum, is $\pi$ times a density of states, since the
$\delta$ contributes weight at every point of the continuum, never at
isolated points. That is why the continuous spectrum reads as a branch
cut on the real axis rather than a set of poles. The limiting value
differs depending on which side of the axis you approach from, and a
function with two different boundary values across a line has a cut
there, by definition.

### The free-particle Green's function, derived from a jump condition

$G_0(x,x';E)\equiv\langle x|R(E)|x'\rangle$ satisfies $(E-H)G_0=\delta(x-x')$
in position space. For $H=-\tfrac12\,d^2/dx^2$ (natural units, $V=0$),
this reads $G_0''+k^2G_0=2\delta(x-x')$, $k\equiv\sqrt{2E}$. A real
$E>0$ sits *on* the free particle's continuous spectrum, where the
previous section says the resolvent has no single value, so a branch has
to be chosen: take $k=\sqrt{2(E+i0^+)}$, the limit from above, which
fixes $\mathrm{Im}\,k>0$ and picks out the *outgoing* solution. That
choice is what the phrase "the retarded Green's function" names. Away
from $x=x'$, $G_0'' +k^2G_0=0$, and the outgoing solution respecting
$x\to\pm\infty$ is $G_0(x,x';E)=Ae^{ik|x-x'|}$.
Integrating the ODE across the kink at $x=x'$ (the second derivative of
$|x-x'|$ contributes exactly the delta function's coefficient) gives the
jump condition $G_0'(x'^+)-G_0'(x'^-)=2$. Direct differentiation of
$Ae^{ik|x-x'|}$ gives $G_0'(x'^+)=iAk$, $G_0'(x'^-)=-iAk$, so
$2iAk=2\Rightarrow A=1/(ik)=-i/k$:

$$
\boxed{\;G_0(x,x';E)=-\frac{i}{k}\,e^{ik|x-x'|}, \qquad k=\sqrt{2E}\;}
$$

The jump condition, not a table of integrals, is what fixed $A$.

### The infinite-well Green's function, derived and verified against the known spectrum

Same method, Dirichlet boundary conditions $G(\pm a,x')=0$ instead of
outgoing waves at infinity. Build $G(x,x';E)=B\sin\big(k(x_<+a)\big)
\sin\big(k(a-x_>)\big)$ ($x_<=\min(x,x')$, $x_>=\max(x,x')$; each factor
individually satisfies the boundary condition at its own end, and the
product is automatically continuous at $x=x'$). The constant Wronskian
of the two building-block solutions works out to $-k\sin(2ka)$
(computed directly, evaluating both solutions and their derivatives at
$x=0$), giving the jump condition $-Bk\sin(2ka)=2$, so:

$$
\boxed{\;G_{\text{well}}(x,x';E)=-\frac{2}{k\sin(2ka)}\sin\big(k(x_<+a)\big)\sin\big(k(a-x_>)\big), \qquad k=\sqrt{2E}\;}
$$

**This has poles exactly where $\sin(2ka)=0$, i.e. $2ka=n\pi$, i.e.
$E=n^2\pi^2/(8a^2)$**: compare directly to the infinite well's already-known
closed-form spectrum, `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(n, 2a)` $=n^2\pi^2/(2(2a)^2)=n^2\pi^2/(8a^2)$
**exactly the same formula**, an independent derivation reproducing a
result this platform already had, not a new assumption. For $a=1$,
computed by `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel`: $E_1=$ {wellE1.toFixed(4)},
$E_2=$ {wellE2.toFixed(4)}.

## Turning a Search Into an Analytic Structure

The resolvent turns "find the bound states" (a hard problem in general;
Solving the Finite Well Numerically needed bisection because
no closed form exists) into "find the poles of one specific function of
a complex variable", the *same* kind of question regardless of how
complicated the potential is, even when the eigenvalues themselves have
no closed form. This is why resolvent and Green's-function methods
dominate more advanced treatments (scattering theory, quantum field
theory): they reformulate spectral problems as complex-analysis
problems, trading "solve a transcendental equation" for "locate a
singularity," a different and often more tractable kind of
question.

## The Infinite Well's Green Function, Two Ways

**Check the closed form against the eigenfunction expansion
$G(x,x';E)=\sum_n\psi_n(x)\psi_n(x')/(E-E_n)$, which is derived from the
spectral theorem rather than from the jump condition, and so is an
independent route to the same object.**

Take $a=1$, $E=$ {greenTestEnergy} (strictly between
$E_1\approx$ {wellE1.toFixed(4)} and $E_2\approx$ {wellE2.toFixed(4)}, so
no pole is nearby), $x=$ {greenTestX}, $x'=$ {greenTestXPrime}. The boxed
closed form gives $G_{\text{well}}=$ {greenClosedForm.toFixed(5)}. The
spectral sum, run at module scope over the first
{greenSpectralTermCount} terms with energies from this platform's
`infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel` and the well's normalized
eigenfunctions, gives {greenSpectralSum.toFixed(5)}: the same to every
decimal shown, with a relative gap of {greenRelativeGap.toExponential(1)}.

The residual is the truncation, not an error in either derivation. The
terms fall off like $1/n^2$, so a $200$-term partial sum still carries a
tail of that order, and the two numbers converge as more terms are kept.
What matters is that a boundary-value ODE solve and a spectral sum over
independently-known eigenfunctions, sharing no step of their derivations,
land on the same value.

## Expecting Poles Where There Is a Cut

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting the resolvent to have poles anywhere in the continuum, the
same way it does for bound states.** It doesn't: the continuous
spectrum shows up as a *branch cut* (a discontinuity in the limiting
value approached from above vs. below the real axis, via
Sokhotski–Plemelj), never as an isolated pole. Only actual point
eigenvalues, isolated from the rest of the spectrum, produce poles.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting that $G(x,x';E)$'s pole locations don't depend on $x,x'$ at
all.** The poles come entirely from where the *denominator*
($k\sin(2ka)$, or $(E-E_n)$ in the spectral sum) vanishes, a property
of the operator $H$ alone. Changing $x,x'$ changes the *residue*
(effectively, how strongly that pole shows up at those particular
points) but never *where* the poles are.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify directly, by substitution, that $G_0(x,x';E)=-\frac{i}{k}
   e^{ik|x-x'|}$ satisfies $G_0''+k^2G_0=0$ for $x\ne x'$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Away from $x=x'$ the sign of $x-x'$ is fixed, so write
   $s\equiv\mathrm{sgn}(x-x')$, a constant on each side, and
   $|x-x'|=s(x-x')$. Then $G_0=-\frac{i}{k}e^{iks(x-x')}$ is an ordinary
   exponential in $x$ with rate $iks$, and differentiating twice just
   brings that rate down twice:

   $$
   \frac{\partial^2G_0}{\partial x^2}=(iks)^2G_0=-k^2s^2G_0=-k^2G_0,
   $$

   because $s^2=1$ on either side. So $G_0''+k^2G_0=0$, with no case
   analysis needed. Doing the two cases separately gives the same thing:
   for $x>x'$, $G_0'=e^{ik(x-x')}$ and $G_0''=ik\,e^{ik(x-x')}$, and
   $ik\,e^{ik(x-x')}+k^2\left(-\tfrac{i}{k}\right)e^{ik(x-x')}=0$; for
   $x<x'$ every $k$ flips sign and the same cancellation happens.

   The step $s^2=1$ is the whole point of the absolute value, and it is
   worth naming rather than passing over. The homogeneous equation is
   blind to the sign: $e^{+ik(x-x')}$ and $e^{-ik(x-x')}$ both solve it,
   and gluing one to each side produces something the equation still
   cannot distinguish from either. What the equation *can* see is the
   derivative at the seam. Since $G_0'=s\,e^{ik|x-x'|}$, the one-sided
   limits are $+1$ and $-1$, so

   $$
   G_0'(x'^+)-G_0'(x'^-)=1-(-1)=2,
   $$

   exactly the jump condition that fixed $A=-i/k$ in the first place. The
   same kink that is invisible to the equation for $x\ne x'$ is precisely
   what manufactures the delta at $x=x'$. Verification and construction
   are two halves of one fact.

   Notice also that $-i/k$ never entered the check. It could not: the
   equation is linear and homogeneous, so it accepts any constant
   multiple, and the constant was pinned by the jump condition and by
   nothing else. A student who reports having "verified the
   normalization" has verified something the homogeneous equation is
   incapable of testing.

   The wrong turn is differentiating $|x-x'|$ all the way through and
   producing a delta term, since $\frac{d}{dx}|x-x'|=s$ and
   $\frac{ds}{dx}=2\delta(x-x')$, then reporting that the identity fails
   by $2\delta$. Nothing failed. That delta is exactly the term the
   question excluded by saying $x\ne x'$, and it is the source term of
   the full equation $G_0''+k^2G_0=2\delta(x-x')$. A second wrong turn is
   deciding that the absolute value makes $G_0$ non-differentiable and
   therefore unverifiable. It is analytic on each open side; the single
   bad point is the one point removed from consideration.

   </details>

2. For the infinite well with $a=2$ (not $a=1$), at what energy does the
   *first* pole of $G_{\text{well}}$ occur? Compare to
   `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(1, 4)`.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Poles sit where $\sin(2ka)=0$, so $2ka=n\pi$ and $k=n\pi/(2a)$. With
   $a=2$ and $n=1$ that is $k=\pi/4$, and since $k=\sqrt{2E}$,

   $$
   E_1=\frac{k^2}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^2=\frac{\pi^2}{32}=0.3084251375 .
   $$

   The general form $E=n^2\pi^2/(8a^2)$ gives the same thing at $n=1,a=2$.
   The engine call agrees to every digit: `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(1, 4)`
   evaluates $\pi^2/(2\cdot1\cdot4^2)=\pi^2/32=0.30842513753404244$, and
   the closed form returns $0.30842513753404244$ as well.

   The comparison to $a=1$ is the useful sanity check. There
   $E_1=\pi^2/8=1.2337005501$, exactly four times larger. Doubling the
   half-width quarters the energy, which is the $1/a^2$ in the formula
   showing up as a clean factor of $4$ rather than as something to take on
   trust.

   The wrong turn is calling `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(2, ...)` or,
   far more commonly, `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(1, 2)`: passing the
   half-width where the function wants the full width. It returns
   $1.2337005501361697$, four times too big, and it is seductive because
   the lesson's own formula is written in terms of $a$ while the engine's
   signature is written in terms of `width`. The lesson spells the
   translation out as `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel(n, 2a)`, and the
   factor-of-four discrepancy is the symptom to recognize: an energy off
   by exactly $4$ in a well problem is almost always a half-width fed in
   as a width.

   A second thing worth checking, because it looks like a pole and is
   not: $\sin(2ka)$ also vanishes at $k=0$, that is at $E=0$. Expand the
   Green's function there. The numerator
   $\sin(k(x_<+a))\sin(k(a-x_>))$ vanishes like $k^2$ while the
   denominator $k\sin(2ka)$ vanishes like $2ak^2$, so the ratio is finite
   and $G_{\text{well}}\to-(x_<+a)(a-x_>)/a$. Numerically at $a=2$,
   $x=0.3$, $x'=-0.2$, the closed form returns $-1.53025$ at $k=10^{-2}$
   and $-1.53000$ at $k=10^{-5}$, converging on the predicted $-1.53$.
   $E=0$ is a removable singularity, not a pole, and it has to be: $n=0$
   would mean $\psi\propto\sin(0)=0$, which is not a state. The formula
   knows on its own that the ground state is $n=1$.

   </details>
3. Explain in one or two sentences why $\mathrm{Im}[1/(x-i\varepsilon)]=
   \varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)$ integrating to exactly $\pi$ for
   *every* $\varepsilon>0$ (not just in some limit) is what makes it a
   delta-sequence, not merely "approximately" one.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a delta-sequence has to get two independent things right, and
   an exact mass settles one of them before the limit is ever taken. The
   family must concentrate, which the shrinking width does, *and* it must
   carry a fixed total weight, so that the limit has a definite
   normalization rather than one that depends on how the limit was
   approached. Here $\int\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)dx=\pi$ holds
   identically in $\varepsilon$, so the weight is decided in advance and
   only the shape is left to change.

   One precision, since the lesson is careful about it and the question's
   wording is loose: $\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)$ is a delta-sequence
   only after dividing by $\pi$. As written it converges to $\pi\delta(x)$,
   and that is exactly where the $\pi$ in
   $\mathcal P(1/x)+i\pi\delta(x)$ comes from. The factor is not
   cosmetic; it propagates into every density of states read off
   $\mathrm{Im}\,R(E)$.

   Where the exactness does concrete work is in the interchange of limit
   and integral. Split a test function around the origin:

   $$
   \int\frac{\varepsilon\,\varphi(x)}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}dx=\pi\varphi(0)+\int\frac{\varepsilon\big[\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)\big]}{x^2+\varepsilon^2}dx .
   $$

   The first term is pulled out using the exact mass, with no error
   attached to it whatsoever. The second vanishes because
   $\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)=O(|x|)$ near the origin cancels one power of
   $x$, leaving something of size $\varepsilon\ln(1/\varepsilon)$: on
   $|x|\le1$ the bound $\varepsilon\ln\!\big((1+\varepsilon^2)/\varepsilon^2\big)$
   is $0.46$, $0.092$, $0.014$ at $\varepsilon=0.1,0.01,0.001$. Had the
   mass only been *approximately* $\pi$, the first term would carry an
   unknown error multiplying $\varphi(0)$, and the estimate would be
   circular: you would be trying to prove the limit while assuming a
   bound on the very quantity in question.

   Substituting $x=\varepsilon\tan\theta$ turns the mass integral into
   $\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}d\theta$ with $\varepsilon$ cancelling
   identically, and dense quadrature in $\theta$ returns $3.141592654$ at
   $\varepsilon=1$, $0.1$, $0.03$ and $10^{-6}$ alike: nine digits,
   unchanged across six orders of magnitude.

   The wrong turn is the height-times-width argument: the peak is
   $1/\varepsilon$ and the width is about $\varepsilon$, the product is
   order one, so it must be a delta. That reasoning gets the shape right
   and the constant wrong, and the constant is the entire content here.
   Peak times full width at half maximum is $(1/\varepsilon)(2\varepsilon)=2$
   exactly, and peak times half-width is $1$; neither is $\pi$. Trusting
   the heuristic drops the $\pi$ from Sokhotski-Plemelj and from every
   density of states derived through it.

   A second wrong turn is integrating the pointwise limit. At any fixed
   $x\ne0$, $\varepsilon/(x^2+\varepsilon^2)\to0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$, so
   the pointwise limit is the zero function almost everywhere and its
   integral is $0$, not $\pi$. The two orders of operation genuinely
   disagree, and the exact mass is what tells you which order is the
   definition: the limit is taken in the pairing against a test function,
   never inside it. That is the same lesson the Dirichlet kernel taught
   one lesson ago, with a Lorentzian in place of a sinc.

   </details>

## Summary

- $R(E)=(E-H)^{-1}=\sum_nP_n/(E-E_n)$, derived directly from the
  spectral theorem; its poles are exactly the discrete eigenvalues, with
  residue equal to that eigenvalue's projector.
- The continuous spectrum shows up as a branch cut, via the
  Sokhotski–Plemelj identity $\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}1/(x-i\varepsilon)=
  \mathcal P(1/x)+i\pi\delta(x)$, whose imaginary part is derived here in
  full from an exact, $\varepsilon$-independent integral.
- $G_0(x,x';E)=-\frac{i}{k}e^{ik|x-x'|}$ (free particle) and
  $G_{\text{well}}(x,x';E)=-\frac{2}{k\sin(2ka)}\sin(k(x_<+a))\sin(k(a-x_>))$
  (infinite well), both derived from a jump condition across $x=x'$.
- The well's Green's function poles land exactly on
  `infiniteSquareWellEnergyLevel`'s already-known spectrum, and its
  closed form agrees with an independent eigenfunction-expansion sum to
  $5$-$6$ significant figures using 200 terms: two different derivations, same number.
