## Motivation

Spectral Decomposition and Degeneracy wrote $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ and
called it "the spectral decomposition," full stop. That's completely
correct when $A$'s spectrum is discrete. But the position operator
$\hat x$ has *no* eigenvalues in $L^2(\mathbb R)$ at all in the ordinary
sense (Continuous Spectra and Rigged Hilbert Space, next lesson, makes
this precise); its "eigenvalues" fill the entire real line continuously.
A sum over discrete $\lambda_i$ literally cannot be the right statement
for such an operator. This lesson gives the one theorem that correctly
covers both cases at once, and, the actual payoff, shows the discrete
formula was never a separate fact, just this general theorem evaluated
on a spectrum with no continuous part.

## Intuition

Replace "eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ with projector $P_i$" by "**every** real
number $\lambda$ gets an infinitesimal projector $dE(\lambda)$," and
replace the sum $\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ by an integral $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$.
The object $E(\lambda)$, a projector for *each* real number $\lambda$,
built to project onto "the part of the space with spectral value $\le
\lambda$", is called a **projection-valued measure** (PVM), and it's
the single object that makes both the discrete and continuous cases
literal instances of one formula, exactly the way a Riemann–Stieltjes
integral $\int f\,dG$ unifies a sum (when $G$ is a staircase function)
and an ordinary integral (when $G$ is differentiable) into one notation.

## Mathematical Development

### The projection-valued measure

A PVM assigns to every (Borel) subset $\Delta\subseteq\mathbb R$ a
projector $E(\Delta)$ on $\mathcal H$, satisfying $E(\mathbb R)=I$
(**completeness**: the whole space, summed over the whole spectrum),
$E(\Delta_1\cap\Delta_2)=E(\Delta_1)E(\Delta_2)$ (**consistency**:
projecting onto an intersection is the same as projecting onto each
piece in turn), and countable additivity for disjoint sets. It's
conventional to package this as a single projector-valued, non-decreasing
function $E(\lambda)\equiv E((-\infty,\lambda])$, the **spectral
resolution of the identity**: $E(\lambda)$ projects onto "everything with
spectral value at most $\lambda$," rising from the zero operator at
$\lambda\to-\infty$ to $I$ at $\lambda\to+\infty$.

### The theorem

**Spectral theorem (PVM form).** Every self-adjoint operator $A$ on a
Hilbert space $\mathcal H$ has a unique projection-valued measure $E$
such that

$$
A=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda\,dE(\lambda), \qquad D(A)=\left\{\psi\in\mathcal H:\int\lambda^2\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle<\infty\right\}
$$

This is a genuinely deep theorem of functional analysis (the general
proof, for the unbounded case, is due to von Neumann); it is **stated
here, not proved**, exactly as Hermitian Operators stated (rather than
fully proved the existence half of) the finite-dimensional spectral
theorem. What *is* derived below, in full, is the connection that makes
this abstract statement earn its keep: showing precisely how it
reproduces the already-proven finite-dimensional result.

Note the theorem's hypothesis is **self-adjoint**, not merely symmetric,
exactly the distinction the last lesson built. A merely symmetric
operator is not guaranteed a PVM at all (the half-line momentum operator,
having no self-adjoint extension, correspondingly has no single
canonical spectral resolution); self-adjointness is precisely the
hypothesis this theorem needs.

### Reduction to the discrete case, derived explicitly

Suppose $A$'s spectrum is purely discrete: eigenvalues $\lambda_1<
\lambda_2<\cdots$ with orthogonal projectors $P_1,P_2,\ldots$ onto their
(possibly degenerate) eigenspaces, $\sum_iP_i=I$, exactly Spectral
Decomposition and Degeneracy's setup. Define

$$
E(\lambda)\equiv\sum_{i:\,\lambda_i\le\lambda}P_i
$$

This is a genuine PVM: it's non-decreasing in $\lambda$ (each term is a
projector, and projectors onto orthogonal subspaces add without
cancellation), it equals $0$ below $\lambda_1$ and $I$ at and above the
largest eigenvalue, and it satisfies the consistency condition (a direct
consequence of $P_iP_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, already proven in Spectral
Decomposition and Degeneracy). $E(\lambda)$, as a function of $\lambda$,
is a **staircase**: constant except for a jump of size $P_i$ exactly at
$\lambda=\lambda_i$.

Now evaluate $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle=\int\lambda\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle$
for this staircase measure directly. $\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle=
\sum_{i:\lambda_i\le\lambda}\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i:\lambda_i\le\lambda}\|P_i\psi\|^2$
is itself a staircase function of $\lambda$, jumping by $\|P_i\psi\|^2$
at each $\lambda_i$. A Riemann–Stieltjes integral against a pure
staircase function is, by definition, exactly the sum of (integrand
value at each jump) $\times$ (jump size):

$$
\int\lambda\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i\|P_i\psi\|^2=\sum_i\lambda_i\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle=\left\langle\psi\left|\sum_i\lambda_iP_i\right|\psi\right\rangle
$$

Since this holds for every $\psi$, the operators themselves agree:
$A=\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$, **exactly** Spectral
Decomposition and Degeneracy's formula, now derived as the special case
of the general theorem where the spectral measure happens to be a
staircase, rather than stated as a separate fact.

### The measurement postulate, unified

Born's rule for a discrete spectrum, "$P(\text{outcome}=\lambda_i)=
\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$," and the continuous-spectrum position
postulate, "$P(x\in\Delta)=\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx$," look like two
different rules that earlier courses stated separately (one in
Operators, Observables & Measurement, one in Wave Mechanics). In PVM
language they are the **same single statement**:

$$
P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle
$$

For a discrete spectrum, $E(\{\lambda_i\})=P_i$, recovering the familiar
formula exactly. For the position operator, $E(\Delta)$ turns out to be
"multiply by the indicator function $\mathbf 1_\Delta(x)$" (verified
directly: this is manifestly a projector, since
$\mathbf1_\Delta(x)^2=\mathbf1_\Delta(x)$ pointwise, and
$\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle=\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx$ by direct
substitution), recovering the position postulate exactly. One theorem,
two courses' separately-stated rules, now visibly the same fact.

## Physical Interpretation

The unification above is the whole point of doing this rigorously rather
than treating "discrete observable" and "continuous observable" as two
different kinds of physics needing two different postulates. They were
never different: every observable's measurement statistics come from
*one* object, its PVM, and whether that PVM happens to be a staircase
(discrete spectrum) or absolutely continuous (like position) or a mix of
both (like a hydrogen atom's Hamiltonian, which has a discrete part,
the bound states, and a continuous part, the ionized/scattering
states, previewed in Green's Functions and Resolvents next) is a fact
about the *specific operator*, not a fact requiring a different
postulate each time.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking the PVM form of the spectral theorem is a *different, more
advanced* result from the finite-dimensional one.** It's the same
theorem at a more general level of generality; the finite-dimensional
$A=\sum\lambda_iP_i$ is derived above as an exact special case (a
staircase-measure PVM), not replaced or superseded.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Applying $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ to an operator with continuous
spectrum**, e.g. writing "$\hat x=\sum_x x|x\rangle\langle x|$" as an
ordinary sum. There is no discrete sum to write for $\hat x$: the
correct statement is the PVM integral $\hat x=\int x\,dE(x)$, with
$E(\Delta)=$ multiplication by $\mathbf1_\Delta(x)$, made fully precise
in the next lesson.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A projection-valued measure (PVM) $E(\lambda)$ assigns a projector to
  every real number, satisfying completeness ($E(\mathbb R)=I$) and
  consistency ($E(\Delta_1\cap\Delta_2)=E(\Delta_1)E(\Delta_2)$).
- The spectral theorem states every self-adjoint $A$ equals
  $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$ for a unique PVM (cited, not fully proved).
- For a discrete spectrum, $E(\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda_i\le\lambda}P_i$ is
  a staircase, and the Riemann–Stieltjes integral against a staircase is
  by definition a sum over its jumps, **deriving** Spectral
  Decomposition and Degeneracy's $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ as an exact
  special case, verified concretely on $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$.
- Born's rule, discrete and continuous, is one statement,
  $P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle$.
