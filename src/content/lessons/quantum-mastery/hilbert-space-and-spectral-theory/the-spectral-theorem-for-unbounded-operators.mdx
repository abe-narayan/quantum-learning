## The Sum That Was Never Justified

Spectral Decomposition and Degeneracy wrote $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ and
called it "the spectral decomposition," full stop. That is correct when
$A$'s spectrum is discrete. But the position operator
$\hat x$ has *no* eigenvalues in $L^2(\mathbb R)$ at all in the ordinary
sense (Continuous Spectra and Rigged Hilbert Space, next lesson, makes
this precise); its "eigenvalues" fill the entire real line continuously.
A sum over discrete $\lambda_i$ literally cannot be the right statement
for such an operator. This lesson gives the
<Term id="spectral-theorem-pvm">one theorem</Term> that covers both cases
at once, and the payoff is that the discrete formula was never a separate
fact: it is this general theorem evaluated on a spectrum with no
continuous part.

## From Projectors to a Measure

Replace "eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ with projector $P_i$" by "**every** real
number $\lambda$ gets an infinitesimal projector $dE(\lambda)$," and
replace the sum $\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ by an integral $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$.
The object $E(\lambda)$, a <Term id="projector">projector</Term> for *each* real number $\lambda$,
built to project onto "the part of the space with spectral value $\le
\lambda$", is called a **projection-valued measure** (PVM), and it's
the single object that makes both the discrete and continuous cases
literal instances of one formula, the same way a Riemann–Stieltjes
integral $\int f\,dG$ unifies a sum (when $G$ is a staircase function)
and an ordinary integral (when $G$ is differentiable) into one notation.

## The Projection-Valued Measure and Its Theorem

### The projection-valued measure

<DefinitionBox title="Projection-valued measure (PVM)">

A PVM assigns to every (Borel) subset $\Delta\subseteq\mathbb R$ a
projector $E(\Delta)$ on $\mathcal H$, satisfying $E(\mathbb R)=I$
(**completeness**: the whole space, summed over the whole spectrum),
$E(\Delta_1\cap\Delta_2)=E(\Delta_1)E(\Delta_2)$ (**consistency**:
projecting onto an intersection is the same as projecting onto each
piece in turn), and countable additivity for disjoint sets. It's
conventional to package this as a single projector-valued, non-decreasing
function $E(\lambda)\equiv E((-\infty,\lambda])$, the **spectral
resolution of the identity**: $E(\lambda)$ projects onto "everything with
spectral value at most $\lambda$," rising from the zero operator at
$\lambda\to-\infty$ to $I$ at $\lambda\to+\infty$.

</DefinitionBox>

### The theorem

<TheoremBox title="Spectral theorem (PVM form)" provenance="cited">

**Hypotheses.** $\mathcal H$ is a complex Hilbert space and $A$ is a
**densely defined self-adjoint** operator on it. Self-adjoint, in the
last lesson's sense: $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$, not merely $D(A)\subseteq
D(A^\dagger)$. No boundedness is assumed.

**Conclusion.** There is a unique projection-valued measure $E$ on the
Borel sets of $\mathbb R$, supported on the spectrum $\sigma(A)$, with

$$
A=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda\,dE(\lambda), \qquad D(A)=\left\{\psi\in\mathcal H:\int\lambda^2\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle<\infty\right\}
$$

where the integral is read weakly: $\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\int\lambda\,d\langle\phi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle$
for all $\phi\in\mathcal H$, $\psi\in D(A)$.

**Corollary (Borel functional calculus).** For any Borel $f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$
the same measure defines $f(A)=\int f(\lambda)\,dE(\lambda)$. Taking
$f(\lambda)=e^{-i\lambda t/\hbar}$ is where $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ acquires a
meaning for an unbounded $H$, and it is the half of Stone's theorem the
last lesson cited.

Bounded case: Hilbert (1906). Unbounded case: von Neumann (1929).
Reed–Simon, *Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics* I, §VIII.3.

</TheoremBox>

Stated here, not proved, the same way Hermitian Operators stated rather
than fully proved the existence half of the finite-dimensional spectral
theorem. What *is* derived below in full is the connection that makes
the abstract statement earn its keep: how it reproduces the
already-proven finite-dimensional result.

The load-bearing hypothesis is **self-adjoint**, not merely symmetric,
which is the distinction the last lesson built. A symmetric operator has
no PVM guaranteed to it. The half-line momentum operator makes that
concrete: having no self-adjoint extension, it has no canonical spectral
resolution either, so there is no well-defined "momentum toward the wall"
measurement statistic to compute. The two lessons meet at that point.

### Reduction to the discrete case, derived explicitly

Suppose $A$'s spectrum is purely discrete: eigenvalues $\lambda_1<
\lambda_2<\cdots$ with orthogonal projectors $P_1,P_2,\ldots$ onto their
(possibly degenerate) eigenspaces, $\sum_iP_i=I$, the setup Spectral
Decomposition and Degeneracy established.

Two hypotheses ride along silently in that sentence, and both are free in
finite dimensions. First, $\sum_iP_i=I$ can hold only in the *strong*
sense when there are infinitely many eigenvalues: the partial sums
converge on each vector, never in operator norm, since
$\|I-\sum_{i\le n}P_i\|=1$ for every $n$ as long as anything is left
over. Second, the $\lambda_i$ are assumed to have no finite accumulation
point, so that each bounded interval contains finitely many of them.
That is what lets the integral below be read as an elementary
Riemann–Stieltjes sum over jumps rather than a Lebesgue–Stieltjes
integral against a measure with infinitely many atoms in a compact set.
Both hold for the harmonic oscillator, where $\lambda_n=(n+\tfrac12)
\hbar\omega$ marches off to infinity in even steps. Neither holds for the
hydrogen Hamiltonian's bound states, where $E_n=-13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$
accumulates at $0$: the sum over jumps still converges, but only as a
Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral, and $0$ itself sits at the seam where the
discrete part meets the continuum. Define

$$
E(\lambda)\equiv\sum_{i:\,\lambda_i\le\lambda}P_i
$$

This satisfies every PVM axiom: it's non-decreasing in $\lambda$ (each term is a
projector, and projectors onto orthogonal subspaces add without
cancellation), it equals $0$ below $\lambda_1$ and rises to $I$ as
$\lambda\to+\infty$ (reaching it at and above the largest eigenvalue when
the list is finite, and only in the limit when it is not), and it satisfies the consistency condition (a direct
consequence of $P_iP_j=0$ for $i\ne j$, already proven in Spectral
Decomposition and Degeneracy). $E(\lambda)$, as a function of $\lambda$,
is a **staircase**: constant except for a jump of size $P_i$ exactly at
$\lambda=\lambda_i$.

Now evaluate $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle=\int\lambda\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle$
for this staircase measure directly:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="⟨ψ|E(λ)ψ⟩ is itself a staircase function of λ, jumping by ‖P_iψ‖² at each λ_i, which is a direct readout of the E(λ) definition above.">
    $$
    \langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle=
    \sum_{i:\lambda_i\le\lambda}\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i:\lambda_i\le\lambda}\|P_i\psi\|^2
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="A Riemann–Stieltjes integral against a pure staircase function is, by definition, exactly the sum of (integrand value at each jump) × (jump size).">
    $$
    \int\lambda\,d\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle=\sum_i\lambda_i\|P_i\psi\|^2=\sum_i\lambda_i\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle=\left\langle\psi\left|\sum_i\lambda_iP_i\right|\psi\right\rangle
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="This holds for every ψ, so the operators themselves agree. That recovers Spectral Decomposition and Degeneracy's formula, now derived as the special case where the spectral measure happens to be a staircase.">
    $$
    A=\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

### The measurement postulate, unified

Born's rule for a discrete spectrum, "$P(\text{outcome}=\lambda_i)=
\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$," and the continuous-spectrum position
postulate, "$P(x\in\Delta)=\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx$," look like two
different rules that earlier courses stated separately (one in
Operators, Observables & Measurement, one in Wave Mechanics). In PVM
language they are the **same single statement**:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "P", symbol: "P(outcome ∈ Δ)", gloss: "The probability the measured value falls in the set Δ. Discrete or continuous, the formula is the same." },
    { id: "psi", symbol: "ψ", gloss: "The quantum state being measured." },
    { id: "E", symbol: "E(Δ)", gloss: "The PVM's projector for the set Δ. It equals P_i for a discrete eigenvalue, or 'multiply by the indicator function' for a continuous set like position." },
  ]}
>

$$
P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle
$$

</EquationReveal>

For a discrete spectrum, $E(\{\lambda_i\})=P_i$, recovering the familiar
formula exactly. For the position operator, $E(\Delta)$ turns out to be
"multiply by the indicator function $\mathbf 1_\Delta(x)$" (verified
directly: this is manifestly a projector, since
$\mathbf1_\Delta(x)^2=\mathbf1_\Delta(x)$ pointwise, and
$\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle=\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx$ by direct
substitution), recovering the position postulate exactly. One theorem,
two courses' separately-stated rules, now visibly the same fact.

## The Measure Carries the Discrete/Continuous Split

<InsightBlock>
  The measure, not the operator, is where the discrete/continuous
  distinction lives. Two postulates collapse into one because the thing
  that differs between them was never the physics; it was the shape of
  $\langle\psi|E(\lambda)\psi\rangle$.
</InsightBlock>

Every observable's measurement statistics come from a single object, its
PVM. Whether that PVM is a staircase (discrete spectrum), absolutely
continuous (position), or a mix of the two is a fact about the specific
operator rather than a fact demanding its own postulate. The hydrogen
Hamiltonian is the case worth holding onto: its spectrum has a discrete
part below zero energy (the bound states, $E_n=-13.6\,\text{eV}/n^2$)
and a continuous part above (the ionized scattering states), so a single
$E(\lambda)$ for a single operator climbs in jumps and then rises
smoothly. Green's Functions and Resolvents, next, reads both parts off
the same resolvent: the jumps are poles, the smooth rise is a branch
cut.

## Reading This as a Separate, Harder Theorem

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the PVM form of the spectral theorem is a *different, more
advanced* result from the finite-dimensional one.** It is the same
theorem stated at a level of generality that admits continuous spectra;
the finite-dimensional $A=\sum\lambda_iP_i$ is derived above as an exact
special case (a staircase-measure PVM), not replaced or superseded.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Applying $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ to an operator with continuous
spectrum**, e.g. writing "$\hat x=\sum_x x|x\rangle\langle x|$" as an
ordinary sum. There is no discrete sum to write for $\hat x$: the
correct statement is the PVM integral $\hat x=\int x\,dE(x)$, with
$E(\Delta)=$ multiplication by $\mathbf1_\Delta(x)$, made fully precise
in the next lesson.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$, what is $E(\lambda)$ for $\lambda=1$
   exactly (the boundary case, using the definition
   $E(\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda_i\le\lambda}P_i$ with $\le$, not $<$)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $E(1)=P_1=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)$, the rank-two projector onto
   $\mathrm{span}\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$. The condition $\lambda_i\le1$
   is satisfied by $\lambda_1=1$ and by nothing else, so exactly one term
   survives.

   The reason the boundary case is worth asking about is that it fixes the
   staircase's *right*-continuity, which is not a stylistic choice. The
   definition box sets $E(\lambda)\equiv E((-\infty,\lambda])$, a half-open
   interval closed on the right, so the value at a jump point belongs to
   the upper step, not the lower one:

   $$
   \lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}E(1-\varepsilon)=0,\qquad E(1)=P_1,\qquad \lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}E(1+\varepsilon)=P_1 .
   $$

   That convention is what makes the jump at $\lambda_i$ equal to $P_i$,
   and it is what the Riemann-Stieltjes reduction consumes when it turns
   $\int\lambda\,dE$ into $\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$. Flip to $<$ and the
   singleton $\{\lambda_i\}$ carries no measure, the staircase never lands
   on a step, and the integral loses all of its mass.

   On the equal superposition $\psi=(|0\rangle+|1\rangle+|2\rangle)/\sqrt3$
   the scalar reading is $\langle\psi|E(1)|\psi\rangle=\|P_1\psi\|^2=
   \tfrac13+\tfrac13=\tfrac23$, the same $2/3$ that
   `pvm-outcome-probability` gets for $P(\text{outcome}\le1.5)$. It has to
   be the same number: there is no spectrum between $1$ and $1.5$, so the
   staircase is flat across that whole stretch.

   Two wrong turns. The first is answering $E(1)=0$, reading "up to
   $\lambda$" as excluding the endpoint the way an open interval would.
   The second is more common and harder to see: answering
   $P_1=|0\rangle\langle0|$, a rank-one projector, because $\lambda=1$
   appears once in the list of *distinct* eigenvalues. It is doubly
   degenerate in $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$, so $P_1$ has rank two. The
   check that catches it in one line is $\mathrm{Tr}\,E(\lambda)$, which
   counts eigenvalues at or below $\lambda$ *with multiplicity*: here
   $\mathrm{Tr}\,E(1)=2$, not $1$.

   </details>

2. Explain in one or two sentences why $E(\Delta)=$ "multiply by
   $\mathbf1_\Delta(x)$" satisfies the projector conditions
   ($E(\Delta)^2=E(\Delta)$, $E(\Delta)^\dagger=E(\Delta)$).

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Both conditions are pointwise facts about a function that only ever
   takes the values $0$ and $1$.

   Idempotence: applying the operator twice multiplies by
   $\mathbf1_\Delta(x)^2$, and $0^2=0$, $1^2=1$, so
   $\mathbf1_\Delta^2=\mathbf1_\Delta$ as a function and
   $E(\Delta)^2=E(\Delta)$ as an operator.

   Self-adjointness: $\mathbf1_\Delta$ is real-valued, so it is unchanged
   by conjugation and can be moved across the inner product freely,

   $$
   \langle\phi|E(\Delta)\psi\rangle=\int\phi^*(x)\,\mathbf1_\Delta(x)\,\psi(x)\,dx=\int\big(\mathbf1_\Delta(x)\phi(x)\big)^*\psi(x)\,dx=\langle E(\Delta)\phi|\psi\rangle .
   $$

   The consistency axiom falls out of the same observation, since
   $\mathbf1_{\Delta_1}\mathbf1_{\Delta_2}=\mathbf1_{\Delta_1\cap\Delta_2}$
   pointwise.

   What deserves a second look is the domain, given how hard the previous
   lesson worked on it. Here there is nothing to check, and that is the
   point rather than an oversight: $|\mathbf1_\Delta(x)\psi(x)|\le|\psi(x)|$
   pointwise, so $E(\Delta)$ maps $L^2(\mathbb R)$ into itself and is
   *bounded*, with $\|E(\Delta)\|\le1$. It is therefore defined on the
   whole space, and so is its adjoint:
   $D(E(\Delta))=D(E(\Delta)^\dagger)=L^2(\mathbb R)$, with no room to
   spare, so the last lesson's symmetric-versus-self-adjoint gap cannot
   open. That is precisely what the spectral theorem buys. It trades one
   unbounded, domain-fussy operator $\hat x$ for a family of bounded
   projectors on which self-adjointness is as cheap as it is in finite
   dimensions.

   The wrong turn is reaching for integration by parts, because that is
   what the last lesson's symmetry check looked like. There is no
   derivative anywhere in $E(\Delta)$ and no boundary term to produce; the
   whole verification is "the indicator is real and squares to itself." A
   second wrong turn is worrying that $\mathbf1_\Delta$ is discontinuous
   and therefore somehow inadmissible. $L^2$ elements are equivalence
   classes up to sets of measure zero, and multiplication by any bounded
   measurable function is a perfectly good bounded operator on them.

   </details>

3. Using $P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle$
   with the position operator's $E(\Delta)$, show this reduces to
   exactly $\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   One substitution, with no machinery in between:

   $$
   \langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^*(x)\big[\mathbf1_\Delta(x)\psi(x)\big]dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathbf1_\Delta(x)|\psi(x)|^2dx=\int_\Delta|\psi(x)|^2dx .
   $$

   The indicator's entire job is to be the limits of integration. It is
   $1$ on $\Delta$ and $0$ off it, so multiplying by it and integrating
   over all of $\mathbb R$ is the same operation as integrating over
   $\Delta$ alone; the last equality is the definition of what
   $\int_\Delta$ means.

   So Wave Mechanics' position postulate was never an independent axiom.
   It is $P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle$
   evaluated on one particular PVM, exactly as
   $P(\text{outcome}=\lambda_i)=\langle\psi|P_i|\psi\rangle$ is the same
   formula evaluated on a staircase one.

   Two limits are worth taking, because they are where the formula earns
   its keep. Setting $\Delta=\mathbb R$ gives
   $\langle\psi|I|\psi\rangle=\|\psi\|^2=1$, which is completeness
   $E(\mathbb R)=I$ reappearing as normalization. Setting
   $\Delta=\{x_0\}$, a single point, gives $\mathbf1_{\{x_0\}}\psi=0$ in
   $L^2$, since it differs from zero only on a set of measure zero, so
   $E(\{x_0\})$ is the *zero operator* and the probability is exactly $0$.
   That is the precise content of the claim that $\hat x$ has no
   eigenvalues: an eigenvector for $x_0$ would have to lie in the range of
   $E(\{x_0\})$, and that range is $\{0\}$. The continuous spectrum is not
   a spectrum whose eigenvectors are merely hard to write down. It is one
   with no eigenvectors in $\mathcal H$ at all.

   The wrong turn is deriving the result by inserting
   $\int|x\rangle\langle x|dx$ and writing
   $\int_\Delta|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^2dx$. It lands on the right
   expression, which is exactly why it passes unchallenged, but it assumes
   the objects this course has not yet built: $|x\rangle$ is not a vector
   of $L^2(\mathbb R)$ and $\langle x|\psi\rangle$ needs the rigged Hilbert
   space of the next lesson before it means anything. The PVM route never
   mentions $|x\rangle$, and that is the whole reason it is the rigorous
   one. A second wrong turn is reporting $P(\hat x=x_0)=|\psi(x_0)|^2$.
   That quantity is a probability *density*, with units of inverse length;
   the probability of a single point is $0$, as the computation above
   shows outright.

   </details>

## Summary

- A projection-valued measure (PVM) $E(\lambda)$ assigns a projector to
  every real number, satisfying completeness ($E(\mathbb R)=I$) and
  consistency ($E(\Delta_1\cap\Delta_2)=E(\Delta_1)E(\Delta_2)$).
- The spectral theorem states every self-adjoint $A$ equals
  $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$ for a unique PVM (cited, not fully proved).
- For a discrete spectrum, $E(\lambda)=\sum_{\lambda_i\le\lambda}P_i$ is
  a staircase, and the Riemann–Stieltjes integral against a staircase is
  by definition a sum over its jumps, **deriving** Spectral
  Decomposition and Degeneracy's $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ as an exact
  special case, verified concretely on $N=\mathrm{diag}(1,1,2)$.
- Born's rule, discrete and continuous, is one statement,
  $P(\text{outcome}\in\Delta)=\langle\psi|E(\Delta)|\psi\rangle$.
