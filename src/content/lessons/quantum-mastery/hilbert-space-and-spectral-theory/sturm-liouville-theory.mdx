## Motivation

The Infinite Square Well, the Finite Square Well, the Harmonic Oscillator,
and the Radial Equation all produced eigenvalue problems that were proved,
separately, in each lesson, to have real energies and orthogonal
eigenfunctions, each proof redoing essentially the same integration by
parts. **Sturm-Liouville theory** is the observation that all four proofs
are literally the same argument, applied to the same general class of
differential operator, differing only in three functions ($p,q,w$) and a
boundary condition. Prove the general theorem once, and every solved
potential in this platform's entire curriculum inherits it for free.

## Intuition

Every 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\psi''+V(x)\psi=E\psi$, is already a special case (with $p=\hbar^2/2m$
constant, $q=V(x)$, $w=1$) of a more general-looking eigenvalue equation
that also happens to cover the hydrogen radial equation and dozens of
other classical eigenvalue problems (Legendre's equation, Bessel's
equation) with different, position-dependent $p,q,w$. The
**Sturm-Liouville operator** is the common template; Hermitian
Operators' finite-dimensional "if $A=A^\dagger$ then real eigenvalues,
orthogonal eigenvectors" theorem is the same statement, applied here to
an *infinite*-dimensional operator built from derivatives instead of a
matrix.

## Mathematical Development

### The Sturm-Liouville form

A **regular Sturm-Liouville problem** on $[a,b]$ is the eigenvalue
equation

$$
(p(x)y')' - q(x)y + \lambda\,w(x)y = 0
$$

with $p(x)>0$ and $w(x)>0$ on $(a,b)$, together with boundary conditions
at $a$ and $b$ that make a specific boundary term (derived below)
vanish, most commonly Dirichlet ($y=0$), Neumann ($y'=0$), a mixed
Robin condition, or (for a *singular* endpoint, where $p$ or $w$
vanishes or the interval is unbounded) simply requiring $y$ stay finite
or square-integrable there, exactly the domain-choice question Hilbert
Spaces and Self-Adjointness raised in full generality.

### Lagrange's identity, derived

Let $y_1,y_2$ both solve the equation, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,
\lambda_2$: $(py_1')'-qy_1=-\lambda_1wy_1$ and $(py_2')'-qy_2=
-\lambda_2wy_2$. Multiply the first by $y_2$, the second by $y_1$, and
subtract:

$$
y_2(py_1')'-y_1(py_2')' = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\,w\,y_1y_2
$$

The left side is a total derivative (checked directly by the product
rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\big[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')\big]=p'(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')+
p(y_2'y_1'+y_2y_1''-y_1'y_2'-y_1y_2'')=y_2(py_1')'-y_1(py_2')'$, using
$(py')'=p'y'+py''$ twice). So:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\Big[p\big(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2'\big)\Big] = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\,w\,y_1y_2
$$

Integrating from $a$ to $b$:

$$
\Big[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')\Big]_a^b = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\int_a^bw\,y_1y_2\,dx
$$

### Orthogonality, derived

If the boundary conditions make the left side vanish (Dirichlet or
Neumann individually kill each term at each endpoint; a singular
endpoint with $p(a)=0$ or $p(b)=0$ kills it regardless of $y$'s value
there), then for $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$:

$$
\int_a^bw(x)\,y_1(x)\,y_2(x)\,dx = 0
$$

**Eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal with
respect to the weight $w$**, the direct Sturm-Liouville generalization
of Hermitian Operators' finite-dimensional orthogonality theorem, proved
by exactly the same "compute the same bracket two ways, subtract"
strategy, now with a boundary term instead of a discrete difference.

### Reality of eigenvalues, derived the same way

Apply the identical calculation to $y$ and its own complex conjugate
$y^*$ (both solve the same real-coefficient equation if $y$ does, with
eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\lambda^*$ respectively):

$$
\Big[p(y^*y'-yy^{*\prime})\Big]_a^b = (\lambda^*-\lambda)\int_a^bw\,|y|^2dx
$$

The same boundary conditions again kill the left side; since $w>0$ and
$\int|y|^2w\,dx>0$ for $y\ne0$, this forces $\lambda^*=\lambda$:
**every Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue is real.**

### Completeness (cited)

That the eigenfunctions additionally form a *complete* basis (every
sufficiently nice function on $[a,b]$ expands in them, the direct
generalization of "a Hermitian matrix's eigenvectors span the whole
space") is the **Sturm-Liouville expansion theorem**, a substantially
harder result requiring genuine functional-analytic machinery (compact
resolvent operators, building on Green's Functions and Resolvents'
$(E-H)^{-1}$); **cited here, not proved**, exactly as the general
spectral theorem was cited two lessons ago. What's been fully derived
above (orthogonality, reality) is already everything Hermitian Operators
proved for matrices; completeness is the one truly new ingredient,
and it's the infinite-dimensional analogue of the same existence
statement the finite-dimensional spectral theorem also had to assume
rather than construct from scratch.

**The infinite square well** ($-a<x<a$, $\hbar=m=1$): $-y''=\lambda y$
is already in Sturm-Liouville form with $p=1,q=0,w=1$, $\lambda=2E$,
Dirichlet boundary conditions $y(\pm a)=0$.

**The hydrogen radial equation** (The Radial Equation, $u(r)=rR(r)$,
$\hbar=m=1$): $-u''+\big[2V_{\text{eff}}(r)\big]u=2Eu$ rearranges to
$-u''+2V_{\text{eff}}(r)u-2Eu=0$, i.e. Sturm-Liouville form with $p=1$,
$q(r)=2V_{\text{eff}}(r)=2\big(V(r)+l(l+1)/2r^2\big)$, $w=1$, $\lambda=2E$,
on the singular interval $(0,\infty)$, with boundary condition $u(0)=0$
at the singular origin (exactly the physically-forced domain choice
Hilbert Spaces and Self-Adjointness's half-line discussion showed can be
subtle; here $p(0)=1\ne0$, so this isn't the same pathology as the bare
momentum operator, but it's the same *kind* of question: what condition
at a boundary makes the boundary term vanish) and $u\to0$ as
$r\to\infty$ (the natural condition at the other, unbounded, singular
end).

| | Infinite well | Hydrogen radial ($u=rR$) |
|---|---|---|
| Interval | $(-a,a)$, regular | $(0,\infty)$, singular at both ends |
| $p(x)$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $q(x)$ | $0$ | $2V_{\text{eff}}(r)=2\big({-}1/r+l(l+1)/2r^2\big)$ (atomic units) |
| $w(x)$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| Eigenvalue $\lambda$ | $2E$ | $2E$ |
| Boundary conditions | $y(\pm a)=0$ | $u(0)=0$, $u\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ |

Same $p$, same $w$, same eigenvalue convention: only $q(x)$ (whether
the potential is a flat zero or a Coulomb-plus-centrifugal well) and the
interval actually differ. Visually, the two potentials that go into
$q$ look nothing alike:

## Physical Interpretation

Every "prove the eigenfunctions are orthogonal" step scattered across
this platform's curriculum (the infinite well's $\sin$ functions, the
harmonic oscillator's Hermite functions, hydrogen's radial functions)
was never a series of independent lucky facts about each specific
potential. They're all forced by the *same* general mechanism: a
self-adjoint boundary-value structure that makes one boundary term
vanish, at which point orthogonality and reality follow from pure
algebra, regardless of how complicated $q(x)$ (the potential) itself
is. This is precisely why an eigenvalue-problem-solving strategy that
worked for the harmonic oscillator was always a safe bet to try again on
hydrogen: not optimism, a theorem.

## Worked Example

**Verify the infinite well's eigenfunctions are actually orthogonal,
using this platform's own `infiniteSquareWellEigenstate` engine
function, independent of the general proof above.**

For $a=1$, computing $\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3$ via
`infiniteSquareWellEigenstate` on a real numerical grid and taking their
pairwise `innerProduct`s directly: $\langle\psi_1|\psi_2\rangle$,
$\langle\psi_1|\psi_3\rangle$, and $\langle\psi_2|\psi_3\rangle$ all come
out on the order of $10^{-16}$ (floating-point roundoff around exactly
zero), while each state's own norm is $1.000\ldots$ to the same
precision. This is the Sturm-Liouville orthogonality theorem's
prediction, $\int_{-a}^aw(x)\psi_m\psi_n\,dx=0$ for $m\ne n$ (here
$w=1$), confirmed by a completely independent numerical computation,
not the boundary-term algebra above, an actual sum over grid points,
landing on exactly zero to machine precision, exactly this platform's
established way of checking a derivation (compare the Chapman-Kolmogorov
numerical check in Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics).

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the weight $w(x)$ is always $1$.** It's $1$ for every example
in this lesson (and every potential this platform's core curriculum
solves), but other Sturm-Liouville problems (Legendre's
equation, Bessel's equation, both outside this course's scope) have
nontrivial $w(x)\ne1$, and the orthogonality statement is with respect
to *that* weight: $\int w\,y_my_n\,dx=0$, not $\int y_my_n\,dx=0$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the hydrogen radial equation "happens to look similar" to
the infinite well's equation, coincidentally.** The table above shows
it's not a resemblance: both are literally the same Sturm-Liouville
operator template with $p=w=1$, differing only in $q(x)$ and the
boundary conditions their respective domains force.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A regular Sturm-Liouville problem is $(py')'-qy+\lambda wy=0$ with
  $p,w>0$ and boundary conditions making a specific boundary term
  vanish.
- Lagrange's identity, $[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')]_a^b=(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)
  \int w y_1y_2\,dx$, derived directly from the product rule, gives both
  orthogonality (different eigenvalues) and reality (applying the same
  identity to $y$ and $y^*$) in a few lines each.
- Completeness of the eigenbasis is cited, the one deep
  ingredient, paralleling the finite-dimensional spectral theorem's own
  existence assumption.
- The infinite well ($q=0$) and the hydrogen radial equation
  ($q=2V_{\text{eff}}(r)$) are the *same* Sturm-Liouville problem with
  different $q$ and boundary conditions, not two separately-lucky
  solvable systems, verified independently on the well's eigenfunctions
  via this platform's real engine, to machine precision.
