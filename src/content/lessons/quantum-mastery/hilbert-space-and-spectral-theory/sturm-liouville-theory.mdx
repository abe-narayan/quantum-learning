## One Theorem Behind Every Solved Potential

The Infinite Square Well, the Finite Square Well, the Harmonic Oscillator,
and the Radial Equation all produced eigenvalue problems that were proved,
separately, in each lesson, to have real energies and orthogonal
eigenfunctions, each proof redoing what is very nearly the same
integration by parts. **<Term id="sturm-liouville-theory">Sturm-Liouville theory</Term>** is the observation that all four proofs
are literally the same argument, applied to the same general class of
differential operator, differing only in three functions ($p,q,w$) and a
boundary condition. Prove the general theorem once, and every solved
potential in this platform's entire curriculum inherits it for free.

## Every 1D Schrodinger Equation in One Form

Every 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}
\psi''+V(x)\psi=E\psi$, is already a special case (with $p=\hbar^2/2m$
constant, $q=V(x)$, $w=1$) of a more general-looking eigenvalue equation
that also happens to cover the hydrogen radial equation and dozens of
other classical eigenvalue problems (Legendre's equation, Bessel's
equation) with different, position-dependent $p,q,w$. The
**Sturm-Liouville operator** is the common template; Hermitian
Operators' finite-dimensional "if $A=A^\dagger$ then real eigenvalues,
orthogonal eigenvectors" theorem is the same statement, applied here to
an *infinite*-dimensional operator built from derivatives instead of a
matrix.

## Lagrange's Identity, and What Follows From It

### The Sturm-Liouville form

A **regular Sturm-Liouville problem** on $[a,b]$ is the eigenvalue
equation

$$
(p(x)y')' - q(x)y + \lambda\,w(x)y = 0
$$

with $p(x)>0$ and $w(x)>0$ on $(a,b)$, together with boundary conditions
at $a$ and $b$ that make a specific boundary term (derived below)
vanish, most commonly Dirichlet ($y=0$), Neumann ($y'=0$), a mixed
Robin condition, or (for a *singular* endpoint, where $p$ or $w$
vanishes or the interval is unbounded) simply requiring $y$ stay finite
or square-integrable there, the domain-choice question Hilbert
Spaces and Self-Adjointness raised in full generality.

### Lagrange's identity, derived

Let $y_1,y_2$ both solve the equation, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,
\lambda_2$: $(py_1')'-qy_1=-\lambda_1wy_1$ and $(py_2')'-qy_2=
-\lambda_2wy_2$. Multiply the first by $y_2$, the second by $y_1$, and
subtract:

$$
y_2(py_1')'-y_1(py_2')' = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\,w\,y_1y_2
$$

The left side is a total derivative (checked directly by the product
rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\big[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')\big]=p'(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')+
p(y_2'y_1'+y_2y_1''-y_1'y_2'-y_1y_2'')=y_2(py_1')'-y_1(py_2')'$, using
$(py')'=p'y'+py''$ twice). So:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\Big[p\big(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2'\big)\Big] = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\,w\,y_1y_2
$$

Integrating from $a$ to $b$:

$$
\Big[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')\Big]_a^b = (\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\int_a^bw\,y_1y_2\,dx
$$

### Orthogonality, derived

If the boundary conditions make the left side vanish (Dirichlet or
Neumann individually kill each term at each endpoint; a singular
endpoint with $p(a)=0$ or $p(b)=0$ kills it as long as $y$ stays
bounded and $py'\to0$ there, which is exactly what the finiteness or
square-integrability condition at a singular endpoint is imposed to
secure), then for $\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2$:

$$
\int_a^bw(x)\,y_1(x)\,y_2(x)\,dx = 0
$$

**Eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal with
respect to the weight $w$**, the direct Sturm-Liouville generalization
of Hermitian Operators' finite-dimensional orthogonality theorem, proved
by the same "compute the same bracket two ways, subtract"
strategy, now with a boundary term instead of a discrete difference.

### Reality of eigenvalues, derived the same way

Apply the identical calculation to $y$ and its own complex conjugate
$y^*$ (both solve the same real-coefficient equation if $y$ does, with
eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\lambda^*$ respectively):

$$
\Big[p(y^*y'-yy^{*\prime})\Big]_a^b = (\lambda^*-\lambda)\int_a^bw\,|y|^2dx
$$

The same boundary conditions again kill the left side; since $w>0$ and
$\int|y|^2w\,dx>0$ for $y\ne0$, this forces $\lambda^*=\lambda$:
**every Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue is real.**

One hypothesis in that argument is easy to miss. The step "$y^*$ solves
the same equation" needs $p,q,w$ real, and the step "the same boundary
conditions kill the left side" needs the *boundary conditions* to be real
as well. A Robin condition $y'(a)=\alpha y(a)$ with complex $\alpha$ is
not satisfied by $y^*$, the boundary term does not vanish, and the
operator is not self-adjoint. Real coefficients and real boundary data
are doing work here, not decoration.

Stating both results together, with everything they assume:

<TheoremBox title="Regular Sturm-Liouville theorem" provenance="derived">

**Hypotheses.** A *finite* interval $[a,b]$; $p\in C^1$ and $q,w$
continuous and real; $p(x)>0$ and $w(x)>0$ on all of $[a,b]$, endpoints
included; real separated boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, or Robin
with real coefficients) at each end.

**Conclusions.** The eigenvalues are real, simple, and form an increasing
sequence $\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\cdots\to+\infty$ with no finite
accumulation point. Eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal
in the weighted inner product $\int_a^b w\,y_my_n\,dx$, and together they
form a complete orthogonal basis of $L^2([a,b],w\,dx)$. The spectrum is
**purely discrete**.

Orthogonality and reality are derived above; completeness is cited below.
Standard reference: Coddington–Levinson, *Theory of Ordinary Differential
Equations*, ch. 7–8.

</TheoremBox>

### Completeness (cited)

That the eigenfunctions additionally form a *complete* basis (every
sufficiently nice function on $[a,b]$ expands in them, the direct
generalization of "a Hermitian matrix's eigenvectors span the whole
space") is the **Sturm-Liouville expansion theorem**, a substantially
harder result requiring substantial functional-analytic machinery (compact
resolvent operators, building on Green's Functions and Resolvents'
$(E-H)^{-1}$); **cited here, not proved**, the same way the general
spectral theorem was cited two lessons ago. What's been fully derived
above (orthogonality, reality) is already everything Hermitian Operators
proved for matrices; completeness is the one truly new ingredient,
and it's the infinite-dimensional analogue of the same existence
statement the finite-dimensional spectral theorem also had to assume
rather than construct from scratch.

**The infinite square well** ($-a<x<a$, $\hbar=m=1$): $-y''=\lambda y$
is already in Sturm-Liouville form with $p=1,q=0,w=1$, $\lambda=2E$,
Dirichlet boundary conditions $y(\pm a)=0$.

**The hydrogen radial equation** (The Radial Equation, $u(r)=rR(r)$,
$\hbar=m=1$): $-u''+\big[2V_{\text{eff}}(r)\big]u=2Eu$ rearranges to
$-u''+2V_{\text{eff}}(r)u-2Eu=0$, i.e. Sturm-Liouville form with $p=1$,
$q(r)=2V_{\text{eff}}(r)=2\big(V(r)+l(l+1)/2r^2\big)$, $w=1$, $\lambda=2E$,
on the singular interval $(0,\infty)$, with boundary condition $u(0)=0$
at the singular origin (the physically-forced domain choice
Hilbert Spaces and Self-Adjointness's half-line discussion showed can be
subtle; here $p(0)=1\ne0$, so this isn't the same pathology as the bare
momentum operator, but it's the same *kind* of question: what condition
at a boundary makes the boundary term vanish) and $u\to0$ as
$r\to\infty$ (the natural condition at the other, unbounded, singular
end).

| | Infinite well | Hydrogen radial ($u=rR$) |
|---|---|---|
| Interval | $(-a,a)$, regular | $(0,\infty)$, singular at both ends |
| $p(x)$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| $q(x)$ | $0$ | $2V_{\text{eff}}(r)=2\big({-}1/r+l(l+1)/2r^2\big)$ (atomic units) |
| $w(x)$ | $1$ | $1$ |
| Eigenvalue $\lambda$ | $2E$ | $2E$ |
| Boundary conditions | $y(\pm a)=0$ | $u(0)=0$, $u\to0$ as $r\to\infty$ |

Same $p$, same $w$, same eigenvalue convention: only $q(x)$ (whether
the potential is a flat zero or a Coulomb-plus-centrifugal well) and the
interval differ. Visually, the two potentials that go into
$q$ look nothing alike:

### Where the regular theorem stops, and hydrogen is the example

Orthogonality and reality carry over to the singular problems unchanged.
**Completeness does not**, and the difference is not a technicality.

The regular theorem concluded a purely discrete spectrum. Hydrogen
violates that conclusion. The radial problem on $(0,\infty)$ is singular
at both ends (the interval is unbounded, and $q(r)$ blows up at the
origin), and its spectrum has two parts: the bound states
$E_n=-1/(2n^2)$ in atomic units, discrete and accumulating at $0$, plus a
**continuous** spectrum filling $E\geq0$, the ionized scattering states.
The bound states are orthogonal but *not complete*. No expansion in
$\{u_n\}$ alone can represent a free electron. Completeness returns only
when the scattering states are included, and the statement that then holds
is the spectral theorem's $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$ with both a staircase
and a continuous part: the mixed spectrum sketched two lessons ago,
appearing here in the course's own worked example.

Two further conclusions of the regular theorem also lapse. The
eigenvalues accumulate at $E=0$ instead of marching to $+\infty$, and
"increasing sequence with no finite accumulation point" fails with them.
The reliable summary is that what this lesson *proves* (orthogonality,
reality) is robust under singularity, while what it *cites* (completeness
of a discrete eigenbasis) is precisely what needs the finite interval and
the non-vanishing $p,w$ at the endpoints.

## Why Orthogonality Never Needed Rechecking

Every "prove the eigenfunctions are orthogonal" step scattered across
this platform's curriculum (the infinite well's $\sin$ functions, the
harmonic oscillator's Hermite functions, hydrogen's radial functions)
was never a series of independent lucky facts about each specific
potential. They're all forced by the *same* general mechanism: a
self-adjoint boundary-value structure that makes one boundary term
vanish, at which point orthogonality and reality follow from pure
algebra, regardless of how complicated $q(x)$ (the potential) itself
is. This is why an eigenvalue-problem-solving strategy that
worked for the harmonic oscillator was always a safe bet to try again on
hydrogen: not optimism, a theorem.

For $a=1$, computing $\psi_1,\psi_2,\psi_3$ via

Two routes, no shared step, same answer.

## Assuming Regularity on a Singular Interval

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the weight $w(x)$ is always $1$.** It's $1$ for every example
in this lesson (and every potential this platform's core curriculum
solves), but other Sturm-Liouville problems (Legendre's
equation, Bessel's equation, both outside this course's scope) have
nontrivial $w(x)\ne1$, and the orthogonality statement is with respect
to *that* weight: $\int w\,y_my_n\,dx=0$, not $\int y_my_n\,dx=0$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking the hydrogen radial equation "happens to look similar" to
the infinite well's equation, coincidentally.** The table above shows
it's not a resemblance: both are literally the same Sturm-Liouville
operator template with $p=w=1$, differing only in $q(x)$ and the
boundary conditions their respective domains force.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Write out $p,q,w$ explicitly for the quantum harmonic oscillator's
   equation $-y''+m^2\omega^2x^2y=2mEy$ ($\hbar=1$, with the mass $m$
   kept explicit), and identify $\lambda$ in terms of $E$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The canonical form is $(py')'-qy+\lambda wy=0$, with a $+$ in front of
   $(py')'$, so the first move is to multiply through by $-1$:

   $$
   -y''+m^2\omega^2x^2y-2mEy=0
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   (1\cdot y')'-\big(m^2\omega^2x^2\big)y+(2mE)(1)\,y=0 .
   $$

   Reading off: $p(x)=1$, $q(x)=m^2\omega^2x^2$, $w(x)=1$, and
   $\lambda=2mE$.

   Check it on the ground state rather than trusting the rearrangement.
   With $\hbar=1$ the ground state is $y_0\propto e^{-m\omega x^2/2}$, so
   $y_0''=\big(-m\omega+m^2\omega^2x^2\big)y_0$ and

   $$
   -y_0''+m^2\omega^2x^2y_0=m\omega\,y_0=2m\left(\frac{\omega}{2}\right)y_0=2mE_0\,y_0,
   $$

   using $E_0=\omega/2$. The mass survives on both sides. Numerically, at
   $m=2,\omega=1,x=0.7$ the left side is $1.22525$ and $2mE_0y_0$ is
   $1.22525$, while $2E_0y_0$ is $0.61263$, low by exactly the factor $m$.

   The spectrum in these variables is
   $\lambda_n=2mE_n=2m\omega\left(n+\tfrac12\right)=m\omega(2n+1)$:
   increasing, evenly spaced, with no finite accumulation point, which is
   what a discrete Sturm-Liouville spectrum is supposed to look like.

   One honesty note about which theorem applies. This problem has
   $p=w=1>0$, but it lives on all of $\mathbb R$, so the interval is
   unbounded and the boxed *regular* theorem does not cover it. It is a
   singular problem. Orthogonality and reality still carry over, because
   those two proofs only need the boundary term to vanish and Gaussian
   decay secures that at both ends. Discreteness and completeness do hold
   here as well, but they hold as facts about this operator, not as
   consequences of the regular theorem, exactly the distinction hydrogen
   is used to make later in the lesson.

   The wrong turn is reporting $\lambda=2E$, importing the convention
   from the well and hydrogen. Both of those were stated with
   $\hbar=m=1$, so their mass was already gone; here it was deliberately
   kept, and $q=m^2\omega^2x^2$ is itself the signal that $m$ has not
   been set to one. The second wrong turn is skipping the multiplication
   by $-1$ and reading off $p=-1$ from $-y''$. The tell is immediate:
   $p>0$ is a hypothesis of every theorem in this lesson, so any answer
   with $p=-1$ has broken the framework before it starts. Multiplying
   through also flips $q$, and a student who flips $p$ but not $q$ ends up
   with $q=-m^2\omega^2x^2$, an inverted oscillator with no bound states
   at all.

   </details>

2. Explain in one sentence why $p(a)=0$ at a singular endpoint (rather
   than a Dirichlet or Neumann condition) is enough to kill the boundary
   term in Lagrange's identity *provided $y$ stays bounded and
   $py'\to0$ there*, using the identity's exact form
   $[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')]_a^b$, and give an example of what would go wrong
   without that proviso.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In one sentence: regroup the bracket as
   $y_2\,(py_1')-y_1\,(py_2')$, and each product is a bounded factor
   times a factor going to zero, so the whole term vanishes at that
   endpoint.

   The regrouping is the entire content, and it is worth saying why the
   obvious alternative argument fails. One would like to say "$p(a)=0$
   multiplied by something bounded is zero," but at a singular endpoint
   $y'$ is typically *not* bounded; that is most of what makes the
   endpoint singular. The quantity under control is the combination
   $py'$, which is why the identity has to be read with $p$ distributed
   onto the derivatives rather than left outside.

   What goes wrong without the proviso is best seen in Legendre's
   equation on $[-1,1]$, where $p(x)=1-x^2$ vanishes at both ends. Take
   $y_1=P_0=1$, so $py_1'=0$, and for $y_2$ take the *second* solution
   $Q_0(x)=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}$. Then $Q_0'=1/(1-x^2)$, so

   $$
   p\,Q_0'=(1-x^2)\cdot\frac{1}{1-x^2}=1
   $$

   identically. The boundary term is
   $y_2(py_1')-y_1(py_2')=Q_0\cdot0-1\cdot1=-1$, and it is $-1$ at every
   $x$, not merely in the limit: evaluated at $x=0.9$, $0.999$ and
   $0.999999$ it returns exactly $-1$ each time. Orthogonality fails
   outright. The repair is the boundary condition "bounded at $\pm1$,"
   which excludes $Q_0$ because $Q_0$ diverges logarithmically there, and
   that is precisely why the Legendre *polynomials* are the physically
   admissible solutions.

   Notice what that example proves about the proviso's two halves.
   $pQ_0'$ is perfectly bounded, in fact constant, and the term still
   does not vanish. Boundedness of $py'$ is not the hypothesis; its
   *vanishing* is, and $Q_0$ fails the other half by being unbounded
   itself. Both halves are load-bearing, and the seductive error is to
   remember only the milder one.

   There is a further case that connects straight back to Hilbert Spaces
   and Self-Adjointness. Consider $(\sqrt x\,y')'+\lambda y=0$ on
   $(0,1)$, where $p(0)=0$. Setting $v=\sqrt x\,y'$ gives
   $v'=-\lambda y$, so $v$ tends to a finite limit $v_0$ and
   $y\approx y(0)+2v_0\sqrt x$ near the origin: *both* local behaviours
   are bounded, and $py'\to v_0$ need not be zero. Here "require $y$
   bounded" fails to single out a domain at all, and a genuine boundary
   condition has to be imposed by hand. That is the limit-circle case,
   and it is the same deficiency-index question von Neumann's theorem
   answered for the half-line momentum operator, arriving now through a
   weight function instead of a wall.

   </details>

3. The reality-of-eigenvalues derivation used $w>0$. Where exactly would
   that derivation break down if $w$ could be negative somewhere on
   $(a,b)$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   At exactly one step: the division. The derivation ends at

   $$
   0=\Big[p(y^*y'-yy^{*\prime})\Big]_a^b=(\lambda^*-\lambda)\int_a^bw\,|y|^2dx,
   $$

   and concluding $\lambda^*=\lambda$ requires cancelling the integral,
   which is legal only if $\int_a^bw|y|^2dx\ne0$. With $w>0$ throughout
   and $y\not\equiv0$, the integrand is non-negative and strictly
   positive on a set of positive measure, so the integral is strictly
   positive and the cancellation is safe. Let $w$ change sign and
   $w|y|^2$ takes both signs, its positive and negative parts can cancel
   exactly, and for such a $y$ the identity reads $(\lambda^*-\lambda)
   \cdot0=0$, which every complex $\lambda$ satisfies.

   The failure mode is worth stating precisely, because it is not that
   the derivation gives a wrong answer. It gives *no* answer. The
   identity remains true and simply stops constraining $\lambda$. This is
   not hypothetical either: indefinite-weight Sturm-Liouville problems
   genuinely admit non-real eigenvalues, always in conjugate pairs, and
   an eigenfunction attached to one of them must satisfy
   $\int w|y|^2dx=0$, since $\lambda^*\ne\lambda$ leaves no alternative.
   The vanishing integral is not a loophole in the proof, it is the
   signature of the eigenfunctions the proof was silently excluding.

   The wrong turn is answering that a negative $w$ makes the integral
   negative and so breaks the cancellation. Dividing by a negative number
   is perfectly legal; only $0$ is forbidden. If $w<0$ on *all* of
   $(a,b)$ nothing breaks at all: substitute $\tilde w=-w>0$ and
   $\tilde\lambda=-\lambda$ and you have an ordinary positive-weight
   problem, so $\tilde\lambda$ is real and therefore so is $\lambda$. The
   danger is an *indefinite* weight, one that changes sign inside the
   interval, not a uniformly negative one. Reading the hypothesis as
   "positive" when what it really controls is "of one sign" is the
   specific slip to avoid.

   A second wrong turn is assuming orthogonality is unaffected because
   Lagrange's identity still holds. The identity does still give
   $\int w\,y_1y_2\,dx=0$ for distinct real eigenvalues, but $w$ no
   longer defines an inner product, only an indefinite bilinear form, so
   the ambient space is a Krein space rather than a Hilbert space. A
   nonzero eigenfunction can then be orthogonal to itself, which no
   Hilbert space permits, and it cannot be normalized. Positivity of $w$
   is doing two jobs, keeping this one denominator away from zero and
   making $\int w\,\bar y z\,dx$ an inner product in the first place, and
   only the first of them is visible in the line where the proof breaks.

   </details>

## Summary

- A regular Sturm-Liouville problem is $(py')'-qy+\lambda wy=0$ with
  $p,w>0$ and boundary conditions making a specific boundary term
  vanish.
- Lagrange's identity, $[p(y_2y_1'-y_1y_2')]_a^b=(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)
  \int w y_1y_2\,dx$, derived directly from the product rule, gives both
  orthogonality (different eigenvalues) and reality (applying the same
  identity to $y$ and $y^*$) in a few lines each.
- Completeness of the eigenbasis is cited, the one deep ingredient, and
  the one carrying the most hypotheses: a finite interval with $p,w>0$ at
  both endpoints. Drop those and a discrete basis can be incomplete, as
  hydrogen's scattering continuum shows.
- The infinite well ($q=0$) and the hydrogen radial equation
  ($q=2V_{\text{eff}}(r)$) are the *same* Sturm-Liouville problem with
  different $q$ and boundary conditions, not two separately-lucky
  solvable systems, verified independently on the well's eigenfunctions
  to machine precision. What they share is orthogonality and reality;
  completeness of a purely discrete basis belongs to the regular case
  only.
