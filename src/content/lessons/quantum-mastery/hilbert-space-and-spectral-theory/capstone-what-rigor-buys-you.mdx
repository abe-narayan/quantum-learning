## Motivation

Five lessons ago, this course opened by pointing out that "check
$A=A^\dagger$" was never quite the whole story. It's worth asking,
now that the whole story has actually been told, an uncomfortable
question: **did any of it matter?** Every prediction the earlier
curriculum made using the informal shortcuts was correct. This capstone
answers *why*: not "physicists got lucky," but "the specific systems
this platform teaches all happen to sit exactly in the regime where the
informal shortcut and the rigorous theorem agree," and closes by being
honest about the one place they can come apart.

## Four shortcuts, reexamined

### 1. "Check $\hat p=\hat p^\dagger$ by integration by parts, done."

Position and Momentum Operators did exactly this and stopped. Hilbert
Spaces and Self-Adjointness showed the full statement needs
$D(\hat p)=D(\hat p^\dagger)$, not just the boundary-term calculation,
and then *derived*, via the Fourier-transform equivalence to a
self-adjoint multiplication operator, that this stronger condition
genuinely holds on the whole line. The shortcut wasn't wrong; it was
incomplete in a way that happened not to matter for $\hat p$ on
$\mathbb R$, because the missing piece (domain equality) turns out to
hold there. It's *not* incomplete in a way that doesn't matter
everywhere; the half-line worked example proved that directly.

### 2. "Write $A=\sum_i\lambda_iP_i$ and move on."

Spectral Decomposition and Degeneracy stated this as *the* spectral
decomposition. The Spectral Theorem for Unbounded Operators showed it's
one special case (a staircase-measure projection-valued measure) of a
strictly more general theorem that also has to cover $\hat x$'s
continuous spectrum. The discrete formula was never wrong for a discrete
spectrum: it's *exactly* what the general integral $\int\lambda\,dE(\lambda)$
reduces to whenever $E(\lambda)$ happens to be a staircase, derived in
full two lessons ago, not merely asserted to be analogous.

### 3. "Use $|p\rangle$ like any other ket: expand in it, integrate over it."

Momentum Space and the Fourier Transform did this freely, and Continuous
Spectra and Rigged Hilbert Space showed $|p\rangle\notin L^2(\mathbb R)$
outright, yet every manipulation built on it was still valid, because
the Gelfand triple $\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'$ makes $|p\rangle$
a fully legitimate object of a different, precisely-defined kind. Using
it "like any other ket" was fine specifically because $\langle
p|\psi\rangle$ is well-defined for every physically realizable $\psi$
(built from Schwartz test functions), which is all any actual
calculation ever needed.

### 4. "Match $\psi,\psi'$ at the boundary, solve, done."

The Finite Square Well and every other solved potential matched
boundary conditions and moved directly to the answer. Sturm-Liouville
Theory showed this always works, for any of these specific potentials,
because each one is secretly the same self-adjoint boundary-value
structure (Lagrange's identity's boundary term vanishing), guaranteeing
real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenfunctions *before* a single line of
the specific potential's algebra is done. Green's Functions and
Resolvents showed the same domain choice is exactly what places the
resolvent's poles at genuine, physical bound-state energies.

## Why none of it ever went wrong

There's a real pattern across all four cases, worth stating directly:
**every boundary/domain condition the earlier curriculum picked by
physical instinct, normalizable at infinity, finite at the origin,
zero at a hard wall, is *exactly* the condition this course's theorems
independently identify as the one making the operator genuinely
self-adjoint.** That's not a coincidence to marvel at either; physically
sensible conditions (finite total probability, finite energy) and
mathematically self-adjoint conditions are pointing at the same target
for exactly the reason Hilbert Spaces and Self-Adjointness's boundary-term
derivations make explicit: both are, underneath, statements about a
boundary contribution vanishing. Physical finiteness kills it by making
the wavefunction small at the boundary; self-adjointness needs it killed
for the same algebraic reason. They agree because they're answering
close to the same question from two directions.

## The one honest exception

This agreement is not a theorem that always holds: Hilbert Spaces and
Self-Adjointness's half-line momentum operator is the genuine
counterexample, not a decorative one. A particle confined to a half-line
by a hard wall has *no* self-adjoint momentum operator at all, for any
domain choice: deficiency indices $(1,0)$, unequal, no self-adjoint
extension exists. The earlier curriculum never collided with this
because it never needed a *momentum* operator on a half-line: the
finite well, the infinite well, and hydrogen's radial equation all use
position-basis Hamiltonians (built from $\hat p^2$, not $\hat p$ itself,
and on the whole line or with $u(0)=0$, not a bare half-line momentum
operator), which sidesteps the problem entirely. A genuinely different
question, "what is the momentum-toward-the-wall observable for a
particle in a box?", would have run straight into this, and no amount
of physical intuition about "just require $\psi(0)=0$" would have
produced a consistent answer, because none exists. Rigor's actual payoff
isn't retroactively validating what already worked; it's knowing, in
advance, which future questions are the ones where instinct alone
wouldn't be enough.

## Everything, from four directions, once more

The infinite well's ladder $E_n=n^2\pi^2/8a^2$ (natural units, $a=1$)
has now been reached, across this course, from every angle: directly
solving $-y''=2Ey$ (Wave Mechanics, before this course began), as the
discrete case of the spectral theorem's staircase PVM (lesson 2), as the
pole locations of a resolvent derived from a completely different jump
condition (lesson 4), and as a Sturm-Liouville eigenvalue problem with
$p=w=1,q=0$ (lesson 5): four independent derivations, the same four
numbers every time, computed directly by this platform's real

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Concluding this course means the earlier curriculum was "wrong" or
"cheating."** It wasn't: every one of its predictions was correct, and
this capstone spent its whole argument explaining precisely why. Rigor
here means understanding *why* the shortcuts worked, not discovering
they didn't.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Concluding rigor never matters because it never changed an answer in
this curriculum.** The half-line momentum operator is a genuine
counterexample to that conclusion: a question just one step to the side
of anything the main curriculum asked has no consistent answer at all,
and only the rigorous framework built here reveals that in advance
rather than after a calculation quietly breaks.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Four shortcuts from the earlier curriculum, "check $A=A^\dagger$,"
  "sum $\lambda_iP_i$," "use $|p\rangle$ freely," "match boundary
  conditions", are each shown, lesson by lesson in this course, to be
  the special case of a more general theorem that happens to hold for
  every system the main curriculum actually solved.
- None of them ever produced a wrong prediction because the physically
  chosen boundary/domain conditions and the mathematically required
  self-adjoint ones coincide for every potential this platform teaches,
  not a coincidence, but not a guarantee either.
- The half-line momentum operator is a genuine, worked case where no
  self-adjoint extension exists at all, showing rigor's real value:
  catching a future question instinct alone can't answer, not just
  re-certifying past answers that were already right.
- The infinite well's spectrum, reached from four independent
  derivations across this course, lands on the same four numbers every
  time: the mathematical texture of a genuinely secure result.
