## A Plane Wave That Is Not a State

Momentum Space and the Fourier Transform used $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$
as "the momentum eigenstate," expanded arbitrary states in it, and
relied on completeness, $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$, without a second
thought, and every physical prediction that came out of it was correct.
This lesson doesn't patch over the resulting mathematical crack; it
builds <Term id="rigged-hilbert-space">the precise structure, the **rigged Hilbert space**</Term>, and shows
exactly what breaks (and why) if you try to force $|p\rangle$ into
$\mathcal H$ itself.

## Three Nested Spaces, Not One

Picture three nested spaces instead of one: a small, extremely
well-behaved space $\Phi$ of "nice" test functions (smooth, rapidly
decaying) sitting inside the ordinary Hilbert space $\mathcal
H=L^2(\mathbb R)$, which itself sits inside a much larger space $\Phi'$
of **generalized functions** (distributions) that only need to pair
sensibly against the nice functions in $\Phi$, not be square-integrable
themselves. $|p\rangle$ lives in the outermost space $\Phi'$: too rough
around the edges (never decaying) to belong to $\mathcal H$, but
perfectly well-defined as a linear functional acting on the nice
functions in $\Phi$.

The picture below makes that nesting concrete, with one example object
anchored in each of the three spaces, drawn directly from this lesson:
a smooth, rapidly-decaying test function innermost in $\Phi$, the
normalizable wave packet $\psi(x)=\int c(p)\phi_p(x)\,dp$ in $\mathcal H$, and
the plane wave $|p\rangle$ (together with $\delta(p-p')$ itself)
outermost in $\Phi'$, too rough to ever join $\mathcal H$.

## The Gelfand Triple, Built

### $|p\rangle$ is not in $L^2(\mathbb R)$

The (unnormalizable) plane wave $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ has
$|\phi_p(x)|^2=1$ everywhere, so

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\phi_p(x)|^2\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1\,dx=\infty
$$

a direct, one-line computation, not a subtle one: $\phi_p\notin
L^2(\mathbb R)$, full stop. This is a harder failure mode than the
vanishing-at-infinity issue Hilbert Spaces and Self-Adjointness dealt
with: $\phi_p$ doesn't even decay, let alone vanish; $|\phi_p|^2$ is a
nonzero constant everywhere on the line.

So $\hat p$ has no eigenvectors, and yet the previous lesson's PVM
assigns it a spectrum filling all of $\mathbb R$. Both statements are
true, which means "spectrum" cannot mean "set of eigenvalues" here. It
is worth having the definition of the spectrum in full before going
further.

<DefinitionBox title="Spectrum, and its three parts">

For a closed operator $A$, the **resolvent set** $\rho(A)$ is the set of
$\lambda\in\mathbb C$ for which $A-\lambda$ has a bounded inverse defined
on all of $\mathcal H$. The **spectrum** is what is left,
$\sigma(A)=\mathbb C\setminus\rho(A)$, and it splits by *how* the inverse
fails:

- **Point spectrum** $\sigma_p$: $A-\lambda$ is not injective. There is an
  eigenvector in $\mathcal H$. This is the only case finite
  dimensions ever produces.
- **Continuous spectrum** $\sigma_c$: $A-\lambda$ is injective with dense
  range, but the inverse is unbounded. No eigenvector exists in
  $\mathcal H$, yet one can find unit vectors $\psi_n\in D(A)$ with
  $\|(A-\lambda)\psi_n\|\to0$. These are approximate eigenvectors:
  arbitrarily close to satisfying the eigenvalue equation, and converging
  to nothing in $\mathcal H$.
- **Residual spectrum** $\sigma_r$: $A-\lambda$ is injective but its range
  is not dense. For a self-adjoint $A$ this set is **empty**, which is
  one of the quieter dividends of self-adjointness.

</DefinitionBox>

For a matrix, $\sigma(A)=\sigma_p(A)$ and nothing else can happen: a
non-injective $A-\lambda$ on a finite-dimensional space is exactly a
non-invertible one. The momentum operator is the standard counterexample
in infinite dimensions. Its spectrum is all of $\mathbb R$ and its point
spectrum is empty, so *every* spectral value of $\hat p$ is a continuous
one. The approximate eigenvectors are concrete: fix a smooth bump
$\chi$ supported on $[-1,1]$, and set
$\psi_L(x)=\chi(x/L)e^{ipx/\hbar}/\|\chi(\cdot/L)\|$. Then
$(\hat p-p)\psi_L=-i\hbar\,\chi'(x/L)e^{ipx/\hbar}/(L\|\chi(\cdot/L)\|)$,
whose norm is $O(1/L)\to0$, while $\psi_L$ itself spreads out and
converges to no $L^2$ function at all. Every $\psi_L$ is a legitimate
state; their limit is not. The rest of this lesson builds the space where
that limit does have a home.

### $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$, derived as a limit

The standard identity $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$ (using
normalization $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$) isn't an
assertion; it's the limit of a hand-computable sequence.
Truncate the overlap integral to a finite box $[-L,L]$ (natural units
$\hbar=1$):

$$
\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(p-p')x}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{e^{i(p-p')x}}{i(p-p')}\right]_{-L}^{L}=\frac{\sin\big((p-p')L\big)}{\pi(p-p')}\equiv D_L(p-p')
$$

a fully elementary antiderivative computation. $D_L(q)\equiv\sin(qL)/(\pi
q)$ (with $D_L(0)=L/\pi$, the removable-singularity value) is the
**Dirichlet kernel**. Its peak height $L/\pi$ grows and its main lobe's
width shrinks like $1/L$ as $L\to\infty$, so the whole kernel piles up
into an ever-narrower spike at $q=0$. What that piling-up does to the
total area is the question the whole delta-sequence claim turns on:

rather than a hand-wave.

### The Schwartz space $\Phi$

Let $\Phi=\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$, the **Schwartz space**: smooth
functions all of whose derivatives decay faster than any power of $x$
(cited definition, standard in analysis). Two facts about $\mathcal S$
make it exactly the right "nice" space (cited, standard results of
Fourier analysis): it's dense in $L^2(\mathbb R)$ (so nothing physical is
lost by restricting to it), and it's invariant under both the Fourier
transform and multiplication/differentiation by polynomials, meaning
$\hat x,\hat p$, and every polynomial combination of them, map $\mathcal
S$ back into $\mathcal S$. That closure property is what a
"space of test functions" needs to be useful.

### The dual space $\Phi'$ and the Gelfand triple

$\Phi'$, the **continuous dual** of $\Phi$, is the space of continuous
linear functionals $F:\Phi\to\mathbb C$: objects defined not by having
their own norm, but by how they *pair* against every nice test function.
Every $\mathcal H$-vector $\chi$ gives one such functional via
$F_\chi(\psi)=\langle\chi|\psi\rangle$, so $\mathcal H\subset\Phi'$
naturally, and combined with $\Phi\subset\mathcal H$ (Schwartz functions
are certainly square-integrable) this gives the **Gelfand triple**
(rigged Hilbert space):

$$
\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'
$$

$|p\rangle$ is defined as the element of $\Phi'$ acting by
$\langle p|\psi\rangle\equiv\int\phi_p^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx=\tilde\psi(p)$
(the Fourier transform of $\psi$, evaluated at $p$) for every
$\psi\in\Phi$, a perfectly well-defined, finite number for every nice
$\psi$ (the defining integral converges because $\psi$ is Schwartz,
decaying faster than $\phi_p$'s constant magnitude needs), even though
$\langle p|p\rangle$ itself (pairing $|p\rangle$ against itself, which
isn't in $\Phi$) is not defined this way at all. This is how
$|p\rangle$ can be used constantly and rigorously against physical
states $\psi$, while never being a vector of $\mathcal H$ itself.

### The Gelfand–Maurin theorem

<TheoremBox title="Gelfand–Maurin (nuclear spectral theorem)" provenance="cited">

**Hypotheses.** $\mathcal H$ is a separable Hilbert space; $A$ is
self-adjoint on it; $\Phi\subset D(A)$ is a **nuclear** space, dense in
$\mathcal H$, invariant under $A$ ($A\Phi\subseteq\Phi$), and continuously
embedded. The Schwartz space $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ satisfies all of
these for $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, which is why it was the right choice of
$\Phi$ above.

**Conclusion.** $A$ has a complete set of generalized eigenvectors
$\{|\lambda\rangle\}\subset\Phi'$ with $\lambda$ ranging over $\sigma(A)$,
and every $\psi\in\Phi$ decomposes as

$$
\psi = \int_{\sigma(A)} d\mu(\lambda)\,\langle\lambda|\psi\rangle\,|\lambda\rangle
$$

against a spectral measure $\mu$. The equality holds in the
$\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing sense: both sides, tested against any
$\varphi\in\Phi$, agree. It is *not* a norm-convergent expansion in
$\mathcal H$, and it does not assert that any $|\lambda\rangle$ is a
vector.

Nuclearity is the hypothesis doing the real work, and it is the one
usually skipped. Without it the generalized eigenvectors need not exist.
Gelfand–Vilenkin, *Generalized Functions* IV; Maurin (1968).

</TheoremBox>

This is the rigorous content of "$\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$": not a
completeness relation in $\mathcal H$, but a decomposition valid whenever
both sides are paired against a test function.

## A Contradiction, Not an Inconvenience

Insisting $|p\rangle\in\mathcal H$ leads to a direct contradiction, not
just an inconvenience: normalization in $\mathcal H$ would require
$\langle p|p\rangle=1$ (finite), but the honest computation above gives
$\delta(0)$, undefined/infinite. There is no consistent way to patch
this within $\mathcal H$ itself: the resolution has to be the different
kind of object built here, not a rescaled or regularized version of the
same kind of object. Physically this is right: no real
measurement apparatus resolves momentum to infinite precision. What gets
measured is always a wave packet, built by integrating $|p\rangle$
against a nice (Schwartz, or at least $L^2$) weighting function,
landing back in $\mathcal H$ every time. $|p\rangle$ itself is the
idealized limit those packets approach, never a state any real
experiment prepares.

## The Delta Normalization as a Limit

**Confirm the Dirichlet kernel's total area is exactly $1$ for every
$L$, directly.**

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty D_L(q)\,dq=\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin(qL)}{\pi q}\,dq=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin(u)}{u}\,du=\frac1\pi\cdot\pi=1
$$

using the substitution $u=qL$ (so $dq=du/L$, and the $L$'s cancel
exactly, independent of $L$) and the standard Dirichlet integral
$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\mathrm{sinc}(u)\,du=\pi$ (cited, a classical
result usually proved by contour integration, outside this course's
scope). This is why the numerical check above found
$\approx1$ at every tested $L$: it is not converging *to* $1$ as $L$
grows, but is *exactly* $1$ for every finite $L$ already. Only the
kernel's *shape* (narrowing, sharpening) changes with $L$, not its total
area, which is the precise mathematical content of "$D_L\to\delta$."

## Treating a Plane Wave as a Lesser Vector

<Callout type="mistake">
**Saying "$|p\rangle$ is in $\mathcal H$, just with infinite norm."**
That's a contradiction in terms: $\mathcal H$'s defining condition is
finite norm; an object with undefined/infinite norm is not a
lower-quality $\mathcal H$-vector; it's a different *kind* of object
entirely (an element of $\Phi'$), and needs the different mathematical
treatment this lesson builds.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$ as an ordinary
$\mathcal H$-completeness relation**, the direct continuum analogue of
$\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|=I$. It's the same idea, but the
equality only makes sense in the $\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing sense (both sides
tested against a nice $\psi\in\Phi$): the Gelfand–Maurin theorem, not a
finite- or even infinite-dimensional Hilbert-space identity by itself.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Explain, using the definition $\int_{-L}^{L}$, why truncating the
   momentum-eigenstate overlap integral to a finite box is what turns an
   undefined (divergent) expression into a well-defined function
   $D_L(q)$ of $L$ and $q$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Start by being precise about what is wrong with the untruncated
   integral, because "divergent" is the wrong word for it and the wrong
   word points at the wrong fix. The antiderivative is
   $e^{iqx}/(iq)$, and as $x\to+\infty$ that expression does not grow: it
   has constant modulus $1/|q|$ and simply circles the same circle
   forever. So $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{iqx}dx$ is not infinite, it is
   *undefined*. There is no limit to take, in either direction, and no
   amount of rescaling produces one.

   Truncation removes exactly that step. Evaluating the antiderivative at
   the finite points $\pm L$ instead of at $\pm\infty$ asks it only for
   values it actually has:

   $$
   D_L(q)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{e^{iqx}}{iq}\right]_{-L}^{L}=\frac{e^{iqL}-e^{-iqL}}{2\pi iq}=\frac{\sin(qL)}{\pi q}.
   $$

   What comes out is an ordinary real-valued function of two finite
   arguments, and every question one wants to ask can now be asked of it
   and answered: peak height $D_L(0)=L/\pi$, main lobe half-width $\pi/L$,
   total area exactly $1$ at every $L$ by the substitution $u=qL$.

   The delta is then *defined* as the limit of this family, and the limit
   is taken in the pairing, not pointwise:
   $\lim_{L\to\infty}\int D_L(q)f(q)\,dq=f(0)$ for every test function
   $f$. That distinction is not pedantry. At any fixed $q\ne0$,
   $\sin(qL)/(\pi q)$ oscillates forever between $\pm1/(\pi|q|)$ as $L$
   grows and has no pointwise limit either, while at $q=0$ it runs off to
   infinity. The family converges nowhere as functions, and everywhere as
   functionals. That is precisely why $\delta$ has to live in $\Phi'$
   rather than in any space of functions.

   The wrong turn is treating $L$ as an error parameter, reading $D_L$ as
   an approximation to $\delta$ that gets better and would be exact at
   $L=\infty$. The area is $1$ at every finite $L$ already, exactly and
   not approximately, so there is no error being driven down; only the
   shape changes. $L$ is not an approximation knob, it is the thing that
   makes the object exist.

   A second wrong turn is suspecting the answer depends on the regulator,
   as if a box cutoff were an arbitrary choice smuggling itself into the
   result. Regulate instead with $e^{-\varepsilon|x|}$ and you get
   $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{iqx-\varepsilon|x|}dx=\frac{\varepsilon}{\pi(q^2+\varepsilon^2)}$,
   a Lorentzian rather than a sinc, with a completely different shape and
   the same total area $1$ and the same distributional limit. That
   Lorentzian is the delta-sequence the next lesson's Sokhotski-Plemelj
   identity runs on. Two regulators, two families, one limit: the check
   that the limit means something.

   </details>

2. The Schwartz space $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ is closed under both the
   Fourier transform and multiplication by $x$. Why is *both* of these
   properties necessary for $\Phi$ to be a sensible "test function"
   space for a theory that uses both $\hat x$ and $\hat p$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The Gelfand-Maurin hypothesis names the requirement exactly:
   $A\Phi\subseteq\Phi$, invariance under the operator. A test space is
   only useful if applying the theory's operators never throws you out of
   it, because otherwise a matrix element can be defined and its
   continuation undefined.

   Take the two failures one at a time.

   Closed under Fourier but not under multiplication by $x$: then
   $\hat x\psi=x\psi(x)$ leaves $\Phi$. Immediately
   $\langle p|\hat x|\psi\rangle$ has no meaning, because $|p\rangle$ is
   defined only by its pairing against elements of $\Phi$ and
   $\hat x\psi$ is no longer one. Every expectation value
   $\langle\psi|\hat x^n|\psi\rangle$ becomes a separate question of
   whether it happens to survive, and the harmonic oscillator's
   $V(\hat x)=\tfrac12m\omega^2\hat x^2$ is already outside the
   framework.

   Closed under multiplication by $x$ but not under Fourier: then
   $\Phi$ works in the position representation and collapses in the
   momentum one. The Fourier transform $\tilde\psi$ is not some auxiliary
   object here; by the definition given above, $\langle p|\psi\rangle
   =\tilde\psi(p)$ *is* the pairing. If $\tilde\psi\notin\Phi$ then the
   momentum-space wavefunction is not a test function, so the same
   construction cannot be run a second time in the $p$ representation,
   and $\hat p$ has no invariant test space of its own.

   The deeper point is that these are not two independent conditions
   bolted together. The Fourier transform trades decay for smoothness:
   $\widehat{x\psi}\propto d\tilde\psi/dp$ and
   $\widehat{\psi'}\propto p\tilde\psi$. So "closed under multiplication
   by $x$" in one representation *is* "closed under differentiation" in
   the other. Schwartz space is defined by demanding decay faster than
   every power together with derivatives of every order, which is exactly
   the self-dual pair of conditions, and that self-duality is why
   $\mathcal S$ is Fourier-invariant at all. One requirement, seen from
   two sides.

   The wrong turn is answering "we use both $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, so we
   need both properties," which restates the question without saying what
   breaks. The sharper way to see the point is to try the most natural
   competitor. $C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$, the smooth compactly supported
   functions, is dense in $L^2$, nuclear, and closed under both
   differentiation and multiplication by $x$, so it passes every test
   except one: a nonzero compactly supported function has a Fourier
   transform that extends to an entire function, and an entire function
   cannot vanish on an open set without vanishing identically, so
   $\tilde\psi$ is never compactly supported. $C_c^\infty$ is not
   Fourier-invariant, and that single failure is what disqualifies it. It
   is the cleanest evidence that the property the question isolates is
   doing real work rather than being listed for symmetry.

   </details>

3. A physically prepared wave packet $\psi(x)=\int c(p)\phi_p(x)\,dp$
   with $c(p)$ a smooth, compactly-supported function is an honest
   $L^2(\mathbb R)$ vector, even though each individual $\phi_p$ isn't.
   Explain, in terms of the Dirichlet-kernel worked example, why
   integrating over many $|p\rangle$'s can land back in $\mathcal H$ even
   though no single one of them does.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The mechanism is interference, and the Dirichlet kernel is that
   interference in its purest form. Better still, it is not merely
   analogous to this question: it is literally an instance of it.

   Take the crudest possible weight, $c(p)=\mathbf1_{[-L,L]}(p)/\sqrt{2\pi}$,
   with the lesson's normalization $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in
   natural units. Then

   $$
   \psi(x)=\int c(p)\,\phi_p(x)\,dp=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-L}^{L}e^{ipx}\,dp=\frac{\sin(Lx)}{\pi x}=D_L(x).
   $$

   That is the same integral the lesson evaluated for the truncated
   overlap, with $x$ and $q$ trading places, because $e^{ipx}$ does not
   care which of its two arguments is being integrated. The truncated
   overlap and the box-weighted wave packet are the same function.

   Now compare norms. A single $\phi_p$ has $|\phi_p(x)|^2=1/(2\pi)$ at
   every $x$: it never decays, nothing anywhere ever cancels, and the
   mass integral is infinite. The superposition decays like $1/|x|$, and

   $$
   \|D_L\|^2=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2(Lx)}{\pi^2x^2}\,dx=\frac{L}{\pi},
   $$

   finite for every finite $L$. Dense midpoint quadrature over
   $|x|\le4000$ returns $0.31828$, $0.63659$ and $1.59152$ at $L=1,2,5$,
   against $L/\pi=0.31831$, $0.63662$, $1.59155$. The same number arrives
   independently from Plancherel, since the transform in this
   normalization is unitary and
   $\|c\|^2=\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dp}{2\pi}=\frac{L}{\pi}$, which is the
   general statement: $\|\psi\|_{L^2(dx)}=\|c\|_{L^2(dp)}$, so the packet
   is in $\mathcal H$ precisely when the weight is, and $c$ smooth with
   compact support is very comfortably in $L^2(dp)$.

   What bought the decay was giving up on a single $p$. Different $p$'s
   rotate their phases at different rates in $x$, so once $|x|$ is large
   enough that $px$ varies by many radians across the support of $c$, the
   integral over $p$ cancels against itself. The width of the support sets
   how fast that happens. Compact support alone already buys enough decay
   for $L^2$ membership, and it is only just enough: the box weight decays
   like $1/|x|$, which is square-integrable and not integrable. Making
   $c$ additionally *smooth* upgrades the decay to faster than every
   power, landing $\psi$ in $\Phi$ rather than merely in $\mathcal H$.

   The wrong turn is the triangle inequality:
   $\|\psi\|\le\int|c(p)|\,\|\phi_p\|\,dp$, which is $\infty$ because
   every $\|\phi_p\|$ is, and concluding that the packet cannot be
   normalizable. The inequality is true and the conclusion does not
   follow, because $\infty$ is an upper bound and upper bounds do not
   forbid anything. It fails to be tight for a specific and instructive
   reason: taking absolute values inside the integral discards every
   phase, and phase cancellation was the entire mechanism.

   A second thing worth getting right rather than avoiding: computing
   $\|\psi\|^2=\iint c^*(p)c(p')\langle p|p'\rangle\,dp\,dp'=\int|c(p)|^2dp$
   using $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$ is legitimate, and it
   reproduces Plancherel. The delta appears there paired against the nice
   function $c$, which is exactly the $\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing it is defined
   by. What is illegitimate is the same move with $c$ concentrated on a
   single $p$, which asks for $\delta(0)$. The delta is safe against test
   functions and undefined against itself, and that one sentence is most
   of what the rigged Hilbert space is for.

   </details>

## Summary

- $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ has $\int|\phi_p|^2dx=\infty$: a one-line
  derivation that $|p\rangle\notin L^2(\mathbb R)$.
- $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$ is the $L\to\infty$ limit of the
  truncated overlap $D_L(p-p')=\sin((p-p')L)/(\pi(p-p'))$, a
  delta-sequence: total area exactly $1$ for every $L$, sharpening
  toward a spike as $L$ grows.
- The Gelfand triple $\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'$ (Schwartz
  functions inside $L^2$ inside distributions) is where $|p\rangle$
  lives, a well-defined linear functional on $\Phi$, never a
  vector of $\mathcal H$ itself.
- The Gelfand–Maurin theorem (cited) makes
  $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$ fully rigorous, as a $\Phi,\Phi'$
  pairing identity.
