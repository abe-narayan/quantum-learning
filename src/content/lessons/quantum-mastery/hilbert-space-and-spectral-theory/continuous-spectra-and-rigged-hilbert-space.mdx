## Motivation

Momentum Space and the Fourier Transform used $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$
as "the momentum eigenstate," expanded arbitrary states in it, and
relied on completeness, $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$, without a second
thought, and every physical prediction that came out of it was correct.
This lesson doesn't patch over the resulting mathematical crack; it
builds <Term id="rigged-hilbert-space">the precise structure, the **rigged Hilbert space**</Term>, and shows
exactly what breaks (and why) if you try to force $|p\rangle$ into
$\mathcal H$ itself.

## Intuition

Picture three nested spaces instead of one: a small, extremely
well-behaved space $\Phi$ of "nice" test functions (smooth, rapidly
decaying) sitting inside the ordinary Hilbert space $\mathcal
H=L^2(\mathbb R)$, which itself sits inside a much larger space $\Phi'$
of **generalized functions** (distributions) that only need to pair
sensibly against the nice functions in $\Phi$, not be square-integrable
themselves. $|p\rangle$ lives in the outermost space $\Phi'$: too rough
around the edges (never decaying) to belong to $\mathcal H$, but
perfectly well-defined as a linear functional acting on the nice
functions in $\Phi$.

normalizable wave packet from the Worked Example in $\mathcal H$, and
the plane wave $|p\rangle$ (together with $\delta(p-p')$ itself)

## Mathematical Development

### $|p\rangle$ is not in $L^2(\mathbb R)$

The (unnormalizable) plane wave $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ has
$|\phi_p(x)|^2=1$ everywhere, so

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\phi_p(x)|^2\,dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}1\,dx=\infty
$$

a direct, one-line computation, not a subtle one: $\phi_p\notin
L^2(\mathbb R)$, full stop. This is a harder failure mode than the
vanishing-at-infinity issue Hilbert Spaces and Self-Adjointness dealt
with: $\phi_p$ doesn't even decay, let alone vanish; $|\phi_p|^2$ is a
nonzero constant everywhere on the line.

So $\hat p$ has no eigenvectors, and yet the previous lesson's PVM
assigns it a spectrum filling all of $\mathbb R$. Both statements are
true, which means "spectrum" cannot mean "set of eigenvalues" here. It
is worth having the actual definition before going further.

<DefinitionBox title="Spectrum, and its three parts">

For a closed operator $A$, the **resolvent set** $\rho(A)$ is the set of
$\lambda\in\mathbb C$ for which $A-\lambda$ has a bounded inverse defined
on all of $\mathcal H$. The **spectrum** is what is left,
$\sigma(A)=\mathbb C\setminus\rho(A)$, and it splits by *how* the inverse
fails:

- **Point spectrum** $\sigma_p$: $A-\lambda$ is not injective. There is an
  eigenvector in $\mathcal H$. This is the only case finite
  dimensions ever produces.
- **Continuous spectrum** $\sigma_c$: $A-\lambda$ is injective with dense
  range, but the inverse is unbounded. No eigenvector exists in
  $\mathcal H$, yet one can find unit vectors $\psi_n\in D(A)$ with
  $\|(A-\lambda)\psi_n\|\to0$ — approximate eigenvectors, arbitrarily
  close to being eigenvectors, converging to nothing in $\mathcal H$.
- **Residual spectrum** $\sigma_r$: $A-\lambda$ is injective but its range
  is not dense. For a self-adjoint $A$ this set is **empty**, which is
  one of the quieter dividends of self-adjointness.

</DefinitionBox>

For a matrix, $\sigma(A)=\sigma_p(A)$ and nothing else can happen: a
non-injective $A-\lambda$ on a finite-dimensional space is exactly a
non-invertible one. The momentum operator is the standard counterexample
in infinite dimensions. Its spectrum is all of $\mathbb R$ and its point
spectrum is empty, so *every* spectral value of $\hat p$ is a continuous
one. The approximate eigenvectors are concrete: fix a smooth bump
$\chi$ supported on $[-1,1]$, and set
$\psi_L(x)=\chi(x/L)e^{ipx/\hbar}/\|\chi(\cdot/L)\|$. Then
$(\hat p-p)\psi_L=-i\hbar\,\chi'(x/L)e^{ipx/\hbar}/(L\|\chi(\cdot/L)\|)$,
whose norm is $O(1/L)\to0$, while $\psi_L$ itself spreads out and
converges to no $L^2$ function at all. Every $\psi_L$ is a legitimate
state; their limit is not. The rest of this lesson builds the space where
that limit does have a home.

### $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$, derived as a limit

The standard identity $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$ (using
normalization $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$) isn't an
assertion; it's the limit of a hand-computable sequence.
Truncate the overlap integral to a finite box $[-L,L]$ (natural units
$\hbar=1$):

$$
\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{1}{2\pi}e^{i(p-p')x}\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\frac{e^{i(p-p')x}}{i(p-p')}\right]_{-L}^{L}=\frac{\sin\big((p-p')L\big)}{\pi(p-p')}\equiv D_L(p-p')
$$

a fully elementary antiderivative computation. $D_L(q)\equiv\sin(qL)/(\pi
q)$ (with $D_L(0)=L/\pi$, the removable-singularity value) is the
**Dirichlet kernel**, and it is a delta-sequence: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty
D_L(q)\,dq=1$ for *every* $L$ (a standard, exact identity, cited here),
while its peak height $L/\pi$ grows and its main lobe's width shrinks
like $1/L$ as $L\to\infty$, concentrating all its area into an
ever-narrower spike at $q=0$:

### The Schwartz space $\Phi$

Let $\Phi=\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$, the **Schwartz space**: smooth
functions all of whose derivatives decay faster than any power of $x$
(cited definition, standard in analysis). Two facts about $\mathcal S$
make it exactly the right "nice" space (cited, standard results of
Fourier analysis): it's dense in $L^2(\mathbb R)$ (so nothing physical is
lost by restricting to it), and it's invariant under both the Fourier
transform and multiplication/differentiation by polynomials, meaning
$\hat x,\hat p$, and every polynomial combination of them, map $\mathcal
S$ back into $\mathcal S$. That closure property is what a
"space of test functions" needs to be useful.

### The dual space $\Phi'$ and the Gelfand triple

$\Phi'$, the **continuous dual** of $\Phi$, is the space of continuous
linear functionals $F:\Phi\to\mathbb C$: objects defined not by having
their own norm, but by how they *pair* against every nice test function.
Every $\mathcal H$-vector $\chi$ gives one such functional via
$F_\chi(\psi)=\langle\chi|\psi\rangle$, so $\mathcal H\subset\Phi'$
naturally, and combined with $\Phi\subset\mathcal H$ (Schwartz functions
are certainly square-integrable) this gives the **Gelfand triple**
(rigged Hilbert space):

$$
\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'
$$

$|p\rangle$ is defined as the element of $\Phi'$ acting by
$\langle p|\psi\rangle\equiv\int\phi_p^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx=\tilde\psi(p)$
(the Fourier transform of $\psi$, evaluated at $p$) for every
$\psi\in\Phi$, a perfectly well-defined, finite number for every nice
$\psi$ (the defining integral converges because $\psi$ is Schwartz,
decaying faster than $\phi_p$'s constant magnitude needs), even though
$\langle p|p\rangle$ itself (pairing $|p\rangle$ against itself, which
isn't in $\Phi$) is not defined this way at all. This is how
$|p\rangle$ can be used constantly and rigorously against physical
states $\psi$, while never being a vector of $\mathcal H$ itself.

### The Gelfand–Maurin theorem

<TheoremBox title="Gelfand–Maurin (nuclear spectral theorem)" provenance="cited">

**Hypotheses.** $\mathcal H$ is a separable Hilbert space; $A$ is
self-adjoint on it; $\Phi\subset D(A)$ is a **nuclear** space, dense in
$\mathcal H$, invariant under $A$ ($A\Phi\subseteq\Phi$), and continuously
embedded. The Schwartz space $\mathcal S(\mathbb R)$ satisfies all of
these for $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, which is why it was the right choice of
$\Phi$ above.

**Conclusion.** $A$ has a complete set of generalized eigenvectors
$\{|\lambda\rangle\}\subset\Phi'$ with $\lambda$ ranging over $\sigma(A)$,
and every $\psi\in\Phi$ decomposes as

$$
\psi = \int_{\sigma(A)} d\mu(\lambda)\,\langle\lambda|\psi\rangle\,|\lambda\rangle
$$

against a spectral measure $\mu$. The equality holds in the
$\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing sense: both sides, tested against any
$\varphi\in\Phi$, agree. It is *not* a norm-convergent expansion in
$\mathcal H$, and it does not assert that any $|\lambda\rangle$ is a
vector.

Nuclearity is the hypothesis doing the real work, and it is the one
usually skipped. Without it the generalized eigenvectors need not exist.
Gelfand–Vilenkin, *Generalized Functions* IV; Maurin (1968).

</TheoremBox>

This is the rigorous content of "$\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$": not a
completeness relation in $\mathcal H$, but a decomposition valid whenever
both sides are paired against a test function.

## Physical Interpretation

Insisting $|p\rangle\in\mathcal H$ leads to a direct contradiction, not
just an inconvenience: normalization in $\mathcal H$ would require
$\langle p|p\rangle=1$ (finite), but the honest computation above gives
$\delta(0)$, undefined/infinite. There is no consistent way to patch
this within $\mathcal H$ itself: the resolution has to be the different
kind of object built here, not a rescaled or regularized version of the
same kind of object. Physically this is right: no real
measurement apparatus resolves momentum to infinite precision. What gets
measured is always a wave packet, built by integrating $|p\rangle$
against a nice (Schwartz, or at least $L^2$) weighting function,
landing back in $\mathcal H$ every time. $|p\rangle$ itself is the
idealized limit those packets approach, never a state any real
experiment prepares.

## Worked Example

**Confirm the Dirichlet kernel's total area is exactly $1$ for every
$L$, directly.**

$$
\int_{-\infty}^\infty D_L(q)\,dq=\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin(qL)}{\pi q}\,dq=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{\sin(u)}{u}\,du=\frac1\pi\cdot\pi=1
$$

using the substitution $u=qL$ (so $dq=du/L$, and the $L$'s cancel
exactly, independent of $L$) and the standard Dirichlet integral
$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\mathrm{sinc}(u)\,du=\pi$ (cited, a classical
result usually proved by contour integration, outside this course's
scope). This is why the numerical check above found
$\approx1$ at every tested $L$: it is not converging *to* $1$ as $L$
grows, but is *exactly* $1$ for every finite $L$ already. Only the
kernel's *shape* (narrowing, sharpening) changes with $L$, not its total
area, which is the precise mathematical content of "$D_L\to\delta$."

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Saying "$|p\rangle$ is in $\mathcal H$, just with infinite norm."**
That's a contradiction in terms: $\mathcal H$'s defining condition is
finite norm; an object with undefined/infinite norm is not a
lower-quality $\mathcal H$-vector, it's a different *kind* of object
entirely (an element of $\Phi'$), and needs the different mathematical
treatment this lesson builds.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$ as an ordinary
$\mathcal H$-completeness relation**, the direct continuum analogue of
$\sum_i|e_i\rangle\langle e_i|=I$. It's the same idea, but the
equality only makes sense in the $\Phi,\Phi'$ pairing sense (both sides
tested against a nice $\psi\in\Phi$): the Gelfand–Maurin theorem, not a
finite- or even infinite-dimensional Hilbert-space identity by itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\phi_p(x)=e^{ipx/\hbar}$ has $\int|\phi_p|^2dx=\infty$: a one-line
  derivation that $|p\rangle\notin L^2(\mathbb R)$.
- $\langle p|p'\rangle=\delta(p-p')$ is the $L\to\infty$ limit of the
  truncated overlap $D_L(p-p')=\sin((p-p')L)/(\pi(p-p'))$, a
  delta-sequence: total area exactly $1$ for every $L$, sharpening
  toward a spike as $L$ grows.
- The Gelfand triple $\Phi\subset\mathcal H\subset\Phi'$ (Schwartz
  functions inside $L^2$ inside distributions) is where $|p\rangle$
  lives, a well-defined linear functional on $\Phi$, never a
  vector of $\mathcal H$ itself.
- The Gelfand–Maurin theorem (cited) makes
  $\int|p\rangle\langle p|\,dp=I$ fully rigorous, as a $\Phi,\Phi'$
  pairing identity.
