## Motivation

Hermitian Operators proved, correctly and completely, that $A=A^\dagger$
forces real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors, for a **matrix**.
Every later course reused that single check, $A=A^\dagger$, on operators
like $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ acting on functions, and it always worked.
That's not a coincidence to wave away, but it's also not the whole
story: on an infinite-dimensional space, $A=A^\dagger$ (checked by
integration by parts) is a genuinely weaker statement than what "the
eigenvalues are provably real and a complete eigenbasis provably exists"
actually requires. This lesson isolates exactly what's missing, calls it
by its real name (**self-adjointness**, not mere Hermiticity), and shows,
with a fully worked example, a case where the missing piece breaks
things outright.

## Intuition

In finite dimensions, "$A$ maps vectors to vectors, and $\langle
u|Av\rangle=\langle Au|v\rangle$ for all $u,v$" is the whole story,
because every linear operator is automatically defined on the *entire*
space and there's only one reasonable domain to talk about. On an
infinite-dimensional function space this stops being true: differential
operators like $\hat p$ aren't defined on every element of $L^2(\mathbb
R)$ (not every $L^2$ function is even differentiable), so *which
functions the operator is allowed to act on*, its **domain** $D(A)$,
becomes part of the operator's actual identity, not an afterthought.
Self-adjointness asks a domain question finite dimensions never has to:
does $A$'s domain match $A^\dagger$'s domain *exactly*, not just does
the boundary-term calculation come out looking symmetric.

## Mathematical Development

### $L^2(\mathbb R)$ is a genuine Hilbert space

$L^2(\mathbb R)=\{\psi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C \mid \int|\psi(x)|^2dx<\infty\}$
with inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx$ is a
Hilbert space, meaning: it's an inner product space (immediate from the
integral's linearity and conjugate symmetry), it's **complete** (every
Cauchy sequence of $L^2$ functions converges to an $L^2$ function: the
Riesz–Fischer theorem, a genuine, nontrivial real-analysis result **cited
here, not proved**: proving completeness of $L^2$ requires the Lebesgue
integral's convergence theorems, outside this course's scope), and it's
**separable** (it has a countable orthonormal basis). Separability is
concrete and checkable: the harmonic-oscillator eigenstates
$\{\psi_n\}_{n=0}^\infty$ this platform already computes via
`harmonicOscillatorEigenstate` are exactly such a countable orthonormal
basis (Hermite functions), so separability isn't an abstract promise:
it's the same basis Wave Mechanics already used to expand any state as
$\sum_nc_n\psi_n$.

Both properties are load-bearing, not decoration. Completeness is what
guarantees a Cauchy sequence of approximate solutions (e.g. the
numerical time-evolution steps Wave Mechanics computed) actually
converges *to something in the space*, rather than to a "hole" outside
it. Separability is what guarantees a countable eigenbasis can exist at
all; without it, "expand $\psi$ in energy eigenstates" wouldn't even
be the right *kind* of statement to make.

### Formally Hermitian ("symmetric") vs. genuinely self-adjoint

An operator $A$ with domain $D(A)\subset\mathcal H$ is **symmetric** if
$\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle A\phi|\psi\rangle$ for all
$\phi,\psi\in D(A)$, exactly the integration-by-parts check
Position and Momentum Operators already did for $\hat p$. The **adjoint**
$A^\dagger$ is a possibly *larger*-domain operator: $D(A^\dagger)$ is the
set of $\phi$ for which *some* $\chi$ satisfies
$\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle\chi|\psi\rangle$ for every
$\psi\in D(A)$, and then $A^\dagger\phi\equiv\chi$. $A$ is
**self-adjoint** only if $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ *exactly*, not merely
$D(A)\subseteq D(A^\dagger)$, which is all "symmetric" guarantees. In
finite dimensions $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)=\mathbb C^n$ automatically for any
matrix, so this distinction is invisible; that's precisely why Hermitian
Operators never needed to make it.

### Deriving that $D(\hat p)$-functions vanish at infinity

The domain that makes $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ symmetric on the whole line
is $D(\hat p)=H^1(\mathbb R)=\{\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R): \psi'\in
L^2(\mathbb R)\}$ (both $\psi$ and its derivative square-integrable).
Position and Momentum Operators asserted that the boundary term in
integration by parts "vanishes for normalizable $\psi,\phi$, which go
to zero at $\pm\infty$", true, but that vanishing is itself a fact that
needs deriving, since $L^2$ membership alone does **not** imply a
function tends to $0$ pointwise (a function can spike arbitrarily high
on shrinking intervals and still have finite $\int|\psi|^2$). What
actually forces the vanishing is $\psi'\in L^2$ *as well*:

For $\psi\in H^1(\mathbb R)$, real or complex,
$\dfrac{d}{dx}|\psi(x)|^2=\psi'(x)\psi^*(x)+\psi(x)\psi'^*(x)=2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi'(x)\psi^*(x)\big)$.
By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $\psi,\psi'\in L^2(\mathbb
R)$, the product $\psi'\psi^*$ is in $L^1(\mathbb R)$ (absolutely
integrable):
$\int|\psi'\psi^*|\,dx\le\|\psi'\|_{L^2}\|\psi\|_{L^2}<\infty$. So

$$
|\psi(x)|^2-|\psi(0)|^2=\int_0^x2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi'\psi^*\big)\,dt
$$

is an integral of an absolutely-integrable function, hence it
**converges to a finite limit as $x\to+\infty$** (and likewise
$x\to-\infty$). So $|\psi(x)|^2$ tends to some limit $L\ge0$. But if
$L>0$, then $|\psi(x)|^2$ stays bounded away from zero for all large
$x$, making $\int|\psi|^2dx$ diverge, contradicting $\psi\in
L^2(\mathbb R)$. So $L=0$: **$\psi(x)\to0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$ for every
$\psi\in H^1(\mathbb R)$**, a genuine derivation from the two
hypotheses $\psi,\psi'\in L^2$, not an assumption smuggled in by calling
$\psi$ "well-behaved."

With that lemma established, the boundary term in
$\int\phi^*(-i\hbar\psi')dx=-i\hbar[\phi^*\psi]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+i\hbar\int\phi'^*\psi\,dx$
genuinely vanishes (both $\phi,\psi\to0$), confirming $\hat p$ is
symmetric on $H^1(\mathbb R)$.

### Why the full-line $\hat p$ is actually self-adjoint, not just symmetric

Symmetry alone leaves open whether $D(\hat p^\dagger)$ might be
*strictly larger* than $H^1(\mathbb R)$. It isn't, and the cleanest way
to see this uses the Fourier transform (Momentum Space and the Fourier
Transform): $\hat p$ is unitarily equivalent, via the Fourier transform,
to the operator "multiply by $\hbar k$" acting on $\phi(k)$. A
multiplication-by-a-real-function operator is manifestly self-adjoint on
its natural maximal domain $\{\phi\in L^2:\;k\phi(k)\in L^2\}$ (no
integration by parts or boundary term is even needed: multiplication
by a real number is its own adjoint entrywise), and that maximal domain
transforms back, under the same unitary Fourier transform, to exactly
$H^1(\mathbb R)$. So $D(\hat p)=D(\hat p^\dagger)=H^1(\mathbb R)$: the
full-line momentum operator is genuinely self-adjoint, not merely
symmetric; this is why every calculation built on $\hat p$ so far in
this platform was safe, even though the domain question was never
raised.

### Where it breaks: momentum on a half-line

Consider $\hat p=-i\,d/dx$ on $L^2(0,\infty)$ (natural units,
$\hbar=1$), the operator that would represent "momentum toward the
wall" for a particle confined to $x>0$ by a hard wall at $x=0$. Try the
symmetric domain $D(\hat p)=\{\psi\in H^1(0,\infty):\psi(0)=0\}$
(the natural guess: a Dirichlet condition at the one boundary point,
directly mirroring the well-worn "wavefunction vanishes at a wall").
Whether *any* choice of domain makes this operator self-adjoint is
settled by von Neumann's **deficiency-index theorem** (cited here, a
genuine and deep result of functional analysis not re-derived in full):
count the dimensions of the solution spaces of
$\hat p^\dagger\psi=+i\psi$ and $\hat p^\dagger\psi=-i\psi$ inside
$L^2(0,\infty)$; call these dimensions $n_+,n_-$, the **deficiency
indices**. A self-adjoint extension exists *only if* $n_+=n_-$.

**Compute the deficiency indices directly.** $\hat p^\dagger\psi=\pm
i\psi$ means $-i\psi'=\pm i\psi$, i.e. $\psi'=\mp\psi$, with solutions
$\psi(x)=Ce^{\mp x}$. Which of these lie in $L^2(0,\infty)$?

$$
\int_0^\infty|e^{-x}|^2dx=\int_0^\infty e^{-2x}dx=\tfrac12<\infty \qquad(\text{normalizable})
$$

$$
\int_0^\infty|e^{+x}|^2dx=\int_0^\infty e^{2x}dx=\infty \qquad(\text{not normalizable})
$$

So the $\psi'=-\psi$ equation (eigenvalue $+i$) has exactly one
$L^2(0,\infty)$ solution ($n_+=1$), while $\psi'=+\psi$ (eigenvalue
$-i$) has none ($n_-=0$). **$n_+\ne n_-$**: by von Neumann's theorem,
**no self-adjoint extension of $\hat p$ on the half-line exists at
all**, not the Dirichlet choice above, not any other local boundary
condition. Every domain choice leaves it merely symmetric at best.

## Physical Interpretation

Self-adjointness isn't a technicality kept separate from physics: it's
exactly the hypothesis Stone's theorem needs to generate a genuinely
unitary time evolution $e^{-iAt/\hbar}$ (cited, not proved here: Stone's
theorem is itself a substantial functional-analysis result). A merely
symmetric operator can fail to generate *any* consistent unitary
evolution, meaning probability wouldn't reliably be conserved if you
tried to use it as a Hamiltonian or a generator of translations. The
half-line result above has a genuine physical reading: **there is no
self-adjoint "momentum toward the wall" operator for a particle
confined to a half-line by a hard, impenetrable wall.** This isn't a
platform limitation to work around; it reflects that "momentum" as a
*generator of translations* doesn't make sense on a space with an
inaccessible edge on only one side (translating far enough in the
$-x$ direction runs the particle straight through the wall, which
isn't a symmetry of the half-line at all). Physically sensible momentum
operators exist only on spaces without a one-sided edge: the whole
line, or a *periodic* interval (both directions structurally
equivalent), never a genuine half-line.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "$A=A^\dagger$ by integration by parts" as the complete
self-adjointness check.** That calculation only ever shows $A$ is
*symmetric* on whatever domain you assumed. Self-adjointness additionally
requires $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ exactly, a domain question that's
automatically settled (and hence invisible) in finite dimensions, but
genuinely open on a function space, as the half-line example shows: a
domain can be symmetric and still admit no self-adjoint extension at
all.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming $L^2$ membership alone implies a function vanishes at
infinity.** It doesn't: only the *combination* $\psi,\psi'\in L^2$
(the actual hypothesis of $\hat p$'s domain) forces the vanishing, via
the Cauchy–Schwarz argument derived above. A function can be square
integrable while spiking arbitrarily high on ever-narrower intervals
without tending to any limit at all.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $L^2(\mathbb R)$ is a genuine Hilbert space: complete (Riesz–Fischer,
  cited) and separable (the Hermite/harmonic-oscillator basis, concrete).
- A **symmetric** operator satisfies $\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle
  A\phi|\psi\rangle$ on its domain $D(A)$; it's **self-adjoint** only if
  $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ exactly, a strictly stronger condition invisible in
  finite dimensions.
- $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ on the whole line is genuinely self-adjoint on
  $D(\hat p)=H^1(\mathbb R)$, derivable via the Fourier-transform
  equivalence to a self-adjoint multiplication operator; every $H^1$
  function provably vanishes at $\pm\infty$, which is exactly what makes
  the earlier boundary-term calculation legitimate rather than assumed.
- $\hat p$ on a half-line has unequal deficiency indices ($n_+=1$,
  $n_-=0$, computed directly), so by von Neumann's theorem it has **no**
  self-adjoint extension at all, a genuine, worked case where the
  informal "check $A=A^\dagger$" recipe would have missed a real
  mathematical obstruction.
