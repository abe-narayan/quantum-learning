  const out = [];
  let acc = 0;
  for (let i = 0; i < xs.length; i++) {
    if (i > 0) {
      const dx = xs[i] - xs[i - 1];
      acc += 0.5 * dx * (f(xs[i - 1]) ** 2 + f(xs[i]) ** 2);

    out.push({ x: xs[i], y: acc });
  }
  return out;
}

## Where Hermitian Stops Being Enough

Hermitian Operators proved that $A=A^\dagger$ forces real eigenvalues and
orthogonal eigenvectors, for a **matrix**. Every later course reused that
same check on operators like $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ acting on functions,
integration by parts in place of a finite sum, and it always worked. Try
that identical check on $\hat p=-i\,d/dx$ for a particle confined to
$x>0$ by a hard wall, and it still goes through cleanly, yet the
operator you get has no self-adjoint version at all, not with this
boundary condition, not with any other. On an infinite-dimensional
space, $A=A^\dagger$ checked by integration by parts is weaker than what
"the eigenvalues are provably real and a complete eigenbasis provably
exists" requires, and the half-line momentum operator is where
that gap becomes a real obstruction rather than a footnote. This lesson
isolates exactly what's missing, gives it its real name
(**self-adjointness**, not mere Hermiticity), and works through that
half-line case in full.

## The Domain Is Part of the Operator

In finite dimensions, "$A$ maps vectors to vectors, and $\langle
u|Av\rangle=\langle Au|v\rangle$ for all $u,v$" is the whole story,
because every linear operator is automatically defined on the *entire*
space and there's only one reasonable domain to talk about. On an
infinite-dimensional function space this stops being true: differential
operators like $\hat p$ aren't defined on every element of $L^2(\mathbb
R)$ (not every $L^2$ function is even differentiable), so *which
functions the operator is allowed to act on*, its **domain** $D(A)$,
becomes part of the operator's identity, not an afterthought.
Self-adjointness asks a domain question finite dimensions never has to:
does $A$'s domain match $A^\dagger$'s domain *exactly*, not just does
the boundary-term calculation come out looking symmetric.

## Symmetric, Self-Adjoint, and the Deficiency Indices

### Why $L^2(\mathbb R)$ qualifies as a Hilbert space

$L^2(\mathbb R)=\{\psi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C \mid \int|\psi(x)|^2dx<\infty\}$
with inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\int\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx$ is a
<Term id="hilbert-space">Hilbert space</Term>, meaning: it's an inner product space (immediate from the
integral's linearity and conjugate symmetry), it's **complete** (every
Cauchy sequence of $L^2$ functions converges to an $L^2$ function: the
Riesz–Fischer theorem, a nontrivial real-analysis result **cited
here, not proved**, since proving completeness of $L^2$ requires the Lebesgue
integral's convergence theorems, outside this course's scope), and it's
**separable** (it has a countable orthonormal basis). Separability is
concrete and checkable: the harmonic-oscillator eigenstates
$\{\psi_n\}_{n=0}^\infty$ this platform already computes via
`harmonicOscillatorEigenstate` are such a countable orthonormal
basis (Hermite functions), so separability isn't an abstract promise:
it's the same basis Wave Mechanics already used to expand any state as
$\sum_nc_n\psi_n$.

Both properties are load-bearing, not decoration. Completeness is what
guarantees a Cauchy sequence of approximate solutions (e.g. the
numerical time-evolution steps Wave Mechanics computed)
converges *to something in the space*, rather than to a "hole" outside
it. Separability is what guarantees a countable eigenbasis can exist at
all; without it, "expand $\psi$ in energy eigenstates" wouldn't even
be the right *kind* of statement to make.

### Formally Hermitian ("symmetric") versus self-adjoint

An operator $A$ with dense domain $D(A)\subset\mathcal H$ is **symmetric** if
$\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle A\phi|\psi\rangle$ for all
$\phi,\psi\in D(A)$, the same integration-by-parts check
Position and Momentum Operators already did for $\hat p$. The **adjoint**
$A^\dagger$ is a possibly *larger*-domain operator: $D(A^\dagger)$ is the
set of $\phi$ for which *some* $\chi$ satisfies
$\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle\chi|\psi\rangle$ for every
$\psi\in D(A)$, and then $A^\dagger\phi\equiv\chi$. Density of $D(A)$ is
what makes that last step legitimate: if $D(A)$ were not dense, two
different $\chi$ could agree against every $\psi\in D(A)$ and differ on
the orthogonal complement, leaving $A^\dagger\phi$ undefined. Every
operator in this lesson is densely defined, and the hypothesis is worth
naming because it is the one silently supplied in finite dimensions. $A$ is
**self-adjoint** only if $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ *exactly*, not merely
$D(A)\subseteq D(A^\dagger)$, which is all "symmetric" guarantees. In
finite dimensions $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)=\mathbb C^n$ automatically for any
matrix, so this distinction is invisible; that is why Hermitian
Operators never needed to make it.

Picture $D(A)$ and $D(A^\dagger)$ as two regions: symmetry only ever
guarantees the smaller one sits inside the larger, $D(A)\subseteq
D(A^\dagger)$, with no promise that there isn't room to spare.
Self-adjointness demands the stronger claim that the two regions
coincide exactly, no leftover room at all. The two operators this
lesson works out land on opposite sides of that line:

### Deriving that $D(\hat p)$-functions vanish at infinity

The domain that makes $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ symmetric on the whole line
is $D(\hat p)=H^1(\mathbb R)=\{\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R): \psi'\in
L^2(\mathbb R)\}$ (both $\psi$ and its derivative square-integrable).
Position and Momentum Operators asserted that the boundary term in
integration by parts "vanishes for normalizable $\psi,\phi$, which go
to zero at $\pm\infty$", true, but that vanishing is itself a fact that
needs deriving, since $L^2$ membership alone does **not** imply a
function tends to $0$ pointwise (a function can spike arbitrarily high
on shrinking intervals and still have finite $\int|\psi|^2$). What
forces the vanishing is $\psi'\in L^2$ *as well*:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Product rule for d/dx|ψ|², valid for any ψ regardless of domain. The identity holds for real or complex ψ.">
    $$
    \frac{d}{dx}|\psi(x)|^2=\psi'(x)\psi^*(x)+\psi(x)\psi'^*(x)=2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi'(x)\psi^*(x)\big)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Cauchy–Schwarz applied to ψ, ψ′ ∈ L²(ℝ) shows the product ψ′ψ* is absolutely integrable (in L¹).">
    $$
    \int|\psi'\psi^*|\,dx\le\|\psi'\|_{L^2}\|\psi\|_{L^2}<\infty
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Integrating the product rule from 0 to x expresses |ψ(x)|² − |ψ(0)|² as an integral of an absolutely-integrable function, so it converges to a finite limit as x → +∞ (and likewise x → −∞).">
    $$
    |\psi(x)|^2-|\psi(0)|^2=\int_0^x2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi'\psi^*\big)\,dt
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="So |ψ(x)|² tends to some limit L ≥ 0. If L > 0, |ψ(x)|² would stay bounded away from zero for all large x, making ∫|ψ|²dx diverge, contradicting ψ ∈ L²(ℝ). So L = 0.">
    $$
    \psi(x)\to0 \ \text{ as } x\to\pm\infty, \qquad \text{for every } \psi\in H^1(\mathbb R)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

Both hypotheses were used, and neither can be dropped: $\psi\in L^2$ rules out a nonzero limit in the last step, $\psi'\in L^2$ is what makes the integral converge in the first place. The vanishing is a consequence of the domain, not a politeness assumed by calling $\psi$ "well-behaved."

With that lemma established, the boundary term vanishes outright (both
$\phi,\psi\to0$), confirming $\hat p$ is symmetric on $H^1(\mathbb R)$:

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "lhs", symbol: "∫φ*(−iℏψ′)dx", gloss: "The matrix element ⟨φ|p̂ψ⟩, the left side of the symmetry check." },
    { id: "boundary", symbol: "[φ*ψ]", gloss: "The boundary term from integration by parts. It vanishes because both φ,ψ → 0 at ±∞, by the lemma just proved." },
    { id: "rhs", symbol: "∫φ′*ψ dx", gloss: "The matrix element ⟨p̂φ|ψ⟩, the right side, once the vanished boundary term is dropped." },
  ]}
>

$$
\int\phi^*(-i\hbar\psi')dx=-i\hbar[\phi^*\psi]_{-\infty}^{\infty}+i\hbar\int\phi'^*\psi\,dx
$$

</EquationReveal>

### Why the full-line $\hat p$ is self-adjoint, not merely symmetric

Symmetry alone leaves open whether $D(\hat p^\dagger)$ might be
*strictly larger* than $H^1(\mathbb R)$. It isn't, and the cleanest way
to see this uses the Fourier transform (Momentum Space and the Fourier
Transform): $\hat p$ is unitarily equivalent, via the Fourier transform,
to the operator "multiply by $\hbar k$" acting on $\phi(k)$. A
multiplication-by-a-real-function operator is manifestly self-adjoint on
its natural maximal domain $\{\phi\in L^2:\;k\phi(k)\in L^2\}$ (no
integration by parts or boundary term is even needed: multiplication
by a real number is its own adjoint entrywise), and that maximal domain
transforms back, under the same unitary Fourier transform, to exactly
$H^1(\mathbb R)$. So $D(\hat p)=D(\hat p^\dagger)=H^1(\mathbb R)$: the
full-line momentum operator is self-adjoint, not merely symmetric; this
is why every calculation built on $\hat p$ so far in this platform was
safe, even though the domain question was never raised.

### Where it breaks: momentum on a half-line

Consider $\hat p=-i\,d/dx$ on $L^2(0,\infty)$ (natural units,
$\hbar=1$), the operator that would represent "momentum toward the
wall" for a particle confined to $x>0$ by a hard wall at $x=0$. Try the
symmetric domain $D(\hat p)=\{\psi\in H^1(0,\infty):\psi(0)=0\}$
(the natural guess: a Dirichlet condition at the one boundary point,
directly mirroring the well-worn "wavefunction vanishes at a wall").
Whether *any* choice of domain makes this operator self-adjoint is
settled by von Neumann's deficiency-index theorem.

<TheoremBox title="von Neumann's deficiency-index criterion" provenance="cited">

Let $A$ be a **densely defined symmetric** operator on a Hilbert space
$\mathcal H$, and define its **deficiency indices**

$$
n_+ = \dim\ker(A^\dagger - i), \qquad n_- = \dim\ker(A^\dagger + i).
$$

Then $A$ admits a self-adjoint extension **if and only if** $n_+=n_-$.
When $n_+=n_-=n\geq1$, the self-adjoint extensions are in one-to-one
correspondence with the unitary maps $\ker(A^\dagger-i)\to\ker(A^\dagger+i)$,
an $n^2$-real-parameter family. When $n_+=n_-=0$, $A$ is already
essentially self-adjoint and the extension is unique.

The hypotheses are only *densely defined* and *symmetric*. Nothing about
boundedness, and nothing about which boundary condition was tried; a
mismatch $n_+\ne n_-$ therefore rules out every domain at once, not just
the one in front of you. Statement and proof: von Neumann (1929);
Reed–Simon, *Methods of Modern Mathematical Physics* II, §X.1.

</TheoremBox>

So the question "is there a self-adjoint momentum on the half-line?"
reduces to counting $L^2(0,\infty)$ solutions of
$\hat p^\dagger\psi=+i\psi$ and $\hat p^\dagger\psi=-i\psi$.

The two shapes are the whole argument. The $e^{-t}$ curve levels off
into a horizontal line: by $X=3$ it has already accumulated $0.4992$ of
its eventual $\tfrac12$, and the rest of the half-line contributes almost
nothing, so the limit exists and the function is in $L^2(0,\infty)$. The
$e^{+t}$ curve becomes a *straight line* on the log axis, of measured
slope $0.869$ per unit $X$, which is $2/\ln10$: exponential growth
without bound. It reaches $8.14\times10^4$ by $X=6$ and would run off
any frame you drew. A horizontal asymptote versus a line of constant
positive slope is what "normalizable" and "not normalizable" look like
when you plot the criterion itself instead of the function.

So the $\psi'=-\psi$ equation (eigenvalue $+i$)
has exactly one $L^2(0,\infty)$ solution ($n_+=1$), while $\psi'=+\psi$
(eigenvalue $-i$) has none ($n_-=0$). **$n_+\ne n_-$**: by von Neumann's theorem,
**no self-adjoint extension of $\hat p$ on the half-line exists at
all**, not the Dirichlet choice above, not any other local boundary
condition. Every domain choice leaves it merely symmetric at best.

## Choosing a Domain Is Choosing a Boundary Condition

<InsightBlock>
  Choosing a domain is choosing a boundary condition, and a boundary
  condition is physics. That is why domain bookkeeping cannot be deferred:
  an operator can pass every algebraic test you can write down and still
  fail to describe anything, because the failure lives in a place the
  algebra never looks.
</InsightBlock>

<Term id="self-adjoint-operator">Self-adjointness</Term> is the hypothesis
Stone's theorem needs, and the theorem is worth stating rather than gesturing at:
every strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group $U(t)$ on
$\mathcal H$ has the form $U(t)=e^{-iAt/\hbar}$ for a unique
**self-adjoint** $A$, and conversely every self-adjoint $A$ generates
one. (Cited, not proved here; Reed–Simon II, §VIII.4.) Symmetry is not
enough for either direction. A merely symmetric operator can fail to
generate *any* consistent unitary evolution, so probability would not be
reliably conserved if you tried to use it as a Hamiltonian or a
generator of translations.

The half-line result therefore reads physically: **there is no
self-adjoint "momentum toward the wall" operator for a particle
confined to a half-line by a hard, impenetrable wall.** That is not a
gap to work around. Momentum *is* the generator of translations, and
translations are not a symmetry of a space with an inaccessible edge on
one side; pushing far enough in the $-x$ direction runs the particle
through the wall. The deficiency count $n_+\ne n_-$ is the analytic
signature of that broken symmetry. Momentum survives on spaces with no
one-sided edge, the whole line or a periodic interval, where both
directions are structurally alike.

## Integration by Parts Mistaken for a Proof

<Callout type="mistake">
**Treating "$A=A^\dagger$ by integration by parts" as the complete
self-adjointness check.** That calculation only ever shows $A$ is
*symmetric* on whatever domain you assumed. Self-adjointness additionally
requires $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ exactly, a domain question that's
automatically settled (and hence invisible) in finite dimensions, but
a real open question on a function space, as the half-line example shows: a
domain can be symmetric and still admit no self-adjoint extension.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $L^2$ membership alone implies a function vanishes at
infinity.** It doesn't: only the *combination* $\psi,\psi'\in L^2$
(the hypothesis of $\hat p$'s domain) forces the vanishing, via
the Cauchy–Schwarz argument derived above. A function can be square
integrable while spiking arbitrarily high on ever-narrower intervals
without tending to any limit at all.
</Callout>

1. Redo the deficiency-index calculation for $\hat p=-i\,d/dx$ on
   $L^2(-\infty,0)$ (a half-line extending the *other* direction).
   Which of $n_+,n_-$ is now $1$, and which is $0$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $n_-=1$ and $n_+=0$, the exact reverse of the $(0,\infty)$ case.

   The eigenvalue equations do not change at all, because the deficiency
   indices are read off $\hat p^\dagger$ acting on the maximal domain, and
   the differential expression $-i\,d/dx$ has no idea which half-line it
   is sitting on:

   $$
   \hat p^\dagger\psi=\pm i\psi \ \Longrightarrow\ \psi'=\mp\psi \ \Longrightarrow\ \psi(x)=Ce^{\mp x}.
   $$

   What changes is the normalization integral, and it changes decisively,
   because the end of the interval that runs to infinity has moved:

   $$
   \int_{-\infty}^{0}|e^{-x}|^2dx=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-2x}dx=\infty,
   \qquad
   \int_{-\infty}^{0}|e^{+x}|^2dx=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{2x}dx=\tfrac12 .
   $$

   The growing and decaying exponentials have traded places, because
   "decaying" was never a property of the function alone: it is a
   statement about which end of the domain is being exhausted. The
   eigenvalue-$(-i)$ solution $e^{+x}$ is now the normalizable one, with
   the same norm $\tfrac12$ that `half-line-deficiency-normalization`
   computes for $e^{-x}$ on the right half-line.

   The verdict does not trade places. Von Neumann's criterion asks only
   whether $n_+=n_-$, and $0\ne1$ just as surely as $1\ne0$, so the left
   half-line momentum operator has no self-adjoint extension either. The
   physics reads the same way: a space with one impenetrable edge is not
   translation invariant, and translation invariance does not care which
   side the edge is on.

   Run the same count on the whole line as a check. Neither exponential
   is in $L^2(\mathbb R)$, since $e^{-x}$ blows up at $-\infty$ and
   $e^{+x}$ blows up at $+\infty$, so $n_+=n_-=0$: essential
   self-adjointness with a unique extension, agreeing with the
   Fourier-transform argument given above.

   The wrong turn is copying the lesson's two integrals verbatim and
   reporting $n_+=1,n_-=0$ again. It is seductive because the ODE
   solutions really are identical and only the limits of integration
   move, which makes the changed part look like bookkeeping. The habit
   that protects you is refusing to call a function "decaying" without
   naming the limit: $e^{+x}$ decays as $x\to-\infty$, and here that is
   the only limit in play.

   </details>

2. Why does the argument that $D(\hat p)$-functions vanish at infinity
   fail if only $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ is assumed, without also
   assuming $\psi'\in L^2(\mathbb R)$? Identify exactly which step of the
   derivation breaks.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Step two breaks: the Cauchy-Schwarz bound

   $$
   \int|\psi'\psi^*|\,dx\le\|\psi'\|_{L^2}\|\psi\|_{L^2}<\infty
   $$

   has $\|\psi'\|_{L^2}$ on its right side. Drop $\psi'\in L^2$ and that
   factor is $+\infty$, so the inequality is vacuously true and asserts
   nothing. The product $\psi'\psi^*$ need not be integrable at all.

   Trace what that costs downstream, because the damage is not where it
   is usually looked for. Step three, the identity

   $$
   |\psi(x)|^2-|\psi(0)|^2=\int_0^x2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\psi'\psi^*\big)\,dt,
   $$

   is still *true*; it is just the fundamental theorem of calculus applied
   to step one. What it loses is convergence: without an absolutely
   integrable integrand, the right side need not approach a limit as
   $x\to+\infty$, so $|\psi(x)|^2$ need not approach a limit either. Step
   four is then arguing about an object that does not exist. Its logic is
   "the limit cannot be positive, therefore it is zero," which presupposes
   that a limit is there to be pinned down. With no limit, that inference
   is a non sequitur, and the conclusion $\psi(x)\to0$ is simply
   unavailable.

   A concrete $L^2$ function that fails the conclusion makes the gap
   tangible. Put a smooth spike of height $n$ and width $n^{-4}$ centred
   at $x=n$, for every integer $n\ge1$, and zero elsewhere. Then

   $$
   \int|\psi|^2dx\;\lesssim\;\sum_{n\ge1}n^2\cdot n^{-4}=\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}<\infty,
   $$

   so $\psi\in L^2(\mathbb R)$, yet $\psi(n)=n$, so $\psi$ does not tend to
   zero and is not even bounded. It is perfectly smooth, so nothing about
   differentiability is at issue. Its derivative runs at order
   $n/n^{-4}=n^5$ across each spike, giving $\int|\psi'|^2\sim n^{10}\cdot
   n^{-4}=n^6$ per spike, wildly divergent, exactly as the lemma demands
   of any counterexample.

   The wrong turn is naming step four, because that is the step where
   $\psi\in L^2$ appears and $L^2$ feels like the hypothesis being
   weakened. Step four is fine: it uses only the hypothesis that was kept.
   The failure happened two steps earlier and silently, at the moment an
   integral was declared convergent. A second wrong turn is answering that
   without $\psi'\in L^2$ the function need not be differentiable, so step
   one fails. The spike function above is smooth, and step one holds for
   it. What $\psi'\in L^2$ supplies is not local regularity but *global*
   control on how much total variation the function is allowed to spend
   out at infinity, and that is the thing being spent when the spikes keep
   getting taller.

   </details>

3. A periodic interval $[0,L]$ with the boundary condition
   $\psi(0)=\psi(L)$ (not $\psi(0)=\psi(L)=0$) turns out to admit a
   one-parameter family of self-adjoint extensions of $\hat p$,
   $\{\psi:\psi(L)=e^{i\theta}\psi(0)\}$ for each real $\theta$. Using
   this lesson's boundary-term argument, explain in one sentence why
   *this* boundary condition (unlike the half-line's) makes the term
   $[\phi^*\psi]_0^L$ vanish.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   In one sentence: an interval has two finite endpoints, so the boundary
   term can cancel *between* them rather than having to vanish *at* each
   of them, and because $\phi$ and $\psi$ are drawn from the same domain
   they carry the same twist, whose modulus is one.

   Written out, with $\psi(L)=e^{i\theta}\psi(0)$ and
   $\phi(L)=e^{i\theta}\phi(0)$, so that $\phi^*(L)=e^{-i\theta}\phi^*(0)$:

   $$
   [\phi^*\psi]_0^L=\phi^*(L)\psi(L)-\phi^*(0)\psi(0)=e^{-i\theta}e^{i\theta}\phi^*(0)\psi(0)-\phi^*(0)\psi(0)=0 .
   $$

   The unimodularity $|e^{i\theta}|=1$ is doing all the work. Any other
   multiplier would rescale one endpoint relative to the other and leave a
   residue.

   The half-line has no such partner. It has one finite endpoint, at
   $x=0$; the other end is $+\infty$, where the $H^1$ lemma already sends
   the contribution to zero on its own. So there is nothing for
   $\phi^*(0)\psi(0)$ to cancel against, and the only way to kill it is to
   demand $\psi(0)=0$ outright. That does make $\hat p$ symmetric, but it
   over-constrains $D(\hat p)$ relative to $D(\hat p^\dagger)=H^1(0,\infty)$,
   which carries no boundary condition at all, and the leftover room is
   what the deficiency count measures.

   The abstract count agrees, which is worth checking rather than
   assuming. On $[0,L]$ both candidate solutions $e^{-x}$ and $e^{+x}$ are
   continuous on a bounded interval and therefore both lie in $L^2(0,L)$,
   so $n_+=n_-=1$. Von Neumann's theorem then predicts a family indexed by
   the unitary maps of a one-dimensional space, which is
   $U(1)=\{e^{i\theta}\}$: one real parameter, $n^2=1$. That is exactly the
   family the question names, arriving from the theorem rather than from
   guessing boundary conditions.

   The wrong turn is claiming the term vanishes at each endpoint
   separately. It vanishes at neither; $\phi^*(0)\psi(0)$ is generically
   nonzero and so is $\phi^*(L)\psi(L)$. A subtler and more instructive
   wrong turn is letting $\phi$ and $\psi$ carry *different* twists,
   $\psi(L)=e^{i\theta}\psi(0)$ and $\phi(L)=e^{i\theta'}\phi(0)$. The
   leftover factor is then $e^{i(\theta-\theta')}$ and the boundary term
   survives. That is not a technicality to wave through: it is why the
   $\theta$ label distinct self-adjoint operators instead of one operator
   with a free knob. Each $\theta$ is a different physical system, a
   different magnetic flux threaded through the ring, and a domain built
   from two different twists at once is not symmetric to begin with.

   </details>

## Summary

- $L^2(\mathbb R)$ is a Hilbert space: complete (Riesz–Fischer,
  cited) and separable (the Hermite/harmonic-oscillator basis, concrete).
- A **symmetric** operator satisfies $\langle\phi|A\psi\rangle=\langle
  A\phi|\psi\rangle$ on its domain $D(A)$; it's **self-adjoint** only if
  $D(A)=D(A^\dagger)$ exactly, a strictly stronger condition invisible in
  finite dimensions.
- $\hat p=-i\hbar\,d/dx$ on the whole line is self-adjoint on
  $D(\hat p)=H^1(\mathbb R)$, derivable via the Fourier-transform
  equivalence to a self-adjoint multiplication operator; every $H^1$
  function provably vanishes at $\pm\infty$, which is what makes
  the earlier boundary-term calculation legitimate.
- $\hat p$ on a half-line has unequal deficiency indices ($n_+=1$,
  $n_-=0$, computed directly), so by von Neumann's theorem it has **no**
  self-adjoint extension at all, a worked case where the
  informal "check $A=A^\dagger$" recipe would have missed a real
  mathematical obstruction.
