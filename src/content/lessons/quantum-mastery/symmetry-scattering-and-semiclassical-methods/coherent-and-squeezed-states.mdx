## The Oscillator State That Behaves Classically

The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator built energy eigenstates $|n\rangle$
algebraically, states of definite energy, but *maximally* non-classical
in another sense: $\langle x\rangle=\langle p\rangle=0$ for every
$|n\rangle$ with $n>0$, nothing at all like a classical oscillator's
position and momentum sweeping back and forth in time. **Coherent
states** are the opposite extreme: not energy eigenstates, but the states
that most closely track a classical trajectory, and (not a coincidence)
exactly the states real laser light approximates. This lesson builds them
directly from $a,a^\dagger$, no new postulate needed.

## Eigenstates of a Non-Hermitian Operator

### Coherent states as eigenstates of â

Define $|\alpha\rangle$ (for any complex $\alpha$) by
$a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$. Since $a$ is *not* Hermitian, this
eigenvalue equation is perfectly consistent with a complex eigenvalue,
unlike anything from Hermitian Operators. Expanding
$|\alpha\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle$ in the Fock basis and using
$a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle$:

$$
a|\alpha\rangle=\sum_n c_n\sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle = \alpha\sum_n c_n|n\rangle
$$

Matching coefficients of $|n\rangle$ (shift the left sum's index):
$c_{n+1}\sqrt{n+1}=\alpha c_n$, i.e. $c_{n+1}=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{n+1}}c_n$.
Iterating from $c_0$: $c_n=\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}c_0$. Normalizing
($\sum_n|c_n|^2=|c_0|^2e^{|\alpha|^2}=1$) fixes $c_0=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}$:

$$
\boxed{\;|\alpha\rangle = e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\,|n\rangle\;}
$$

The number-state probabilities $|c_n|^2=e^{-|\alpha|^2}|\alpha|^{2n}/n!$
are exactly **Poisson-distributed** with mean $\langle N\rangle=|\alpha|^2$
(a checkable statement: this is the Poisson distribution's own
closed form). Built directly from this platform's own truncated
`annihilationOperator`/`creationOperator` matrices (the same finite Fock
basis The Quantum Harmonic Oscillator used):

### Coherent states saturate the uncertainty bound

Write $x=\sqrt{\tfrac1{2}}(a+a^\dagger)$, $p=i\sqrt{\tfrac12}(a^\dagger-a)$
(natural units $m=\omega=\hbar=1$, this platform's own `positionOperator`
convention). For real $\alpha$, using $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$,
$\langle\alpha|a^\dagger=\alpha\langle\alpha|$, and $aa^\dagger=a^\dagger
a+1$:

$$
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt2\,\alpha, \qquad
\langle x^2\rangle = \tfrac12\left(2\alpha^2+2\alpha^2+1\right)=2\alpha^2+\tfrac12
\;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta x^2 = \tfrac12
$$

$$
\langle p\rangle = 0, \qquad \langle p^2\rangle = \tfrac12 \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta p^2=\tfrac12
$$

$$
\Delta x\,\Delta p = \tfrac12
$$

**Exactly** the Heisenberg minimum from Expectation Values and
Uncertainty, and $\Delta x=\Delta p=1/\sqrt2$: identical to the ground
state $|0\rangle$'s own spread. This is the coherent state's defining
feature: it's a *displaced* vacuum, same shape, shifted mean position and
momentum, which is why it tracks a classical trajectory (the
"blob" doesn't spread or distort as $\langle x\rangle,\langle p\rangle$
oscillate). Checked directly against the real engine's coherent-state
amplitudes at $\alpha=1.5$: $\Delta x=\Delta p=0.70710678$ and
$\Delta x\,\Delta p=0.50000000$, both to eight digits.

That agreement is worth one paragraph of scepticism, because every
operator in the computation is a $30\times30$ truncation of an
operator that lives on an infinite-dimensional space, and
truncation is not innocent. On a finite Fock space the canonical
commutator *fails*: computing $[a,a^\dagger]$ in this platform's own
$30$-level matrices gives $1$ on the first $29$ diagonal entries and
$1-30=-29$ on the last. It has to, since a finite matrix commutator is
traceless and $\mathbb 1$ is not, so no finite matrices satisfy
$[a,a^\dagger]=\mathbb 1$ at all. Every derivation above used that
commutator. The numbers come out right anyway for one checkable reason:
the coherent state at $\alpha=1.5$ carries weight $2\times10^{-22}$ at
$n=29$, so the one corrupted entry is never touched. Push $\alpha$ up
toward $\sqrt{30}$ and the same code would start returning nonsense with
no warning. The infinite-dimensional statement is the true one; the
matrix is a well-chosen approximation to it, valid here because the state
is confined far below the ceiling.

### Squeezed states: unequal spread, still minimal

Coherent states are not the *only* minimum-uncertainty states, just the
ones with $\Delta x=\Delta p$. Define a new operator via a **Bogoliubov
transformation**, $b=a\cosh r+a^\dagger\sinh r$ (real squeeze parameter
$r$), and its eigenstates $b|\beta\rangle=\beta|\beta\rangle$: the
<Term id="squeezed-states">**squeezed states**. $b$ satisfies</Term> the *same* commutation relation as
$a$: $[b,b^\dagger]=\cosh^2r\,[a,a^\dagger]+\sinh^2r\,[a^\dagger,a]=(\cosh^2r-\sinh^2r)[a,a^\dagger]=1$,
so everything the earlier coherent-state derivation used (eigenstates
existing, normalizability, the Fock-like expansion argument) carries over
to $b,|\beta\rangle$ unchanged, just relabeled.

Inverting the Bogoliubov transform, $a=b\cosh r-b^\dagger\sinh r$
(direct algebra: $b\cosh r-b^\dagger\sinh r=(a\cosh r+a^\dagger\sinh
r)\cosh r-(a^\dagger\cosh r+a\sinh r)\sinh r=a(\cosh^2r-\sinh^2r)=a$),
checked here as an exact matrix identity. This one really does hold at
*any* truncation, and it is worth seeing why it escapes the problem just
raised: $b\cosh r-b^\dagger\sinh r=a(\cosh^2r-\sinh^2r)$ is a linear
identity among the matrices themselves, using no commutator anywhere, so
the corrupted last row of $[a,a^\dagger]$ never enters. The panels below
agree to $4\times10^{-16}$ in a $6$-level basis, which is far too small
to hold a coherent state and still exact for this identity. The
$[b,b^\dagger]=1$ claim above is the opposite case: it *does* rest on the
commutator, and so it is an infinite-dimensional statement that no
truncation reproduces exactly.

Substituting into $x=\sqrt{\tfrac12}(a+a^\dagger)$:
$a+a^\dagger=(\cosh r-\sinh r)(b+b^\dagger)=e^{-r}(b+b^\dagger)$ (using
$\cosh r-\sinh r=e^{-r}$), so $x=e^{-r}\times\sqrt{\tfrac12}(b+b^\dagger)$.
Since $b,b^\dagger$ obey the identical algebra $a,a^\dagger$ did, the
*same* calculation that gave $\Delta\left[\sqrt{\tfrac12}(a+a^\dagger)\right]=1/\sqrt2$
for $|\alpha\rangle$ gives $\Delta\left[\sqrt{\tfrac12}(b+b^\dagger)\right]=1/\sqrt2$
for $|\beta\rangle$, so:

$$
\Delta x = \frac{e^{-r}}{\sqrt2}
$$

Similarly $a^\dagger-a=(\cosh r+\sinh r)(b^\dagger-b)=e^{r}(b^\dagger-b)$,
giving:

$$
\Delta p = \frac{e^{r}}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{e^{-r}}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{e^{r}}{\sqrt2}=\frac12
$$

**Still exactly minimal**, for *any* $r$: squeezing trades $\Delta x$
against $\Delta p$ in inverse lockstep, never violating (or loosening)
the Heisenberg bound.

## What Squeezing Trades Away

"Squeezing" is what the name suggests: at $r=0.5$, position spread
shrinks to about $61\%$ of the coherent-state value while momentum spread
grows to about $165\%$, the ellipse of uncertainty (a visual
picture, even though this lesson stays in the algebra) stretched along
one quadrature and compressed along the other, its *area* fixed by
Heisenberg. This isn't a mathematical curiosity: squeezed light is a real
laboratory tool (LIGO's gravitational-wave detector uses squeezed light
specifically to push measurement noise below what an ordinary coherent
state, let alone classical light, could achieve on the "compressed"
quadrature, at the unavoidable cost of extra noise on the other one).

## The Uncertainty Product, Still Exactly One Half

**Compute $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ for $r=0.5$ directly from the boxed
formulas, and confirm the product is still exactly $\tfrac12$.**

$e^{-0.5}\approx0.6065$, so $\Delta x=0.6065/\sqrt2\approx0.4289$.
$e^{0.5}\approx1.6487$, so $\Delta p=1.6487/\sqrt2\approx1.1658$.
Product: $0.4289\times1.1658\approx0.5000$, matching $\tfrac12$ to four
decimal places, a direct numeric confirmation (not just the general
algebraic argument) that squeezing preserves the minimum-
uncertainty product while redistributing it unevenly.

## Assuming Minimal Uncertainty Means Equal Spread

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming any state with small $\Delta x$ is "squeezed."** Squeezed
states are specifically the minimum-uncertainty states with *unequal*
$\Delta x,\Delta p$ that still saturate $\Delta x\Delta p=\tfrac12$
exactly; a state with small $\Delta x$ but $\Delta x\Delta p\gg\tfrac12$
(most states) is just localized, not squeezed in this technical sense.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking $a$ being non-Hermitian makes $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$
suspicious.** Only Hermitian operators are guaranteed *real* eigenvalues
and an orthonormal eigenbasis (Hermitian Operators); $a$ is not Hermitian,
so a complex eigenvalue $\alpha$ and a non-orthogonal eigenbasis
(distinct $|\alpha\rangle$'s have nonzero overlap) are both expected, not
contradictions.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. For $\alpha=2$, what is the coherent state's mean photon number
   $\langle N\rangle$, and what specific probability distribution
   describes $P(n)$ around it?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\langle N\rangle=|\alpha|^2=4$, and $P(n)$ is the Poisson distribution
   with mean $4$:

   $$
   P(n)=|c_n|^2=e^{-|\alpha|^2}\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}=e^{-4}\frac{4^n}{n!}.
   $$

   That comes straight off the boxed Fock expansion, with no extra
   physics: $c_n=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!}$, so
   $|c_n|^2=e^{-|\alpha|^2}|\alpha|^{2n}/n!$, which is the Poisson mass
   function with $\lambda=|\alpha|^2$ written out. Reading $\langle
   N\rangle=|\alpha|^2$ off it is then just the standard fact that a
   Poisson distribution's mean is its parameter.

   The distribution's shape at $\lambda=4$ has one feature worth noticing,
   because it is the giveaway that this is Poisson and not a Gaussian
   dressed up. It is *doubly* peaked at $n=3$ and $n=4$, with
   $P(3)=P(4)=0.1954$, and asymmetric about the mean: $P(2)=0.1465$ but
   $P(6)=0.1042$, and the right tail runs much further than the left. The
   tie is exact whenever $\lambda$ is a whole number, since
   $P(\lambda)/P(\lambda-1)=\lambda/\lambda=1$.

   The physically loaded consequence is the variance. For a Poisson
   distribution $\mathrm{Var}(N)=\lambda$, so

   $$
   \Delta N=\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}=|\alpha|=2,\qquad
   \frac{\Delta N}{\langle N\rangle}=\frac{1}{|\alpha|}=\frac12.
   $$

   This is shot noise, and it is the reason "the laser is stable" and "the
   photon number is definite" are different claims. A coherent state is
   not an eigenstate of $N$ and its photon number fluctuates by $\sqrt{\langle
   N\rangle}$ every time you count, no matter how quiet the laser is. The
   Fano factor $\mathrm{Var}(N)/\langle N\rangle$ equals exactly $1$, which
   is the standard dividing line: light with Fano factor above $1$ is
   super-Poissonian (thermal light, where the distribution is
   Bose-Einstein and much broader), below $1$ is sub-Poissonian and has no
   classical description at all (a Fock state $|n\rangle$ sits at the
   extreme, Fano factor $0$).

   One hypothesis is doing real work here: this is the distribution for an
   ideal single-mode coherent state. Calling a real laser beam Poissonian
   is a very good approximation well above threshold and a poor one near
   it, and any loss the beam suffers keeps it Poissonian while shrinking
   $|\alpha|$, which is a special property of coherent states rather than
   a general fact about attenuated light.

   </details>

2. Compute $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ for a squeezed state with $r=1$, and
   confirm the product is still $\tfrac12$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \Delta x=\frac{e^{-1}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{0.3678794}{1.4142136}=0.26013005,
   \qquad
   \Delta p=\frac{e^{1}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{2.7182818}{1.4142136}=1.92211551.
   $$

   $$
   \Delta x\,\Delta p=\frac{e^{-1}}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{e^{1}}{\sqrt2}=\frac{e^{-1}e^{1}}{2}=\frac12.
   $$

   Numerically $0.26013005\times1.92211551=0.500000000000$, and it is worth
   being clear that this is not a coincidence surviving to twelve digits.
   The product is $\tfrac12$ as an algebraic identity, because $e^{-r}$ and
   $e^{r}$ multiply to $1$ for every real $r$; the numeric evaluation only
   confirms the arithmetic was done correctly, and would agree to whatever
   precision it was carried out in.

   Compared with $r=0.5$ in the worked example above, the trade has become
   much more lopsided. At $r=1$, position spread is $e^{-1}=36.8\%$ of the
   coherent-state value and momentum spread is $e^{1}=271.8\%$ of it, where
   at $r=0.5$ the figures were $61\%$ and $165\%$. The trade is
   exponential in $r$, so each additional unit of $r$ costs a factor $e$ on
   one quadrature to buy a factor $e$ on the other, and the experimental
   difficulty grows accordingly. Optics quotes this in decibels of noise
   power, $10\log_{10}(e^{2r})=20r/\ln10$, so $r=1$ is $8.69$ dB of
   squeezing, which is a substantial figure for a real apparatus.

   The wrong turn worth naming: reading a small $\Delta x$ as "more
   information about $x$, for free". Nothing is free here. The squeezed
   state is useful precisely when the experiment reads out one quadrature
   and can afford to be blind on the conjugate one, which is why
   interferometers use it and why a measurement sensitive to both
   quadratures gains nothing at all from squeezing.

   </details>

3. Explain why $[b,b^\dagger]=1$ (shown above from
   $[b,b^\dagger]=\cosh^2r[a,a^\dagger]+\sinh^2r[a^\dagger,a]$) is exactly
   what's needed for $b$'s eigenstates to exist as a normalizable family,
   the same way $[a,a^\dagger]=1$ was needed for $|\alpha\rangle$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the entire construction of $|\alpha\rangle$ used *only* the
   commutator, never anything else about $a$. Go back through it and check:

   The Fock ladder came from $[a,a^\dagger]=1$. That single relation gives
   $[N,a]=-a$ and $[N,a^\dagger]=a^\dagger$ for $N=a^\dagger a$, hence a
   spectrum of $N$ that is bounded below (since $\langle N\rangle=\|a\psi\|^2\geq0$)
   and spaced by exactly $1$, hence an orthonormal basis $|n\rangle$ with
   $a|n\rangle=\sqrt n|n-1\rangle$ and $a^\dagger|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$.

   The recursion came from that ladder. Writing $|\alpha\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle$
   and imposing $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ gave
   $c_{n+1}=\alpha c_n/\sqrt{n+1}$, so $c_n=\alpha^nc_0/\sqrt{n!}$.

   Normalizability came from the resulting series. $\sum_n|\alpha|^{2n}/n!=e^{|\alpha|^2}$
   converges for *every* complex $\alpha$, which is why the eigenstates
   form a family indexed by the whole plane rather than a discrete set.

   Not one of those three steps mentioned $x$, $p$, or the oscillator
   Hamiltonian. So any operator satisfying the same commutator inherits the
   same conclusions verbatim: $[b,b^\dagger]=1$ gives a $b$-Fock basis
   $|n\rangle_b$, the same recursion in that basis, the same $e^{|\beta|^2}$,
   and therefore a normalizable $|\beta\rangle$ for every complex $\beta$.
   That is the whole content of the Bogoliubov transformation being
   **canonical**, and $\cosh^2r-\sinh^2r=1$ is the one line where it is
   checked.

   The argument is much sharper if you ask what goes wrong otherwise.
   Take the general linear combination $b=ua+va^\dagger$ with real $u,v$,
   so $[b,b^\dagger]=u^2-v^2=\kappa$, and consider the three cases.

   If $\kappa>0$, rescale $b\to b/\sqrt\kappa$ and the canonical case is
   recovered unchanged; only the labelling of $\beta$ shifts. Nothing new.

   If $\kappa<0$, then $b^\dagger/\sqrt{|\kappa|}$ is the annihilation
   operator and $b$ is proportional to a *creation* operator. A creation
   operator has no eigenvectors whatsoever. Impose
   $a^\dagger|\psi\rangle=\beta|\psi\rangle$ on $|\psi\rangle=\sum_nc_n|n\rangle$:
   the coefficient of $|0\rangle$ on the left is zero, forcing $\beta
   c_0=0$, and the coefficient of $|n+1\rangle$ gives
   $c_{n+1}=c_n\sqrt{n+1}/\beta$. So for $\beta\neq0$ we get $c_0=0$ and
   the recursion propagates that zero to every $c_n$; for $\beta=0$ the
   recursion runs the same way with the same conclusion. The point spectrum
   is empty, not merely awkward. This is the case $b=a\cos\vartheta+a^\dagger\sin\vartheta$
   with $\vartheta>45°$ falls into, which is why the hyperbolic functions
   and not the trigonometric ones are the right parametrization.

   If $\kappa=0$, then $u=v$ and $b=u(a+a^\dagger)=u\sqrt2\,x$ is
   proportional to the position operator: Hermitian, with purely
   continuous spectrum. Its "eigenvectors" are the position kets, which
   are not vectors in the Hilbert space at all, only elements of the
   larger distribution space that Continuous Spectra and Rigged Hilbert
   Space sets up. The eigenvalue equation still has solutions in that
   weaker sense, but there is no normalizable family, and the whole
   coherent-state analogy collapses.

   One further hypothesis is not algebraic and deserves naming, because
   the commutator alone does not supply it: the $b$-vacuum has to be a
   genuine vector in the *original* oscillator's Hilbert space, not just an
   abstract Fock-space object. It is, for every finite $r$, because
   $b=S(r)\,a\,S^\dagger(r)$ with $S(r)=\exp[\tfrac r2(a^2-(a^\dagger)^2)]$
   unitary, so the $b$-Fock basis is the image of the ordinary one under a
   unitary map and the squeezed vacuum's Fock coefficients (which go like
   $\tanh^n r$) are square-summable whenever $|\tanh r|<1$. Take $r$ to
   infinity and that fails, which is the finite-dimensional shadow of a
   real phenomenon: in quantum field theory, Bogoliubov transformations
   that are not unitarily implementable produce genuinely inequivalent
   vacua.

   </details>

4. As $r\to0$, what do the squeezed-state formulas for $\Delta x,\Delta p$
   reduce to, and why does that make physical sense given how $b$ was
   defined?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Both collapse to the coherent-state value:
   $\Delta x=e^{0}/\sqrt2=1/\sqrt2$ and $\Delta p=e^{0}/\sqrt2=1/\sqrt2$,
   with the product still $\tfrac12$.

   That is forced by the definition rather than being a limit that happens
   to work out. At $r=0$, $\cosh r=1$ and $\sinh r=0$, so
   $b=a\cosh r+a^\dagger\sinh r$ is literally $a$, its eigenvalue equation
   $b|\beta\rangle=\beta|\beta\rangle$ is literally $a|\alpha\rangle=\alpha|\alpha\rangle$,
   and $|\beta\rangle$ *is* the coherent state $|\alpha=\beta\rangle$. There
   is no separate calculation to do. The squeezed states are a
   one-parameter deformation of the coherent states that contains them as
   its $r=0$ member, and the deformation is smooth because $\cosh$ and
   $\sinh$ are.

   The first-order behaviour is the part worth extracting, because it shows
   what the exponentials are protecting. Expanding,
   $\Delta x\approx(1-r)/\sqrt2$ and $\Delta p\approx(1+r)/\sqrt2$: to
   leading order the squeeze shrinks one spread and grows the other by the
   same additive amount. But the linearized product is
   $(1-r)(1+r)/2=(1-r^2)/2$, which sits *below* the Heisenberg bound.
   Nothing physical is violated, since the linearization is not a state;
   what it shows is that keeping $\Delta x\Delta p$ pinned at exactly
   $\tfrac12$ requires the multiplicative pair $e^{-r},e^{+r}$ and not
   merely a symmetric-looking additive trade. That is why the Bogoliubov
   parametrization uses hyperbolic functions: they are the ones whose
   defining identity $\cosh^2r-\sinh^2r=1$ makes the trade exactly
   reciprocal at every order in $r$, not just the first.

   Worth reading the limit in reverse as well. Since $r=0$ is an interior
   point of the family and $\Delta x\Delta p$ is constant across it, no
   amount of squeezing ever escapes the bound the coherent state already
   saturates. Squeezing redistributes uncertainty; it never removes any.

   </details>

## Summary

- Coherent states $|\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}\sum_n(\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!})|n\rangle$
  are eigenstates of $a$ (not Hermitian, so a complex eigenvalue is
  expected), with Poisson-distributed photon number, verified directly
  against this platform's own truncated ladder operators.
- They saturate $\Delta x\Delta p=\tfrac12$ with **equal**
  $\Delta x=\Delta p=1/\sqrt2$: a displaced vacuum, the closest quantum
  analogue to a classical oscillator trajectory.
- Squeezed states, eigenstates of the Bogoliubov-transformed
  $b=a\cosh r+a^\dagger\sinh r$, generalize this to **unequal**
  $\Delta x=e^{-r}/\sqrt2$, $\Delta p=e^r/\sqrt2$, still exactly saturating
  the same bound for any $r$.
