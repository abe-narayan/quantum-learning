  const states = berryLoopStates(theta, steps);
  const accumulated = states.slice(1).reduce(
    (acc, current) => {
      const overlap = acc.previous.innerProduct(current);
      return { previous: current, sum: acc.sum + Math.atan2(overlap.im, overlap.re) };

    { previous: states[0], sum: 0 }
  );

## A Phase That Depends Only on the Path

Holonomic and topological quantum computing build logic gates out of
the phase this lesson derives: a control field is walked slowly
around a loop in parameter space, and the qubit picks up a phase that
depends only on the loop's shape, not on how fast or exactly how it was
traversed. Gates built this way inherit that same insensitivity, which is
part of why the geometric phase is treated as a serious resource for
noise-resistant qubit control. Before
any of that is usable, though, the phase itself has to be shown to exist,
and that takes two questions and one answer.

First: if a quantum system's Hamiltonian changes over time, but *slowly*,
does the system simply track along with the instantaneous ground state,
the way a marble stays near the bottom of a slowly-tilting bowl? Second:
if it does, is that all that happens, or does the system remember
something about the *path* the Hamiltonian took, even after returning
exactly to where it started? The first question is the **adiabatic
theorem**. The second question's answer, yes, it remembers, as a
measurable phase, is **Berry's phase**, and both come out of the same
four lines of algebra.

## Leakage Rate, and the Phase That Survives It

### Setting up the adiabatic expansion

Let $H(t)$ have instantaneous eigenstates $|n(t)\rangle$,
$H(t)|n(t)\rangle=E_n(t)|n(t)\rangle$, at every fixed $t$ (a well-defined
notion, since diagonalizing a Hermitian operator at one instant needs no
information about how it changes).

Two hypotheses go in at this point and are used at every step after it.
First, the level of interest stays **non-degenerate** along the whole
path: $E_n(t)\neq E_m(t)$ for every $m\neq n$ and every $t$. That is what
makes $|n(t)\rangle$ a well-defined ray rather than an arbitrary vector
in a subspace, and the leakage formula derived below divides by
$E_n-E_m$, so a level crossing does not merely weaken the theorem; it
destroys the derivation. Second, $|n(t)\rangle$ can be chosen to depend
smoothly on $t$, so that $|\dot n\rangle$ exists. The phase of each
$|n(t)\rangle$ is free at every instant, and the whole geometric-phase
story is about what that freedom does and does not permit.

Both hypotheses can be relaxed, at a cost. If the level is degenerate
throughout, the phase $\gamma_n$ is replaced by a **matrix** acting
within the degenerate subspace, the non-abelian **Wilczek–Zee
holonomy** (1984), and the resulting matrices generally do not commute.
That is the version holonomic quantum computing, named at the outset,
uses to build multi-qubit gates: a scalar phase alone
cannot generate a non-trivial gate, but a non-commuting holonomy can.

Expand the true state as

$$
|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)\,e^{i\theta_n(t)}\,|n(t)\rangle, \qquad
\theta_n(t) = -\frac1\hbar\int_0^t E_n(t')\,dt'
$$

pulling out the ordinary **dynamical phase** $\theta_n$ explicitly, so
$c_n(t)$ captures everything else. Substituting into
$i\hbar\partial_t|\psi\rangle=H|\psi\rangle$ and using
$H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$, the $E_n$ terms cancel *exactly* against
$\dot\theta_n=-E_n/\hbar$ (this is why $\theta_n$ was defined
that way), leaving:

$$
\sum_n \left[\dot c_n|n\rangle + c_n|\dot n\rangle\right]e^{i\theta_n} = 0
$$

Projecting onto $\langle m|e^{-i\theta_m}$ and separating the $n=m$ term
from the rest:

$$
\dot c_m = -c_m\langle m|\dot m\rangle - \sum_{n\neq m} c_n\langle m|\dot n\rangle\, e^{i(\theta_n-\theta_m)}
$$

### The leakage rate between levels

Differentiating the eigenvalue equation $H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$ with
respect to $t$ and projecting onto $\langle m|$ ($m\neq n$, using
$\langle m|H=E_m\langle m|$) gives the rate at which the Hamiltonian's own
change couples level $n$ into level $m$:

$$
\langle m|\dot n\rangle = \frac{\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle}{E_n-E_m}, \qquad m\neq n
$$

This is the derivation's payoff: the coupling out of $|n(t)\rangle$ into
any other level $m$ is small whenever $\hbar|\langle m|\dot
H|n\rangle|\ll|E_n-E_m|^2$, the actual **adiabaticity condition**
("changes slowly" made precise: slow relative to the energy gap squared,
not just slow in some vague sense). When this holds throughout the
evolution, the $n\neq m$ sum above is negligible, and to excellent
approximation:

$$
\dot c_n = -c_n\langle n|\dot n\rangle \;\Longrightarrow\; c_n(t)=c_n(0)\exp\!\left[-\int_0^t\langle n(t')|\dot n(t')\rangle\,dt'\right]
$$

**This is the adiabatic theorem**: a system that starts in $|n(0)\rangle$
stays in $|n(t)\rangle$ for all $t$, up to a phase, never mixing into any
other level, provided the gap-squared condition above holds throughout. A
fully rigorous bound (accounting for the *whole* history, not just the
instantaneous rate) needs more care than this lesson gives; the mechanism
above, the actual leakage rate and why it's suppressed, is the substance
of the argument, presented honestly as a leading-order case rather than a
complete proof.

### The extra phase is geometric, not dynamical

$\langle n|\dot n\rangle$ is **purely imaginary**: differentiating
$\langle n|n\rangle=1$ gives $\langle\dot n|n\rangle+\langle
n|\dot n\rangle=0$, i.e. $\langle n|\dot n\rangle+\overline{\langle
n|\dot n\rangle}=0$, so its real part vanishes. Write
$\langle n|\dot n\rangle=i\dot\gamma_n(t)$ for a real function
$\gamma_n$. Then $c_n(t)=c_n(0)\,e^{i\gamma_n(t)}$: a real phase, on
top of the dynamical phase $\theta_n$ already factored out. And
$\gamma_n(t)=i\int_0^t\langle n(t')|\dot n(t')\rangle\,dt'=i\oint\langle
n|\nabla_R n\rangle\cdot d\vec R$ (chain rule, $R$ the Hamiltonian's slow
parameters) depends only on the **path** $R(t)$ traces through parameter
space, not on how fast it's traversed. This path-dependence, not
time-dependence, is what makes $\gamma_n$ **geometric**: this is
**Berry's phase**.

### Berry phase for spin-1/2 in a rotating field

Take $H(t)=-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}2\,\hat n(t)\cdot\vec\sigma$ (a spin-$\tfrac12$
in a field along a slowly-changing direction $\hat n(t)$, Stern-Gerlach
and The Bloch Sphere's own machinery), with $\hat n(t)$ tracing a cone of
fixed half-angle $\theta$ at azimuthal rate $\dot\phi=\omega\ll\omega_0$
(the adiabaticity condition here: slow compared to the *constant* energy
gap $\hbar\omega_0$ between the two instantaneous eigenstates, a
particularly clean case of the general gap condition above). The
"spin-up-along-the-field" instantaneous eigenstate is exactly The Bloch
Sphere's $|+,\hat n\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$,
this platform's own `blochStateFromAngles`.

Computing $\gamma$ over one full loop ($\phi:0\to2\pi$ at fixed $\theta$)
as a real discretized loop integral, summing the phase of
$\langle\psi(t_i)|\psi(t_{i+1})\rangle$ at each small step (computed step
by step, not read off a formula), and comparing directly to the
famous closed-form prediction $\gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega$, minus half the
**solid angle** $\Omega=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)$ the loop encloses on the
sphere of field directions:

## Geometric, and Measurable

The geometric phase is real, not a computational artifact: interfering a
spin (or a photon, where the analogous effect is well-measured) that took
the loop against one that didn't produces an observable fringe shift. The
loop returns exactly to its starting field direction, so the dynamical
phase alone would predict no leftover phase distinguishing "went around
the loop" from "stayed put"; $\gamma$'s survival despite that shows it is
a property of the *loop itself*, traced out in parameter space. Neither
endpoint accounts for it. That is exactly the sense in which it is
geometric rather than dynamical. The minus sign and
the factor of $\tfrac12$ both trace back to spin-$\tfrac12$'s specific
$\cos(\theta/2),\sin(\theta/2)$ half-angle structure, not to any
arbitrary convention.

## A Spin-Half Dragged Around a Cone

**Compute the <Term id="adiabatic-theorem-berry-phase">Berry phase</Term> for $\theta=60°$ by hand from the solid-angle
formula, and compare to the computed loop integral.**

$\Omega=2\pi(1-\cos60°)=2\pi(1-\tfrac12)=\pi$. So
$\gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega=-\tfrac\pi2\approx-1.5708$, a clean, exactly
checkable value. The platform's discretized loop integral at
$\theta=60°$ gives $\gamma\approx-1.5708$ as well. Across all seven
angles plotted above the largest discrepancy is $4.3\times10^{-7}$, which
is the residual of chopping the loop into $3000$ finite steps, with no
disagreement of substance. That is an independent numerical
confirmation of the closed-form geometric formula, not a restatement
of it.

## Confusing a Ray With a State

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking the adiabatic theorem guarantees the system returns to
*exactly* $|n(0)\rangle$ after a closed loop, with no leftover
difference.** It returns to the same *ray* (state up to overall phase),
but that phase, dynamical plus geometric, is real and physically
detectable via interference; "same state" and "same state up to an
observable relative phase" are different claims.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Confusing the adiabaticity condition with "just go slowly."** The
actual condition, $\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|\ll|E_n-E_m|^2$, is
about the rate of change *relative to the energy gap*: a system with a
tiny gap needs a much slower sweep than one with a large gap, at the
*same* literal speed of change.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the solid angle $\Omega$ and the Berry phase $\gamma$ for
   $\theta=90°$ (the field sweeping exactly along the equator), and
   compare to the numeric value read off the plot above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\Omega=2\pi$ and $\gamma=-\pi$, and at this one angle the discretized
   loop integral returns $-\pi$ *exactly*, not merely to seven digits.

   The solid angle first:

   $$
   \Omega=2\pi(1-\cos90°)=2\pi(1-0)=2\pi,\qquad \gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega=-\pi\approx-3.1415926536.
   $$

   An equatorial loop bisects the sphere, so it encloses exactly half of
   the full $4\pi$, and the Berry phase is minus a quarter of the total
   solid angle available. The plot's numeric point at $\theta=90°$ reads
   $-3.1415926536$, agreeing with the closed form to every digit printed.

   That exactness is worth a second look, because at $\theta=60°$ the same
   3000-step calculation was off by $4.3\times10^{-7}$. Here the residual
   is zero to machine precision, and there is a reason. The discretized
   integral sums the argument of $\langle\psi(\phi_i)|\psi(\phi_{i+1})\rangle$
   over $N$ steps of size $\Delta=2\pi/N$, and with
   $|\psi(\phi)\rangle=\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+e^{i\phi}\sin(\theta/2)|1\rangle$
   that overlap is

   $$
   \langle\psi(\phi_i)|\psi(\phi_{i+1})\rangle=\cos^2(\theta/2)+e^{i\Delta}\sin^2(\theta/2).
   $$

   At $\theta=90°$ the two weights are both $\tfrac12$, so the overlap
   collapses to $\tfrac12(1+e^{i\Delta})=e^{i\Delta/2}\cos(\Delta/2)$,
   whose argument is $\Delta/2$ on the nose. Summing $N$ of those gives
   $N\Delta/2=\pi$ for *any* number of steps, so the answer is exact even
   at $N=3$. Away from the equator the per-step argument is
   $\arctan\!\big[\sin^2(\theta/2)\sin\Delta\,/\,(\cos^2(\theta/2)+\sin^2(\theta/2)\cos\Delta)\big]$,
   which departs from the ideal $\sin^2(\theta/2)\,\Delta$ at order
   $\Delta^3$; $N$ of those leave an $O(\Delta^2)$ residue, which is the
   $4\times10^{-7}$ seen at $N=3000$. Quadrupling $N$ should cut it by
   sixteen, and does.

   The physical content of $\gamma=-\pi$ is the one every treatment of
   this example stops at: $e^{-i\pi}=-1$, so walking the field once around
   the equator brings a spin-1/2 back to *minus* its original state. It is
   the same sign a $2\pi$ rotation produces, arrived at here without
   rotating the spin at all, only the field it follows.

   The tempting wrong turn is to call $-1$ a global phase and therefore
   unobservable. It is global only for a system that took the loop in its
   entirety. In the interference measurement the lesson describes, the
   arm that went around the loop is compared against an arm that did not,
   and a relative factor of $-1$ moves the fringes by half a period, which
   is the largest shift the experiment can produce. The practical
   complication is the opposite one: the dynamical phase $\theta_n$ is
   also accumulating during the loop and is generally huge, so a real
   experiment has to cancel it (spin echo, or running the two arms with
   opposite loop orientation) before the geometric part is visible.

   </details>

2. Explain in one or two sentences why $\langle n|\dot n\rangle$ being
   purely imaginary is essential to $\gamma_n$ coming out real (a
   physically sensible phase), rather than some complex number.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $c_n(t)=c_n(0)\exp[-\int\langle n|\dot n\rangle\,dt']$ puts
   $\langle n|\dot n\rangle$ in an exponent: its imaginary part rotates
   $c_n$, and its real part would rescale $|c_n|$. A real part is
   therefore not a complex phase at all, it is growth or decay of the
   amplitude, which unitary evolution inside a single non-degenerate level
   cannot produce.

   And it cannot appear, for a reason that costs one line. Differentiating
   the normalization $\langle n(t)|n(t)\rangle=1$ gives
   $\langle\dot n|n\rangle+\langle n|\dot n\rangle=2\,\mathrm{Re}\langle
   n|\dot n\rangle=0$. So $\langle n|\dot n\rangle=i\dot\gamma_n$ with
   $\gamma_n$ real, and $c_n(t)=c_n(0)e^{i\gamma_n(t)}$ has modulus fixed
   at $|c_n(0)|$ for all time, exactly as a state that never leaves level
   $n$ requires.

   The hypothesis actually being used is worth naming, because it is easy
   to violate by accident. The argument needs $|n(t)\rangle$ to be
   normalized at *every* $t$, not just at $t=0$. Feed the same loop
   integral a family of eigenvectors whose norm drifts (what a numerical
   eigensolver hands back if you forget to renormalize each step) and
   $\mathrm{Re}\langle n|\dot n\rangle\neq0$, producing a spurious
   apparent decay of $c_n$ that has nothing to do with leakage between
   levels. The cure is to renormalize before differencing, not to discard
   the real part by hand. The discretized computation in this lesson is
   immune to it for free, since it reads the *argument* of each overlap
   and never looks at the modulus.

   </details>

3. As $\theta\to0$ (a vanishingly small cone, the field barely moving off
   the north pole), what does the solid-angle formula predict for
   $\gamma$? Does this match physical intuition about a "loop" that
   barely encloses any area?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   It predicts $\gamma\to0$, and quadratically, which is the part worth
   getting right:

   $$
   \Omega=2\pi(1-\cos\theta)=4\pi\sin^2(\theta/2)\approx\pi\theta^2,
   \qquad \gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega\approx-\tfrac{\pi\theta^2}{2}.
   $$

   Intuition agrees, and the quadratic behaviour is what makes the
   agreement meaningful. A small cap on a sphere is well approximated by a
   flat disk of radius $\theta$, whose area is $\pi\theta^2$: the phase
   tracks enclosed *area*, not the loop's circumference $2\pi\sin\theta$,
   which vanishes only linearly. Halving the cone angle therefore quarters
   the phase. The state-space picture says the same thing: the
   instantaneous eigenstate is
   $|0\rangle+\tfrac\theta2 e^{i\phi}|1\rangle+O(\theta^2)$, so the only
   $\phi$-dependent component has amplitude $O(\theta)$ and the phase it
   can generate over a cycle is $O(\theta^2)$. The plot bears this out:
   at $\theta=10°$, $\gamma\approx-0.0477$, and $\pi\theta^2/2$ with
   $\theta=10°=0.1745$ rad gives $0.0478$.

   The far more interesting limit is the other degenerate one. As
   $\theta\to\pi$ the loop again shrinks to a point, this time around the
   south pole, so the phase must again be unobservable. The formula gives
   $\Omega\to4\pi$ and $\gamma\to-2\pi$, which is not zero but is zero
   *modulo* $2\pi$, and a phase is only ever defined modulo $2\pi$. The
   two shrinking-loop limits are consistent, but only because the value at
   $\theta=\pi$ came out an exact multiple of $2\pi$. It had to: the same
   physical loop can be regarded as enclosing either the cap of solid
   angle $\Omega$ or its complement $4\pi-\Omega$, with opposite
   orientation, and the two answers differ by $2\pi$. That ambiguity being
   invisible is Dirac's monopole quantization condition wearing different
   clothes, and it is the reason the coefficient in $\gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega$
   cannot be adjusted freely.

   One thing not to conclude: that a small cone makes the geometric phase
   negligible in practice. It is small per loop, but it is coherent and
   path-dependent rather than random, so $N$ circuits of the same small
   cone accumulate $N\gamma$. And nothing else obstructs running many of
   them, since $|\dot{\hat n}|=\omega\sin\theta\to0$ makes the
   adiabaticity condition *easier* to satisfy for a narrow cone, not
   harder.

   </details>

4. The adiabaticity condition here was $\omega\ll\omega_0$. Using the
   leakage-rate formula $\langle m|\dot n\rangle=\langle m|\dot
   H|n\rangle/(E_n-E_m)$, explain qualitatively why a *larger* field
   strength (larger $\omega_0$) makes the adiabatic approximation *easier*
   to satisfy at fixed sweep rate $\omega$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the gap enters the *final* suppression twice while the driving
   term only grows once, and the second factor of the gap comes from the
   dynamical phase, not from the leakage rate.

   Work the two ingredients out for this Hamiltonian. With
   $H=-\tfrac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\hat n(t)\cdot\vec\sigma$,

   $$
   \dot H=-\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}\,\dot{\hat n}(t)\cdot\vec\sigma,\qquad |\dot{\hat n}|=\omega\sin\theta,
   $$

   since the tip of $\hat n$ runs around a circle of radius $\sin\theta$
   at angular rate $\omega$. Because $\hat n$ is a unit vector,
   $\dot{\hat n}\perp\hat n$, so $\dot{\hat n}\cdot\vec\sigma$ is purely
   off-diagonal in the instantaneous eigenbasis of $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$,
   with off-diagonal magnitude $|\dot{\hat n}|$. The gap is
   $|E_n-E_m|=\hbar\omega_0$. Hence

   $$
   |\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|=\frac{\hbar\omega_0\,\omega\sin\theta}{2},
   \qquad
   |\langle m|\dot n\rangle|=\frac{|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|}{|E_n-E_m|}=\frac{\omega\sin\theta}{2}.
   $$

   Read that second result carefully, because it names the tempting wrong
   turn. The leakage *rate* $\langle m|\dot n\rangle$ does not depend on
   $\omega_0$ at all. Raising the field strength enlarges the denominator
   $E_n-E_m$, but it enlarges $\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle$ by precisely the
   same factor, since a stronger field means the Hamiltonian itself is
   bigger and so its time derivative is too. "Bigger gap, smaller
   coupling" is not the mechanism here, and an argument that stops at
   $\langle m|\dot n\rangle$ predicts, wrongly, that field strength is
   irrelevant.

   The suppression lives one step further on. In the exact equation
   $\dot c_m=-c_m\langle m|\dot m\rangle-\sum_{n\neq m}c_n\langle m|\dot
   n\rangle e^{i(\theta_n-\theta_m)}$, what matters is not the
   instantaneous rate but the *accumulated* transfer
   $\int\langle m|\dot n\rangle\,e^{i(\theta_n-\theta_m)}\,dt$. The
   exponent oscillates at frequency $(E_n-E_m)/\hbar=\omega_0$, far faster
   than $\langle m|\dot n\rangle$ varies, so integrating by parts gives
   boundary terms of size

   $$
   \left|\frac{\hbar\,\langle m|\dot n\rangle}{E_n-E_m}\right|
   =\frac{\hbar\,|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|}{|E_n-E_m|^2}
   =\frac{\omega\sin\theta}{2\omega_0},
   $$

   and the interior term is smaller still. That is where the *square* of
   the gap in the adiabaticity condition
   $\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|\ll|E_n-E_m|^2$ comes from: one power
   converts $\dot H$ into a rate of change of eigenvectors, and the second
   is the price the rapidly rotating relative dynamical phase charges for
   letting any of that leakage actually build up.

   With the numbers in front of you the stated condition is now exact
   rather than qualitative: transfer amplitude $\sim\omega\sin\theta/2\omega_0$,
   so $\omega\ll\omega_0$ suffices for every $\theta$, and a narrow or
   nearly-polar cone ($\sin\theta$ small) is more forgiving still. Doubling
   $\omega_0$ at fixed $\omega$ halves the leakage amplitude and quarters
   the leaked probability, which is why strong-field, large-gap systems
   are the ones where geometric-phase gates are practical.

   </details>

## Summary

- The adiabatic theorem follows directly from expanding
  $|\psi(t)\rangle=\sum_nc_n(t)e^{i\theta_n(t)}|n(t)\rangle$ in the
  Schrödinger equation: inter-level coupling
  $\langle m|\dot n\rangle=\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle/(E_n-E_m)$ is
  suppressed whenever $\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|\ll|E_n-E_m|^2$.
- The same derivation's $n=m$ term is a purely imaginary
  $\langle n|\dot n\rangle$, giving a real, path-dependent (not
  time-dependent) phase $\gamma_n$: the geometric, or Berry, phase.
- For spin-$\tfrac12$ dragged around a cone by a slowly rotating field,
  $\gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega$ ($\Omega$ the solid angle traced), verified
  here as a real discretized loop integral over real Bloch-sphere
  states, matching the closed-form prediction to within
  $4.3\times10^{-7}$ across every angle swept.
