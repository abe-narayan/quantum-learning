## Three Lessons That Turn Out to Be One

Three lessons, seemingly about different things: The WKB Approximation
turned a rapidly-oscillating quantum phase into a classical trajectory.
The Adiabatic Theorem and Berry Phase showed a slowly-driven quantum state
tracking its instantaneous eigenstate, picking up a geometric phase along
the way. Neither, on its own, looks like it's about *symmetry* in the way
Clebsch-Gordan and Wigner-Eckart obviously are. But both are instances of
exactly the same underlying phenomenon: the divergent part, in either
case, is physically nothing more than oscillations piling up faster and
faster. This capstone makes that convergence explicit.

## Two Kinds of Phase, Two Kinds of Limit

### Which phase grows without bound, and which does not

WKB's quantization condition, $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}p(x)\,dx=(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar$,
says the classical action integral (in units of $\hbar$) is quantized. As
$\hbar\to0$ at fixed classical action, $n\to\infty$: the *same* classical
trajectory corresponds to an enormous, and enormously precise, quantum
number. The WKB phase $\frac1\hbar\int p\,dx$ diverges in this limit,
accumulating an unbounded number of oscillations, the sense in
which "the wave nature stops mattering for gross features" and a sharp
classical trajectory emerges.

The adiabatic theorem's dynamical phase, $\theta_n(t)=-\frac1\hbar\int_0^t
E_n(t')\,dt'$, behaves identically: the *slower* a loop is traversed
(deeper into the regime where the adiabatic theorem itself is valid), the
larger $\theta_n$ grows, unboundedly. But the Berry (geometric) phase
$\gamma_n$ derived alongside it does **not** grow with the loop's
duration at all; it depends only on the path's shape in parameter space.
This is the same split WKB exhibits: an unbounded, "counts how much
classical time/action has passed" piece, and a bounded, purely geometric
residue.

### The classical shadow: adiabatic invariance and the Hannay angle

WKB's quantization condition is not a purely quantum invention: it is the
direct descendant of a **classical** fact, that the action
variable $I=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dx$ is an **adiabatic invariant**,
staying constant under a slowly-varying classical Hamiltonian (a
classical theorem, cited here from Hamiltonian mechanics and not from
quantum theory). Old quantum theory's Bohr-Sommerfeld
rule simply quantized this classical invariant in units of $\hbar$; WKB
derives the *same* condition properly, as the leading semiclassical
approximation to the true Schrödinger equation, matching what
The WKB Approximation built.

Berry phase has an equally direct classical shadow: **Hannay's angle**,
a purely classical geometric phase shift picked up by the *angle*
variable conjugate to $I$, when the same slow, closed loop in parameter
space is traversed classically instead of quantum mechanically (cited
here as the classical analogue, well beyond this course's scope to
derive, but the existence of a classical counterpart is itself the
point: Berry phase is not an artifact of quantization but a geometric
fact about slowly-varied systems that quantum mechanics inherits rather
than invents).

## Where the Classical Shadow Falls

Three lessons, one convergent picture: **WKB** shows a classical
trajectory crystallizing out of a rapidly-oscillating quantum phase as
$\hbar\to0$. **The adiabatic theorem** shows a quantum state tracking a
classical-like path through parameter space as changes slow down. **Berry
phase** shows that even in that classical-tracking limit, a stubbornly
geometric, non-classical-looking quantity survives, and turns out to have
its own classical shadow anyway (Hannay's angle), closing the loop
between "quantum phase" and "classical geometry" completely. Symmetry
enters at the foundation of all three: WKB's quantization condition
counts states via a symmetry of the classical phase space (Liouville's
theorem, area preservation under Hamiltonian flow, cited); Berry's phase
is literally defined by parallel transport around a loop, a statement
about the *geometry* (symmetry of parallel transport) of the space of
quantum states themselves.

## Racing a Geometric Phase Against a Dynamical One

**At what loop duration $T$ does the dynamical phase from the plot above
first reach a full $2\pi$, and how does this compare to the fixed
geometric phase at that same $T$?**

$\theta_{\rm dyn}(T)=\omega_0T/2=2\pi\Rightarrow T=4\pi/\omega_0\approx12.566$
(natural units, $\omega_0=1$). At that duration, the geometric phase is
still exactly $\pi/2$, unchanged from its value at $T=1$. So by then the
dynamical phase has run a full turn around the circle while the geometric
phase has managed a quarter turn: a ratio of **four**, and still climbing,
since only one of the two depends on $T$ at all. At $T=40$, the right
edge of the plot, the ratio is $20/(\pi/2)\approx12.7$.

## Treating the Geometric Residue as Bookkeeping

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking Berry phase is a purely quantum phenomenon with no classical
meaning.** Hannay's angle is its direct classical shadow, in the action-
angle formalism; the quantum and classical pictures aren't in tension,
they're two levels of the same geometric statement about slowly-varied
systems.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Treating WKB's semiclassical limit and the adiabatic theorem's slow-driving
limit as unrelated approximation schemes that happen to share the word
"classical."** Both are the same kind of limit: a phase built from
integrating something (momentum in space, for WKB; energy in time, for the
adiabatic theorem) diverges, while a geometric residue built from a closed
loop or quantized action survives, exactly the pattern this capstone's
plot makes explicit.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the same $\omega_0=1$ convention, find the loop duration $T$ at
   which the dynamical phase equals $10\pi$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $T=20\pi\approx62.832$.

   $$
   \theta_{\rm dyn}(T)=\frac{\omega_0T}{2}=10\pi \;\Longrightarrow\; T=\frac{20\pi}{\omega_0}=20\pi\approx62.832.
   $$

   Two observations make this more than arithmetic. First, that duration
   sits well off the right edge of the plot, which stops at $T=40$.
   Extrapolating is legitimate here only because $\theta_{\rm dyn}$ is
   exactly linear in $T$ with no saturation anywhere, which is precisely
   the "unbounded" claim the capstone is making: there is no $T$ at which
   the dynamical phase stops growing.

   Second, compare the two phases at that duration. The geometric phase is
   still $\pi/2$, its value at every $T$, so the ratio is
   $10\pi/(\pi/2)=20$. At $T=4\pi$ the same ratio was $4$; at $T=40$ it was
   about $12.7$. The ratio grows linearly and without limit, which is the
   quantitative form of "one of these depends on the clock and the other
   does not."

   Worth being precise about what $\theta_{\rm dyn}=10\pi$ means
   physically: $e^{i10\pi}=1$, so the dynamical phase has come back to
   where it started, having wound five full times. A single measurement at
   that instant cannot tell $10\pi$ from $0$. This is why the
   dynamical phase carries no geometric information and why real
   geometric-phase experiments go to the trouble of cancelling it rather
   than measuring it: its value is enormous, it depends on details of
   timing no experiment controls to that precision, and modulo $2\pi$ it is
   effectively a random number.

   </details>

2. Explain in your own words why the Berry phase curve in the plot above
   is exactly flat, using the path-dependence (not time-dependence)
   argument from The Adiabatic Theorem and Berry Phase.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the integral that defines $\gamma$ has no $dt$ left in it once
   the chain rule has been applied, so the loop's duration has nothing to
   attach to.

   Follow the cancellation explicitly. The adiabatic derivation produced

   $$
   \gamma_n=i\int_0^T\langle n(t)|\dot n(t)\rangle\,dt
   =i\int_0^T\langle n|\nabla_R n\rangle\cdot\frac{d\vec R}{dt}\,dt
   =i\oint_{\mathcal C}\langle n|\nabla_R n\rangle\cdot d\vec R,
   $$

   where $\mathcal C$ is the closed curve the control parameters trace. The
   $dt$ in the measure is consumed by the $d\vec R/dt$ from the chain rule.
   What remains is a line integral over a curve, and a curve is a set of
   points in parameter space, not a schedule for visiting them. Stretch $T$
   by a factor of ten, run the loop unevenly, pause halfway around: the
   curve $\mathcal C$ is unchanged, so $\gamma$ is unchanged.

   Contrast the dynamical phase, $\theta_n=-\frac1\hbar\int_0^TE_n\,dt$.
   Nothing in that integrand is a rate of change of anything, so the $dt$
   survives, and the integral simply accumulates for as long as the loop
   takes. That is the whole difference between the two curves in the plot.

   Two qualifications keep this from being over-claimed. The invariance is
   under *orientation-preserving* reparametrization only: traverse the same
   loop backwards and $\gamma$ changes sign, since $d\vec R$ reverses. And
   the flat line in the plot is the strict adiabatic value. At finite $T$
   the true phase carries non-adiabatic corrections of order $1/T$ (from
   the inter-level terms the derivation dropped), so the line is exact only
   in the limit the horizontal axis is walking toward and becomes a better
   approximation as $T$ grows. The plot's honest reading is therefore not
   "geometric phase is constant" but "geometric phase converges to a
   constant while dynamical phase diverges", which is a stronger statement
   about their difference, not a weaker one.

   </details>

3. WKB's quantization condition and the classical adiabatic invariance of
   $I=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dx$ are described here as "the same statement,"
   one classical and one quantized. What specifically gets added going
   from the classical statement to the WKB one?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Three things get added, and the third is the one that makes the pairing
   more than an analogy.

   **A discrete set of allowed values.** Classically, $I$ is a real number
   and every value is available; a pendulum can swing at any amplitude.
   Adiabatic invariance says only that whatever $I$ you have, slow changes
   preserve it. WKB says the allowed $I$ are $\hbar(n+\tfrac12)$ for
   non-negative integers $n$, so the continuum of classical orbits is
   replaced by a discrete ladder. Equivalently, each quantum state occupies
   a phase-space area of exactly $2\pi\hbar$, which is where the
   semiclassical density of states comes from.

   **The half-integer offset, which is not classical at all.** The $\tfrac12$
   in $\int p\,dx=(n+\tfrac12)\pi\hbar$ has no counterpart in $I$. It is
   the Maslov index: the WKB connection formulas cost the wave a phase of
   $\pi/2$ at each of the two classical turning points, where the naive
   semiclassical form breaks down and has to be matched through an Airy
   function. Old quantum theory's Bohr-Sommerfeld rule quantized the
   classical invariant without it and got the harmonic oscillator's zero
   point wrong; WKB derives it, which is the concrete sense in which WKB is
   a derivation rather than a postulate.

   **Control of the error.** Classical adiabatic invariance is a theorem
   about exact dynamics under slow driving. WKB is an asymptotic
   approximation to the Schrödinger equation, exact only as $\hbar\to0$,
   with corrections that are small when the de Broglie wavelength varies
   slowly compared to itself and that fail outright near turning points.
   They are statements of different logical type about the same integral.

   The reason the pairing is not a coincidence is Ehrenfest's, and it runs
   the other direction. A quantity can be consistently quantized under slow
   driving only if it is an adiabatic invariant. If $I$ drifted under slow
   variation, then $n=I/\hbar-\tfrac12$ would have to drift too, and $n$ is
   an integer that cannot change continuously. So classical adiabatic
   invariance of $I$ is exactly what makes the *quantum* adiabatic theorem
   consistent in the semiclassical regime: the quantum number is stuck
   because the classical action it quantizes is stuck. That is the link
   this capstone is pointing at, with the two lessons standing on opposite
   sides of it.

   </details>

4. Name, in one sentence each, the "unbounded" phase and the "fixed,
   geometric" phase in all three of: WKB, the adiabatic theorem applied
   to a general slow loop, and the spin-$\tfrac12$-in-a-rotating-field
   example specifically.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **WKB.** The unbounded piece is the action phase
   $\frac1\hbar\int p(x)\,dx$, which grows without limit as $\hbar\to0$ at
   fixed classical orbit, piling up oscillations no experiment resolves.
   The fixed geometric piece is the quantized enclosed phase-space area
   $\oint p\,dx=2\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12)$: a property of the closed orbit as a
   curve, unchanged by where you start on it or how fast you go around.

   **The adiabatic theorem, general slow loop.** The unbounded piece is
   the dynamical phase $\theta_n=-\frac1\hbar\int_0^TE_n(t)\,dt$, growing
   linearly with the loop's duration. The fixed geometric piece is the
   Berry phase $\gamma_n=i\oint_{\mathcal C}\langle n|\nabla_R n\rangle\cdot d\vec R$,
   a functional of the closed curve $\mathcal C$ in parameter space and of
   nothing else.

   **Spin-$\tfrac12$ in a rotating field.** The unbounded piece is
   $\theta=\omega_0T/2$, half the Larmor frequency times however long the
   loop took, which is the brand curve in the plot above. The fixed
   geometric piece is $\gamma=-\tfrac12\Omega=-\pi(1-\cos\theta)$, minus
   half the solid angle the field direction traces out, which is $-\pi/2$
   for the $\theta=60°$ cone used throughout.

   One clarification the phrasing invites. "Fixed" always means fixed with
   respect to the limit being taken, not constant in general. The Berry
   phase moves the moment you change the loop's shape, as the whole
   $\gamma$-versus-$\theta$ sweep in the previous lesson shows, and the WKB
   action changes with the orbit. What none of them does is depend on the
   thing that is being sent to a limit, which is $\hbar$ in the first case
   and $T$ in the other two. That is the shared structure the capstone is
   naming: in each pair, one quantity counts how much of the limit
   parameter has been spent and the other records the geometry that
   survives spending it.

   </details>

## Summary

- WKB's action phase and the adiabatic theorem's dynamical phase both
  diverge in their respective classical/slow limits; a geometric residue
  (quantized action for WKB, Berry phase for the adiabatic theorem)
  stays exactly fixed, the same split appearing in both settings.
- WKB's Bohr-Sommerfeld condition is the quantization of a
  classical adiabatic invariant, $I=\frac1{2\pi}\oint p\,dx$; Berry's
  phase has an equally real classical shadow, Hannay's angle.
- Computed directly for this course's own spin-$\tfrac12$ example: the
  dynamical phase grows without bound as the loop slows down, while the
  Berry phase sits at a fixed $\pi/2$ throughout, a concrete instance of
  the general pattern this capstone unifies.
