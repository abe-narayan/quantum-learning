## The Gap the Introduction Admitted

Fine Structure (Introduction) was honest about a gap: it named spin-orbit
coupling as one of two effects splitting hydrogen's $l$-degeneracy, stated
its rough size ($\sim\alpha^2\times13.6\text{ eV}$), and then explicitly
declined to compute it, citing "<Term id="degenerate-perturbation-theory">degenerate perturbation theory</Term>... beyond
this course's scope." Ordinary (non-degenerate) perturbation theory,
Time-Independent Perturbation Theory's $E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq
n}|H'_{mn}|^2/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})$, breaks down the moment $E_n^{(0)}$ is
degenerate: the denominator vanishes for any other degenerate state $m$,
and the formula divides by zero. Hydrogen's $n=2$ level is $4$-fold
degenerate (2s and 2p, before spin; $8$-fold with spin) in the
non-relativistic theory, so this is the generic situation, not an edge
case. This lesson derives the fix, then uses it to finish
the calculation the earlier lesson set aside.

## Diagonalizing Within the Degenerate Subspace

### Why non-degenerate perturbation theory fails

Suppose $H_0$ has a degenerate eigenspace: $H_0|\alpha\rangle=E^{(0)}|\alpha\rangle$
for every $|\alpha\rangle$ in some subspace of dimension $g>1$. Any linear
combination $\sum_\alpha c_\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ is *equally* a valid
zeroth-order eigenstate of $H_0$ at energy $E^{(0)}$; nothing in $H_0$
alone picks out a preferred combination. But turning on $H'=\lambda H'$
generically does pick one out (it lifts the degeneracy), so the "correct"
zeroth-order state, the one that continuously connects to the true
perturbed eigenstate as $\lambda\to0$, has to be *derived*, not
assumed to be any particular $|\alpha\rangle$ from the original basis.

### The fix: diagonalize H′ within the degenerate subspace

Write $|\psi\rangle=|\psi^{(0)}\rangle+\lambda|\psi^{(1)}\rangle+\cdots$,
$H=H_0+\lambda H'$, with $|\psi^{(0)}\rangle=\sum_\alpha
c_\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ (undetermined $c_\alpha$, restricted to the
degenerate subspace). Substituting into $H|\psi\rangle=E|\psi\rangle$ and
collecting the first-order-in-$\lambda$ terms:

$$
H_0|\psi^{(1)}\rangle + H'|\psi^{(0)}\rangle = E^{(0)}|\psi^{(1)}\rangle + E^{(1)}|\psi^{(0)}\rangle
$$

Project onto $\langle\beta|$, another state of the *same* degenerate
subspace. Since $H_0$ is Hermitian, $\langle\beta|H_0=E^{(0)}\langle\beta|$,
so $\langle\beta|H_0|\psi^{(1)}\rangle=E^{(0)}\langle\beta|\psi^{(1)}\rangle$
cancels exactly against the matching term on the right, leaving:

$$
\boxed{\;\sum_\alpha \langle\beta|H'|\alpha\rangle\, c_\alpha = E^{(1)} c_\beta\;}
$$

This is an ordinary matrix eigenvalue equation: $H'_{\text{sub}}\vec
c=E^{(1)}\vec c$, where $H'_{\text{sub}}$ is $H'$'s $g\times g$ matrix
restricted to the degenerate subspace (Hermitian, since $H'$ is). Its
eigenvalues are the correct first-order energy corrections, splitting the
degeneracy; its eigenvectors are the correct zeroth-order states, the
specific combinations of $|\alpha\rangle$'s that $H'$ doesn't mix with
each other at this order. This is a derivation, not a stated recipe: it
uses nothing beyond first-order perturbation expansion and $H_0$'s
Hermiticity, both already established in Time-Independent Perturbation
Theory and Hermitian Operators.

One hypothesis is worth stating before it gets used, because this
lesson's own example violates it. The claim "the eigenvectors of
$H'_{\text{sub}}$ *are* the correct zeroth-order states" needs
$H'_{\text{sub}}$ to have **non-degenerate** eigenvalues. When it does,
first order lifts the degeneracy completely, each eigenvector is pinned,
and the ordinary non-degenerate machinery takes over from second order
on. When $H'_{\text{sub}}$ has a repeated eigenvalue, first order has
only *partially* resolved the subspace: inside each surviving eigenvalue
block the correct combination is still undetermined, and settling it
requires going to second order, or finding a further operator that
commutes with both $H_0$ and $H'$ and using its eigenvalues as extra
labels.

Hydrogen's $2p$ level is the second case, not the first.
$\vec L\cdot\vec S$ below has eigenvalue $+\tfrac12$ four times and $-1$
twice, so first order leaves a $4$-fold and a $2$-fold degeneracy
standing. Those are not an accident to be resolved at higher order: they
are the $2j+1$ values of $m_j$, and rotational invariance of
$H_0+H'_{SO}$ protects them exactly. It is the second route that applies
here, with $J_z$ supplying the extra label $m_j$. Splitting *those*
degeneracies requires breaking the rotational symmetry with an external
field, which is what the Zeeman effect does.

### Applying it: hydrogen's 2p spin-orbit coupling

The spin-orbit interaction has the form $H'_{SO}=\xi(r)\,\vec L\cdot\vec
S$, a radial function times the dot product of orbital and spin angular
momentum (the standard, Thomas-precession-corrected coefficient
$\xi(r)=\frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\frac1r\frac{dV}{dr}$ is cited here rather than
re-derived: getting the factor of $\tfrac12$ right requires the
relativistic kinematics of the electron's rotating rest frame, well
outside this lesson's scope, an honest citation matching Fine Structure
(Introduction)'s own convention). For fixed $n,l$, $\xi(r)$ is the *same*
number for every state in that $(n,l)$ subspace (it depends only on $r$,
not on $m_L,m_S$), so it factors out of the degenerate-subspace
diagonalization entirely: **only the dimensionless angular operator $\vec
L\cdot\vec S$ needs diagonalizing.**

Take hydrogen's $2p$ subspace: $l=1$, $6$ states once spin is included
($m_L\in\{1,0,-1\}$, $m_S\in\{+\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\}$). Building $\vec
L\cdot\vec S=L_xS_x+L_yS_y+L_zS_z$ directly from this platform's own
$j=1$ and $j=\tfrac12$ operators (`angularMomentumX/Y/Z`, tensored
together the same way Addition of Angular Momentum tensored two spin-$\tfrac12$
operators, just with unequal $j$'s now):

diagonal wherever $\vec J^{\,2}$ is.

### The actual energy splitting

$\langle H'_{SO}\rangle_{nlj}=\langle\xi(r)\rangle_{nl}\times\tfrac12[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)]$,
with $\langle\xi(r)\rangle_{nl}=\frac{\alpha^2}{2n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1)}$ in
atomic units ($\alpha\approx1/137$; this uses the standard closed-form
radial integral $\langle1/r^3\rangle_{nl}=1/(n^3l(l+\frac12)(l+1))$,
cited, but checked numerically just below rather than trusted blindly).
Converting to eV (using $13.6\text{ eV}=\alpha^2mc^2/2$) gives:

$$
\boxed{\;E_{SO}^{(1)}(n,l,j) = (13.6\text{ eV})\,\frac{\alpha^2\left[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34\right]}{2\,n^3\,l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)}\;}
$$

**Checking $\langle1/r^3\rangle_{2p}$ numerically**, by trapezoidal
integration of $\int R_{21}(r)^2\,(1/r)\,dr$ using this platform's own
`radial2p` from the Hydrogen Atom course:

$$
\langle 1/r^3\rangle_{2p}^{\text{numeric}} \approx 0.041667, \qquad
\frac1{n^3l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)}\Big|_{n=2,l=1} = \frac1{24} \approx 0.041667
$$

## How a Sixfold Degeneracy Actually Splits

The 2p level's $6$-fold spin-orbital degeneracy is actually lifted, not
just relabeled: $2p_{3/2}$ (four states, $m_j=\pm\tfrac32,\pm\tfrac12$)
sits *above* the unperturbed energy, $2p_{1/2}$ (two states,
$m_j=\pm\tfrac12$) sits *below* it, exactly as required for the
weighted-average splitting to vanish ($4\times(+\tfrac12)+2\times(-1)=0$,
the trace of $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ being zero, a basis-independent fact
already visible in the uncoupled panel above). Physically, $\vec
L\cdot\vec S>0$ means the electron's spin and orbital magnetic moments
are aligned (higher energy, like two aligned magnets require energy to
hold in place against their mutual repulsion in this configuration);
$\vec L\cdot\vec S<0$ means anti-aligned (lower energy). $2s$ ($l=0$) is
untouched by this particular splitting, since $\vec L=0$ there makes
$\vec L\cdot\vec S\equiv0$ identically, regardless of any diagonalization.

## Hydrogen's 2p Spin-Orbit Splitting

**Confirm the $j=\tfrac32$ eigenvalue of $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ directly from
the coupled-basis formula, without reading it off the matrix.**

$\tfrac12[j(j+1)-l(l+1)-s(s+1)]$ with $j=\tfrac32,l=1,s=\tfrac12$:
$j(j+1)=\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac52=\tfrac{15}4=3.75$; $l(l+1)=2$;
$s(s+1)=\tfrac34$. So $\tfrac12[3.75-2-0.75]=\tfrac12(1)=0.5$, matching
every diagonal entry in the $j=\tfrac32$ block of the coupled-basis panel
above exactly ($U^\dagger(\vec L\cdot\vec S)U$'s first four diagonal
entries), computed here from the formula alone, independently of the
matrix diagonalization.

## Applying the Non-Degenerate Formula Anyway

<Callout type="mistake">
**Applying the non-degenerate formula $E_n^{(2)}=\sum_{m\neq
n}|H'_{mn}|^2/(E_n^{(0)}-E_m^{(0)})$ directly to a degenerate level.** The
denominator is exactly zero for any other state in the same degenerate
subspace; the fix is the different first step derived above (diagonalize
$H'$ within the subspace *first*), not a small patch on top of the old
formula.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the uncoupled $|m_L,m_S\rangle$ basis is "good enough" since
it's a valid basis for the degenerate subspace.** It's a valid basis, but
not the *correct zeroth-order* one: the uncoupled-basis panel above shows
$\vec L\cdot\vec S$ mixing these states, meaning a state that
starts as pure $|m_L,m_S\rangle$ does not stay an energy eigenstate once
$H'_{SO}$ turns on.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using the boxed splitting formula, compute $E_{SO}(2p_{3/2})-E_{SO}(2p_{1/2})$
   for $n=3,l=1$ (the $3p$ level), and compare its order of magnitude to
   the $2p$ splitting computed above.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   For $l=1$ the denominator's angular factor is
   $l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)=1\cdot\tfrac32\cdot2=3$, and the brackets are
   $j(j+1)-l(l+1)-\tfrac34$, giving $+1$ at $j=\tfrac32$ and $-2$ at
   $j=\tfrac12$. So at $n=3$,

   $$
   E_{SO}(3p_{3/2})=\frac{(13.6)\alpha^2(+1)}{2\cdot27\cdot3}=4.4728\times10^{-6}\text{ eV},
   \qquad
   E_{SO}(3p_{1/2})=\frac{(13.6)\alpha^2(-2)}{2\cdot27\cdot3}=-8.9457\times10^{-6}\text{ eV},
   $$

   and the splitting is

   $$
   \Delta E_{3p}=1.34185\times10^{-5}\text{ eV},
   $$

   using `RYDBERG_EV` $=13.6$ and $\alpha=1/137$, the two constants this
   lesson's `spinOrbitEnergyEV` actually uses.

   Same order of magnitude as the $2p$ splitting, $10^{-5}$ eV, and
   smaller by a clean factor. The bracket difference is
   $1-(-2)=3$, which cancels the $3$ in the denominator exactly, so for
   any $p$ level the splitting collapses to

   $$
   \Delta E_{np}=\frac{(13.6\text{ eV})\,\alpha^2}{2n^3},
   $$

   depending on nothing but $n$. Hence
   $\Delta E_{3p}/\Delta E_{2p}=(2/3)^3=8/27=0.2963$, and indeed
   $4.52874\times10^{-5}\times8/27=1.34185\times10^{-5}$. The engine
   returns the ratio as $0.2962962962962963$, which is $8/27$ to machine
   precision.

   The $1/n^3$ is worth reading physically rather than just recording. It
   comes from $\langle1/r^3\rangle_{nl}=1/\big(n^3l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)\big)$:
   spin-orbit coupling is a short-range effect, weighted by how much of
   the wavefunction sits close to the nucleus, and higher-$n$ orbitals
   keep the electron further out. Fine structure fades quickly up the
   Rydberg ladder.

   The wrong turn is expecting the $n^{-3}$ scaling to be $n^{-2}$ by
   analogy with the gross Bohr energies $E_n=-13.6/n^2$. The two scalings
   are different because they weight different radial quantities: the
   Bohr energy tracks $\langle1/r\rangle\propto n^{-2}$ while the
   spin-orbit shift tracks $\langle1/r^3\rangle\propto n^{-3}$ at fixed
   $l$. Assuming one scaling governs everything hydrogenic gives $3p$ a
   splitting of $2.0\times10^{-5}$ eV, high by half again.

   </details>

2. The trace of $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ (sum of all six diagonal entries in
   either basis) is exactly zero. Using the four $j=\tfrac32$ eigenvalues
   ($+\tfrac12$ each) and two $j=\tfrac12$ eigenvalues ($-1$ each), verify
   this by direct arithmetic, and explain in words why a basis change
   (uncoupled to coupled) can never change a matrix's trace.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   4\times\left(+\tfrac12\right)+2\times(-1)=2-2=0 .
   $$

   The uncoupled basis reaches the same zero by a completely different
   route, which is the part worth seeing. In the $|m_L,m_S\rangle$ basis
   $L_x$ and $L_y$ have no diagonal entries at all (they raise or lower
   $m_L$), and the same is true of $S_x,S_y$, so the only surviving
   diagonal contribution is from $L_zS_z$, and the $k$-th diagonal entry
   is simply $m_Lm_S$. Running over $m_L\in\{1,0,-1\}$ and
   $m_S\in\{\pm\tfrac12\}$ gives
   $+\tfrac12,-\tfrac12,0,0,-\tfrac12,+\tfrac12$, which is exactly the
   diagonal a rebuild of the matrix prints, and it sums to zero because
   $\sum m_L=0$.

   The structural reason is cleaner than either arithmetic. The trace of
   a tensor product factorizes, $\mathrm{Tr}(A\otimes B)=\mathrm{Tr}A\cdot
   \mathrm{Tr}B$, and every angular-momentum component is traceless, since
   its eigenvalues $m$ run symmetrically from $-j$ to $j$. So

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}(\vec L\cdot\vec S)=\sum_{i}\mathrm{Tr}(L_i)\,\mathrm{Tr}(S_i)=0
   $$

   for any $l$ and any $s$, not just this case. The vanishing is forced,
   not arithmetic luck.

   Why a basis change cannot touch it: the trace is invariant under
   similarity, $\mathrm{Tr}(S^{-1}AS)=\mathrm{Tr}(AS S^{-1})=\mathrm{Tr}(A)$,
   by cyclicity of the trace. Note that this needs only *invertibility* of
   $S$, not unitarity; $U^\dagger(\vec L\cdot\vec S)U$ qualifies because
   $U^\dagger=U^{-1}$. Equivalently, the trace is the sum of the
   eigenvalues, and eigenvalues are properties of the operator rather than
   of the coordinates chosen to write it down.

   Physically this is the statement that spin-orbit coupling has no
   center of gravity to move. It redistributes the six states about the
   unperturbed $2p$ energy without shifting their weighted mean, which is
   why $2p_{3/2}$ rises by $1.5\times10^{-5}$ eV while $2p_{1/2}$ falls by
   twice that: four states up one unit, two states down two units.

   The wrong turn is adding the two *distinct* eigenvalues,
   $\tfrac12+(-1)=-\tfrac12$, and concluding the trace is nonzero. The
   trace runs over all six basis vectors, so each eigenvalue enters with
   its multiplicity; "two eigenvalues" is not "two diagonal entries."
   That slip is the same one that produces a nonzero center of gravity and
   therefore predicts a spurious overall shift of the $2p$ level, which
   spectroscopy would have caught immediately.

   </details>
3. Why does $2s$ ($l=0$) hydrogen have no spin-orbit splitting at all,
   directly from $\vec L\cdot\vec S$'s definition (not from the boxed
   formula's $l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)$ denominator, which is undefined at
   $l=0$ for a different reason)?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because at $l=0$ the operator $\vec L$ is not small, it is *zero*. The
   $l=0$ representation of the rotation group is one-dimensional, so
   $L_x$, $L_y$ and $L_z$ are all the zero operator on that subspace, and
   therefore

   $$
   \vec L\cdot\vec S=L_xS_x+L_yS_y+L_zS_z=0
   $$

   identically, as an operator on the two-dimensional $|m_S=\pm\tfrac12\rangle$
   space. Every matrix element vanishes, not merely the diagonal ones, so
   $H'_{SO}=\xi(r)\,\vec L\cdot\vec S$ is the zero perturbation and the
   $2s$ doublet stays exactly degenerate at every order in *this*
   interaction. No diagonalization is required or even meaningful: the
   matrix to diagonalize is the $2\times2$ zero matrix.

   The question's parenthetical is pointing at a real trap, so it is worth
   spelling out why the formula cannot be used here. At $l=0$ the
   denominator $l(l+\tfrac12)(l+1)$ vanishes, and so does the numerator's
   bracket, since $l=0$ forces $j=\tfrac12$ and
   $\tfrac34-0-\tfrac34=0$. The boxed expression is genuinely $0/0$: an
   indeterminate form, from which no limit can be read off honestly.

   Underneath the indeterminacy is a real divergence, which is the part
   most treatments skip. $\langle1/r^3\rangle_{nl}=\int|R_{nl}|^2r^{-1}dr$
   converges for $l\ge1$ because $R_{nl}\sim r^l$ kills the integrand at
   the origin, but $R_{n0}(0)$ is finite and nonzero, so the $l=0$
   integrand behaves like $|R_{n0}(0)|^2/r$ and the integral diverges
   logarithmically. The honest description of the $l=0$ case is therefore
   $\infty\times0$, and only the fact that the angular factor is exactly
   the zero *operator*, independent of whatever $\xi(r)$ does, settles it.
   Taking a limit in $l$ would be reasoning about a product where one
   factor is unbounded.

   Worth adding for completeness: the $2s$ level does still receive a
   relativistic correction of the same order, but it comes from the Darwin
   term, which is nonzero precisely where the wavefunction is nonzero at
   the nucleus and so acts only on $l=0$. Spin-orbit and Darwin are
   complementary; "no spin-orbit splitting" is not "no fine structure."

   The wrong turn is answering that $\langle\vec L\cdot\vec S\rangle=0$
   "by symmetry," as an expectation value that happens to cancel. The
   claim here is much stronger and needs to be, because a vanishing
   expectation value in one state says nothing about the off-diagonal
   elements that degenerate perturbation theory lives on. A perturbation
   with zero diagonal and nonzero off-diagonal entries still splits a
   degenerate level, as the $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ block in the next answer
   shows: its $|0,+\tfrac12\rangle$ diagonal entry is $0$ and it splits
   the level anyway.

   </details>

4. Explain, using this lesson's derivation, why "diagonalize $H'$ within
   the degenerate subspace" is a strictly necessary first step and not
   just good practice: what specifically goes wrong in the boxed
   first-order formula, $\sum_\alpha\langle\beta|H'|\alpha\rangle
   c_\alpha=E^{(1)}c_\beta$, if a non-eigenvector combination is used
   instead?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   What goes wrong is that the equation has no solution at all. The boxed
   expression is not a formula you evaluate on a state of your choosing;
   it is $H'_{\text{sub}}\vec c=E^{(1)}\vec c$, an eigenvalue equation,
   and it is a *condition* the coefficients must satisfy. Feed it a
   $\vec c$ that is not an eigenvector and $H'_{\text{sub}}\vec c$ fails
   to be proportional to $\vec c$, so no scalar $E^{(1)}$ exists making
   both sides agree. Component $\beta$ demands
   $E^{(1)}=(H'_{\text{sub}}\vec c)_\beta/c_\beta$, and different $\beta$
   return different numbers. The system is inconsistent, not merely
   inaccurate.

   Watch it fail on this lesson's own operator. Take the uncoupled state
   $|m_L=1,m_S=-\tfrac12\rangle$, which looks like a perfectly respectable
   zeroth-order state. Naive first-order perturbation theory would offer
   its diagonal element, $m_Lm_S=-\tfrac12$. But $\vec L\cdot\vec S$
   connects it to $|m_L=0,m_S=+\tfrac12\rangle$ with matrix element
   $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=0.7071$, so within that two-dimensional block

   $$
   H'_{\text{sub}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\\[2pt] \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} & 0\end{pmatrix},
   \qquad \text{eigenvalues } -1 \text{ and } +\tfrac12 .
   $$

   A rebuilt matrix returns exactly $-1.000000$ and $+0.500000$. The naive
   answer $-\tfrac12$ is neither of them; it sits strictly between the two
   true shifts. This is not a small error to be improved at second order,
   it is a value the exact answer never takes.

   The deeper reason is a solvability condition, and it explains why the
   requirement is structural rather than stylistic. The first-order
   equation is
   $(H_0-E^{(0)})|\psi^{(1)}\rangle=(E^{(1)}-H')|\psi^{(0)}\rangle$, and
   $H_0-E^{(0)}$ annihilates the entire degenerate subspace. An equation
   of the form (singular operator) times unknown equals known is solvable
   only when the right-hand side is orthogonal to that kernel, and
   projecting onto $\langle\beta|$ is precisely the act of imposing that
   orthogonality. In the derivation above, this is why
   $\langle\beta|H_0|\psi^{(1)}\rangle$ cancels against
   $E^{(0)}\langle\beta|\psi^{(1)}\rangle$ and the unknown
   $|\psi^{(1)}\rangle$ drops out. Choose a non-eigenvector and the
   condition is violated, which shows up downstream as $|\psi^{(1)}\rangle$
   acquiring a component inside the degenerate subspace with a zero
   energy denominator: the same $0/0$ that broke the non-degenerate
   formula in the first place, reappearing one step later.

   The wrong turn is treating the diagonalization as a convenience that
   makes the algebra tidier, and reasoning that any basis of the subspace
   ought to give the same physics since the subspace itself is
   basis-independent. The subspace is; the split states are not. $H'$
   selects a preferred basis inside it, and which combinations survive as
   the correct zeroth-order states is exactly the physical content
   perturbation theory is being asked for. A second wrong turn is trusting
   diagonal elements because the off-diagonal ones "look small": $0.7071$
   here is larger than either diagonal entry in that block, and even a
   small off-diagonal element dominates when the diagonal splitting it
   competes against is zero, which is what degeneracy means.

   </details>

## Summary

- Degenerate perturbation theory's key extra step: the correct
  zeroth-order states are the eigenvectors of $H'$ restricted to
  (projected onto) the degenerate subspace, derived directly from the
  first-order Schrödinger equation, not assumed.
- Applied to hydrogen's $2p$ level, $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ is
  non-diagonal in the uncoupled $|m_L,m_S\rangle$ basis but exactly
  diagonal in the coupled $|j,m_j\rangle$ basis, checked directly via this
  platform's own angular-momentum operators, not asserted by symmetry.
- The resulting $2p_{3/2}$/$2p_{1/2}$ spin-orbit splitting is a
  computed number ($\approx4.5\times10^{-5}\text{ eV}$), the specific
  calculation Fine Structure (Introduction) named and explicitly withheld.
