export function sWavePhaseShift(ka) {

  const delta0 = sWavePhaseShift(ka);
  return ((4 * Math.PI) / (k * k)) * Math.sin(delta0) ** 2;
}

## From One Dimension to Three

Scattering Off a Step Potential and Resonant Transmission Through a
Barrier solved 1D scattering completely: match $\psi,\psi'$ at each
boundary, read off reflection and transmission. Real scattering
experiments happen in three dimensions, off a potential $V(r)$
depending only on distance from a center.

## Partial Waves, Phase Shifts, and the S-Matrix

### Separating the 3D problem into independent channels

For a central potential $V(r)$, $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=[H,L_z]=0$ (rotational
symmetry, the same fact Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics
used to separate the hydrogen atom), so energy eigenstates can be built
with definite $l$: $\psi(r,\theta)=\sum_lR_l(r)P_l(\cos\theta)$ (only
$m=0$ needed, since an incident beam along a fixed axis and a central
potential share no preferred azimuthal direction). Substituting
$u_l(r)=rR_l(r)$ into the full 3D Schrödinger equation gives, for each
$l$ separately:

$$
-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u_l''(r) + \left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right]u_l(r) = E\,u_l(r), \qquad u_l(0)=0
$$

This is **exactly a 1D Schrödinger equation** on the half-line $r\ge0$,
so the identical continuity-of-$\psi,\psi'$ matching technique from the 1D
scattering lessons applies unchanged. Two things are new here,
neither cosmetic: (1) an extra **centrifugal barrier**
$\hbar^2l(l+1)/2mr^2$, absent from any real 1D problem, pushing the
particle away from $r=0$ for $l>0$; (2) the boundary condition
$u_l(0)=0$ (needed for the actual 3D wavefunction $R_l(r)=u_l(r)/r$ to
stay finite at the origin) replaces the 1D problem's free asymptotic
condition on *both* sides. And because $l$ is conserved (a central
potential cannot scatter a particle from one $l$ into another), the full
3D problem splits into a **discrete, independent set of 1D-like
scattering problems**, one per $l$, usually called **partial-wave
channels**. This channel structure, not the boundary-matching mechanics
itself, is the real qualitative difference from 1D.

### Phase shifts: the entire content of the scattering information

Beyond the potential's range, $u_l(r)$ solves the *free* radial equation,
whose two independent solutions are the spherical Bessel functions
$j_l(kr)$ and $n_l(kr)$ (cited here as the standard closed-form free
solutions, the direct analogue of $\sin,\cos$ solving the free 1D
equation). Any physical solution's large-$r$ behavior takes the universal
form:

$$
u_l(r) \;\xrightarrow{r\to\infty}\; A_l\sin\!\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}2 + \delta_l\right)
$$

where $kr-l\pi/2$ is exactly the free-particle ($V=0$) asymptotic phase,
and $\delta_l$, the **phase shift**, is the *only* place the potential
still shows up at large $r$. Every observable in elastic scattering, the
differential cross section, the total cross section, the $S$-matrix, is
built entirely from the set $\{\delta_l\}$.

### Cross section and the S-matrix

The scattering amplitude and cross sections (a standard, longer algebraic
construction from matching the partial-wave sum to the physical
"incident plane wave plus outgoing spherical wave" boundary condition,
cited here without the full re-derivation, the same convention Resonant
Transmission Through a Barrier used for its own longer matching algebra):

<TheoremBox title="Partial-wave cross-section formula" provenance="cited">

**Hypotheses.** $V$ is real (so scattering is purely elastic, with no
absorption), spherically symmetric, and **short-range**: it must fall off
faster than $1/r$, fast enough that $rV(r)\to0$. Short-range is the
hypothesis that quietly does the most work here, because it is what makes
$\delta_l$ a finite number at all. It fails for the Coulomb potential.
An incoming particle in a $1/r$ tail is still being pushed at
arbitrarily large $r$, its asymptotic phase acquires a divergent
$\eta\ln(2kr)$ term, and no constant $\delta_l$ exists to absorb it.
Rutherford's own experiment, pictured above, therefore sits outside the
formula this lesson is about, and Coulomb scattering has to be treated by
a separate modified partial-wave analysis. The hard sphere below is at the
opposite extreme, with strictly zero range beyond $r=a$.

$$
f(\theta) = \frac1k\sum_{l=0}^\infty (2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad
\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta)|^2
$$

$$
\boxed{\;\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^\infty (2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l\;}
$$

</TheoremBox>

(the total cross section's sum-of-squares form, rather than a
square-of-sums, follows from the Legendre polynomials' orthogonality
killing every cross term upon integrating $|f(\theta)|^2$ over solid
angle).

<DefinitionBox title="S-matrix">

The **$S$-matrix** in channel $l$ is defined as
$S_l\equiv e^{2i\delta_l}$, the <Term id="partial-wave-scattering-s-matrix">ratio of the outgoing to incoming</Term>
spherical-wave coefficient hiding inside $\sin(kr-l\pi/2+\delta_l)=\frac1{2i}\left[e^{i(kr-l\pi/2+\delta_l)}-e^{-i(kr-l\pi/2+\delta_l)}\right]$.

</DefinitionBox>

For a real potential (no absorption), $\delta_l$ is real, so
$|S_l|=|e^{2i\delta_l}|=1$ **exactly**: this is the direct 3D
generalization of Scattering Off a Step Potential's $R+T=1$, now a
per-channel statement (incoming flux in channel $l$ equals outgoing flux
in that *same* channel $l$, since channels don't mix) where 1D had a
single global balance between two channels.

### Worked example: hard-sphere scattering

A hard sphere of radius $a$ ($V=\infty$ for $r<a$, $V=0$ outside) forces
$u_l(a)=0$ exactly (impenetrable wall). Outside, $u_l(r)\propto
kr\left[\cos\delta_l\,j_l(kr)-\sin\delta_l\,n_l(kr)\right]$, the
asymptotically-consistent combination; setting this to zero at $r=a$
gives the general result $\tan\delta_l=j_l(ka)/n_l(ka)$ (cited for
general $l$; worked exactly for $l=0$ below). Using the standard closed
forms $j_0(x)=\sin(x)/x$, $n_0(x)=-\cos(x)/x$:

$$
\tan\delta_0 = \frac{j_0(ka)}{n_0(ka)} = \frac{\sin(ka)/ka}{-\cos(ka)/ka} = -\tan(ka)
\;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\;\delta_0 = -ka\;}
$$

An exact, hand-checkable closed form, not an approximation. Substituting
into the boxed cross-section formula (keeping only $l=0$, the dominant
channel at low energy, since higher $l$'s are suppressed by the
centrifugal barrier):

$$
\sigma_0(ka) = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2(ka)
$$

## Why Low Energy Sees Only One Channel

At low energy ($ka\ll1$), $\sin(ka)\approx ka$, so
$\sigma_0\to(4\pi/k^2)(ka)^2=4\pi a^2$: **four times** the sphere's
classical cross-sectional area $\pi a^2$. This is a strange-looking
result, and a correct one, not a paradox: a classical particle either hits
the sphere (cross section $\pi a^2$) or misses entirely; a long-wavelength
quantum wave diffracts around the whole sphere, and the wave nature of
scattering, not the sphere's literal silhouette, sets the cross section.
The plot above shows this limit is approached smoothly and directly
from the same closed form governing every $ka$, with no separate
asymptotic argument needed.

## Dropping the Centrifugal Term

<Callout type="mistake">
**Forgetting the centrifugal term when writing down the "radial
Schrödinger equation."** Without $\hbar^2l(l+1)/2mr^2$, the equation
describes a 1D problem, not the $l$-th partial wave of a 3D one;
this term is exactly what makes different $l$'s behave differently even
for the *same* potential $V(r)$.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $\delta_l$ alone (without the $(2l+1)$ weight) sets each
channel's contribution to $\sigma$.** The boxed formula weights
$\sin^2\delta_l$ by $(2l+1)$, the degeneracy of the $l$-th channel's $m$
values; a small $\delta_l$ at large $l$ can still matter if $(2l+1)$ is
large enough, though in practice the centrifugal barrier usually
suppresses $\delta_l$ itself fast enough at low energy that $l=0$
dominates, as used above.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using $\delta_0=-ka$, compute $\sigma_0$ for $ka=0.5$ (sphere radius
   $a=1$), and express your answer as a multiple of $4\pi a^2$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $\sigma_0=0.9194\times4\pi a^2\approx11.553\,a^2$.

   With $a=1$ and $ka=0.5$ we have $k=0.5$, so

   $$
   \sigma_0=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2(ka)=\frac{4\pi}{0.25}\,(0.4794255)^2=16\pi\times0.2298488=11.5535.
   $$

   The cleaner route is to divide by $4\pi a^2$ before evaluating
   anything, because $k$ and $a$ then disappear into the single
   dimensionless variable the physics actually depends on:

   $$
   \frac{\sigma_0}{4\pi a^2}=\frac{(4\pi/k^2)\sin^2(ka)}{4\pi a^2}=\frac{\sin^2(ka)}{(ka)^2}=\mathrm{sinc}^2(ka),
   $$

   and $\mathrm{sinc}^2(0.5)=(0.4794255/0.5)^2=(0.958851)^2=0.919395$. This
   is the quantity the plot above draws, which is why its curve starts at
   exactly $1$ and has zeros at $ka=\pi,2\pi,\dots$.

   Two things follow from writing it that way. The answer depends only on
   $ka$, so a sphere twice as large probed at half the wavenumber gives the
   identical ratio; there is no separate size scale in the problem. And
   $ka=0.5$ is already deep enough into the long-wavelength regime that the
   cross section sits within $9\%$ of the full $4\pi a^2$ limit, which is
   worth knowing because "low energy" sounds like it should demand
   $ka\ll1$ by orders of magnitude and does not.

   The wrong turn is to answer $\sigma_0\approx11.55$ and stop. That
   number is in units of $a^2$ with $a$ set to $1$, and the ratio to the
   classical geometric cross section $\pi a^2$ is $11.553/\pi=3.68$, still
   most of the way to the factor of four. Quoting a bare number here hides
   the only comparison that means anything.

   </details>

2. Explain why $u_l(0)=0$ is required for *every* $l$, directly from
   $R_l(r)=u_l(r)/r$ needing to stay finite (not blow up) as $r\to0$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The short version is the one the question asks for: $R_l=u_l/r$, so
   $u_l(0)\neq0$ makes $R_l$ diverge like $1/r$ at the origin, and a
   wavefunction cannot do that. Near $r=0$ the radial equation is dominated
   by the centrifugal term, and its two behaviours are $u_l\sim r^{l+1}$
   (regular, giving $R_l\sim r^l$, finite) and $u_l\sim r^{-l}$ (irregular,
   giving $R_l\sim r^{-l-1}$). Imposing $u_l(0)=0$ selects the first and
   discards the second, in every channel.

   That answer is correct but incomplete, and the gap is worth opening,
   because "finite" is not actually the criterion a Hilbert space applies.
   Membership in $L^2$ is, and the radial measure is $r^2\,dr$:

   $$
   \int|\psi|^2\,d^3r=\int_0^\infty |R_l(r)|^2 r^2\,dr=\int_0^\infty|u_l(r)|^2\,dr.
   $$

   For $l\geq1$ the irregular solution $u_l\sim r^{-l}$ gives
   $\int_0 r^{-2l}\,dr$, which diverges, so those solutions genuinely are
   not states and normalizability alone throws them out. For $l=0$ it does
   not. The irregular $s$-wave solution is $u_0\to\text{const}$, giving
   $R_0\sim1/r$ and $\int_0|u_0|^2dr$ perfectly finite. The function
   $1/r$ *is* square-integrable near the origin in three dimensions. So the
   argument that works for $l\geq1$ fails precisely in the channel this
   lesson cares about most.

   What rules it out is that $R_0=1/r$ does not solve the Schrödinger
   equation at the origin. Using $\nabla^2(1/r)=-4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$, the
   candidate $\psi=1/r$ satisfies $H\psi=E\psi$ everywhere except $r=0$,
   where it leaves behind an uncancelled delta function. Accepting it would
   mean silently adding a point interaction at the origin that is not in
   the $V(r)$ you wrote down. Stated in the language of Hilbert Spaces and
   Self-Adjointness: the radial operator on the half-line is symmetric but
   not self-adjoint until a boundary condition at $r=0$ is chosen, there is
   a one-parameter family of admissible choices, and $u_0(0)=0$ is the one
   that reproduces the three-dimensional Laplacian. The other choices are
   not errors; they are the delta-shell and Fermi-pseudopotential
   interactions used to model contact forces in cold-atom physics, which is
   a *different* Hamiltonian.

   One hypothesis is quietly assumed throughout: that $V(r)$ is less
   singular than $1/r^2$ near the origin. A potential diverging as fast as
   or faster than $-1/r^2$ competes with the centrifugal term on equal
   footing, the two near-origin behaviours stop being $r^{l+1}$ and
   $r^{-l}$, and for a strong enough attractive $-\lambda/r^2$ the spectrum
   becomes unbounded below and no boundary condition at the origin saves
   it. Hard-sphere and finite-range potentials, this lesson's subject
   matter, are all comfortably inside the safe regime.

   </details>

3. Why can a central potential never scatter an incident $l=0$ wave into
   an outgoing $l=1$ wave, in terms of a conserved quantity from
   Orbital Angular Momentum and Spherical Harmonics?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $\vec L^{\,2}$ commutes with $H$, so $l$ is a constant of the
   motion and no amount of time evolution can change it.

   The one-line proof is worth doing explicitly, since it shows the
   conclusion holds to all orders and not just perturbatively. Let
   $|l\rangle$ and $|l'\rangle$ be eigenstates of $\vec L^{\,2}$ with
   eigenvalues $\hbar^2l(l+1)$ and $\hbar^2l'(l'+1)$. Then

   $$
   0=\langle l'|[H,\vec L^{\,2}]|l\rangle=\hbar^2\left[l(l+1)-l'(l'+1)\right]\langle l'|H|l\rangle,
   $$

   so $\langle l'|H|l\rangle=0$ whenever $l\neq l'$ (the bracket is nonzero
   for distinct non-negative integers). $H$ is block diagonal in $l$, hence
   so is $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$, and an amplitude that starts entirely in $l=0$
   stays there for all time. This is the formal content of the lesson's
   claim that the 3D problem "splits into independent channels": the
   splitting is exact, not an approximation good at low energy.

   The commutator itself comes from rotational invariance. $\vec L$
   generates rotations, and $H=p^2/2m+V(r)$ is built from two rotational
   scalars, so $[H,\vec L]=0$ and therefore $[H,\vec L^{\,2}]=0$. In the
   language of Clebsch-Gordan Coefficients and the Wigner-Eckart Theorem,
   $V(r)$ is a rank-$0$ spherical tensor, and Wigner-Eckart says a rank-$0$
   operator has vanishing matrix elements between states of different $l$
   (and different $m$). The partial-wave decomposition is a selection rule,
   nothing more exotic.

   Naming the hypothesis matters here, because it is the one real
   scattering problems break. "Central" has to mean $V$ depends on $r$
   alone. A spin-orbit term $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ is the near miss worth
   getting right, because it looks like a counterexample and is not one:
   $\vec L^{\,2}$ commutes with every component of $\vec L$ and with all
   of $\vec S$, so $[\vec L^{\,2},\vec L\cdot\vec S]=0$ identically and
   $l$ remains conserved. What spin-orbit coupling costs is $m_l$ and
   $m_s$ separately, leaving $l$, $j$ and $m_j$ good, so the phase shift
   picks up a second label, $\delta_{l,j}$, and the interaction can flip
   the spin without ever moving amplitude between $l$ channels. A genuine
   tensor force
   $S_{12}\propto3(\vec\sigma_1\cdot\hat r)(\vec\sigma_2\cdot\hat r)-\vec\sigma_1\cdot\vec\sigma_2$
   is a rank-2 tensor and does mix $l$ by two units: the deuteron is the
   textbook case, a bound state that is roughly $96\%$ $l=0$ and $4\%$
   $l=2$ with the same $j$. Any external field, even a weak one, breaks
   full rotational symmetry too, and then not even $j$ survives.

   The tensor force is what makes the hypothesis's exact shape matter, so
   it is worth stating the conclusion in the form the proof actually
   supports. Rotational invariance alone buys conservation of $j$ and
   $m_j$, since it says only that $\vec J$ generates a symmetry. Getting
   $l$ as well needs the stronger statement that the interaction commutes
   with $\vec L^{\,2}$, which holds for $V(r)$ and for $\vec L\cdot\vec S$
   and fails for $S_{12}$. So the correct claim is "$l$ is conserved by
   dynamics commuting with $\vec L^{\,2}$", and a rotationally invariant
   force need not qualify: the deuteron is the standing counterexample.

   Parity is a useful cross-check on the specific case asked about. A
   central potential conserves parity, $l=0$ is even and $l=1$ is odd, so
   the transition asked about is forbidden twice over, and even a
   non-central but parity-preserving perturbation could not produce it.

   </details>

4. If $|S_l|<1$ for some channel, what would that imply physically about
   the potential, given the derivation above connected $|S_l|=1$ directly
   to $\delta_l$ being real?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   That particles are disappearing from channel $l$: the potential is
   absorptive, and the elastic problem this lesson set up is no longer the
   whole story.

   Track it through the algebra. $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$, so writing
   $\delta_l=\alpha_l+i\beta_l$ gives $|S_l|=e^{-2\beta_l}$, and
   $|S_l|<1$ requires $\beta_l>0$: a complex phase shift with positive
   imaginary part. Since the reality of $\delta_l$ followed from $V$ being
   real, a complex $\delta_l$ means a complex potential, conventionally
   written $V-iW$ with $W>0$, the **optical potential** of nuclear physics.
   The continuity equation then has a sink term rather than balancing, so
   flux into the interaction region exceeds flux out.

   The essential point is that nothing about quantum mechanics has broken.
   $W$ is bookkeeping for channels the elastic calculation declined to
   model: the projectile knocked a nucleon out, excited the target, or was
   captured. Restore those channels and the full multi-channel $S$-matrix
   is exactly unitary again. $|S_l|<1$ is a statement about an incomplete
   description, not about lost probability.

   The quantitative consequence is the part worth carrying away. With
   $f=\frac1{2ik}\sum_l(2l+1)(S_l-1)P_l(\cos\theta)$, the cross sections
   separate into

   $$
   \sigma_{\text{el}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\,|1-S_l|^2,
   \qquad
   \sigma_{\text{r}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\left(1-|S_l|^2\right),
   $$

   the second counting everything that left the elastic channel, and
   $\sigma_{\text{tot}}=\sigma_{\text{el}}+\sigma_{\text{r}}$ still obeying
   the optical theorem $\sigma_{\text{tot}}=(4\pi/k)\,\mathrm{Im}f(0)$.
   Note that $\sigma_{\text{r}}$ vanishes identically when $|S_l|=1$, which
   is the consistency check that this reduces to the lesson's elastic case.

   Now the counter-intuitive fact this makes visible, and the wrong turn it
   guards against. It is natural to read absorption as "the particle was
   swallowed, so it did not scatter". Set $S_l=0$, total absorption in
   channel $l$, and the formulas give

   $$
   \sigma_{\text{r}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1),\qquad \sigma_{\text{el}}=\frac{\pi}{k^2}(2l+1),
   $$

   equal. A perfectly black channel scatters elastically exactly as much as
   it absorbs. Summing over the $l\lesssim ka$ that a black disk of radius
   $a$ blocks gives $\sigma_{\text{tot}}\to2\pi a^2$, twice the geometric
   area, for the same reason as the $4\pi a^2$ result earlier in this
   lesson: removing amplitude from the forward beam requires a diffracted
   wave to interfere it away, and that shadow-forming wave is itself
   elastic scattering that a detector off-axis will register.

   The one thing $|S_l|>1$ would imply is that the model has a source in
   it, creating particles from nothing. Unitarity of the underlying
   many-channel theory forbids it, so $|S_l|\leq1$ is a hard bound rather
   than a convention, and a fit that returns $|S_l|>1$ is a fit with an
   error in it.

   </details>

## Summary

- A central potential separates 3D scattering into independent $l$
  channels, each an effective 1D problem with an extra centrifugal term
  $\hbar^2l(l+1)/2mr^2$ and the boundary condition $u_l(0)=0$.
- Every observable is built from the phase shifts $\delta_l$:
  $\sigma=(4\pi/k^2)\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$, and the $S$-matrix
  $S_l=e^{2i\delta_l}$ satisfies $|S_l|=1$ exactly, the 3D generalization
  of 1D scattering's $R+T=1$.
- Hard-sphere scattering gives the exact closed form $\delta_0=-ka$, and
  the low-energy limit $\sigma_0\to4\pi a^2$, four times the classical
  geometric cross section, both computed here from that closed form.
