## Beyond Two Spin-Halves

Addition of Angular Momentum combined two spin-$\tfrac12$ particles by
direct inspection: four candidate states, checked by hand against $\vec
J^{\,2}$ and $J_z$. That approach doesn't scale. Real problems need $l=1$
electrons coupled to spin, two different orbital angular momenta coupled
to each other, arbitrary $j_1,j_2$. This lesson derives the **general**
machinery: a recursion relation that generates every Clebsch-Gordan
coefficient for any $j_1\otimes j_2$, then <Term id="clebsch-gordan-wigner-eckart">a theorem, Wigner-Eckart, that</Term>
turns "does this transition happen at all" into a question answerable
from symmetry alone.

## The Ladder Recursion, and What It Buys

### The general recursion

Let $|j,m\rangle=\sum_{m_1,m_2}C^{j,m}_{j_1m_1;j_2m_2}|j_1,m_1\rangle|j_2,m_2\rangle$
(sum restricted to $m_1+m_2=m$, since $J_z=J_{1z}+J_{2z}$ forces this).
Apply the total lowering operator $J_-=J_{1-}+J_{2-}$ to both sides. The
left side uses the ordinary single-$j$ ladder formula (Ladder Operators
and the Angular Momentum Spectrum); the right side distributes over the
product states, each factor lowering independently:

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Apply J₋ = J₁₋ + J₂₋ to both sides of the coupling expansion. The left side reduces to the ordinary single-j ladder formula acting on the coupled state |j,m⟩, giving the coefficient one step down in m.">

$$
\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m-1)}\;C^{j,m-1}_{j_1m_1;j_2m_2}
$$

  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="On the right, J₁₋ lowers only the j1 factor of the product state. This term is the coupled-state coefficient before lowering, with j1's magnetic number one higher.">

$$
{}=\sqrt{j_1(j_1+1)-m_1(m_1+1)}\;C^{j,m}_{j_1,m_1+1;j_2m_2}
$$

  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="J₂₋ lowers only the j2 factor, producing the second right-hand term, with j2's magnetic number one higher, obtained by matching the coefficient of |j1,m1⟩|j2,m2⟩ on both sides after the ladder action.">

$$
{}+\sqrt{j_2(j_2+1)-m_2(m_2+1)}\;C^{j,m}_{j_1m_1;j_2,m_2+1}
$$

  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

This single relation, applied
repeatedly, generates every coefficient within a fixed $j$ multiplet from
one known starting point.

### Where each multiplet starts

The **top state** of the top multiplet is unique: $m=j_1+j_2$ has only one
product state with that total, $|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle$, so
$|j_1+j_2,\,j_1+j_2\rangle=|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle$ exactly, no
recursion needed. Lowering it via the recursion above generates the
entire $j=j_1+j_2$ multiplet. At the next value of $m$ down from the top,
exactly **two** product states share that $m$; the $j=j_1+j_2$
combination is already fixed by the ladder result, and the state
**orthogonal** to it (within that 2-dimensional same-$m$ subspace) is, by
construction, annihilated by lowering into anything outside its own
multiplet, making it the top state of the *next* multiplet,
$j=j_1+j_2-1$. Repeating (ladder down, peel off the orthogonal
complement, start the next $j$) generates every multiplet down to
$j=|j_1-j_2|$ (the triangle rule's lower bound, the same bound Addition
of Angular Momentum found by exhaustive counting for $j_1=j_2=\tfrac12$).
The one remaining freedom at each new top state (an overall phase) is
fixed by the **Condon-Shortley convention**: the coefficient of the
term with the largest $m_1$ is taken real and positive.

### Worked example: l=1⊗s=1/2

Apply this by hand to $j_1=1,j_2=\tfrac12$ (an orbital electron's $l=1$
coupled to its spin, six total states).

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The top state is unique: only one product state has m = j1+j2, so no recursion is needed to identify it.">
    $$
    \left|\tfrac32,\tfrac32\right\rangle=\left|1,1\right\rangle\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Apply J₋ = L₋ + S₋. The left side uses the ordinary single-j ladder formula.">
    $$
    \sqrt{\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac52-\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12}=\sqrt3
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="The right side distributes over the two product-state terms, each factor lowering independently.">
    $$
    \sqrt{1\cdot2-1\cdot0}\,\left|1,0\right\rangle\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\sqrt{\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac32-\tfrac12\cdot(-\tfrac12)}\,\left|1,1\right\rangle\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle=\sqrt2\,\left|1,0;\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\left|1,1;\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Divide both sides by √3, the left-side ladder normalization from the previous step.">
    $$
    \left|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac23}\,\left|1,0\right\rangle\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac13}\,\left|1,1\right\rangle\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

The **orthogonal** combination of the same two product states
($|1,1\rangle|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\rangle$ and
$|1,0\rangle|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle$), with the largest-$m_1$ term
($|1,1\rangle|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\rangle$) taken positive, starts the
$j=\tfrac12$ multiplet: $\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac23}|1,1\rangle|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\rangle-\sqrt{\tfrac13}|1,0\rangle|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle$.
Continuing the recursion on both multiplets generates all six states.
Building all six as explicit vectors and checking them against this
platform's own total $\vec J^{\,2}$ and $J_z$ (built from
`angularMomentumX/Y/Z` at $j_1=1$ and $j_2=\tfrac12$, the same
approach Addition of Angular Momentum used, generalized to unequal
$j_1,j_2$) confirms every coefficient, to floating-point precision:

| State | $\vec J^{\,2}$ eigenvalue | $J_z$ eigenvalue |

| $|3/2,+3/2\rangle$ | $3.75$ | $1.5$ |
| $|3/2,+1/2\rangle$ | $3.75$ | $0.5$ |
| $|3/2,-1/2\rangle$ | $3.75$ | $-0.5$ |
| $|3/2,-3/2\rangle$ | $3.75$ | $-1.5$ |

### The Wigner-Eckart theorem

<DefinitionBox title="Spherical tensor operator">

A **spherical tensor operator** of rank $k$, $T^k_q$ ($q=-k,\ldots,k$), is
defined by transforming under rotations exactly like $|k,q\rangle$ does:

$$
[J_z,T^k_q]=q\,T^k_q, \qquad [J_\pm,T^k_q]=\sqrt{k(k+1)-q(q\pm1)}\,T^k_{q\pm1}
$$

</DefinitionBox>

These are *exactly* the same commutation relations $J_z,J_\pm$ satisfy
with the angular-momentum basis kets $|k,q\rangle$ themselves. Sandwiching
$T^k_q$ between angular-momentum eigenstates,
$\langle j'm'|T^k_q|jm\rangle$, and demanding consistency with these
commutators as $m,m',q$ vary produces the *identical* recursion relation
in $(m,m',q)$ derived above for Clebsch-Gordan coefficients
$\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle$. A linear recursion with the same structure
and the same boundary conditions has (for fixed $j,j',k$) a
one-dimensional solution space, so the two objects can only differ by an
overall constant, independent of $m,m',q$:

<TheoremBox title="Wigner–Eckart theorem" provenance="derived">

**Hypotheses.** $T^k_q$ is a spherical tensor operator of rank $k$ in
the sense just defined, and $|jm\rangle,|j'm'\rangle$ are eigenstates of
one and the same total angular momentum $\vec J$. Any further quantum
numbers (a radial label $n$, say) ride along inside the reduced matrix
element and are suppressed in the notation below; they change
$\langle j'\|T^k\|j\rangle$ and nothing else. The proportionality
argument needs $j,j',k$ to satisfy the triangle rule, and when they do
not, both sides are zero and the statement is vacuously true. One
convention warning: many texts carry a $1/\sqrt{2j'+1}$ on the
right-hand side, which rescales the reduced matrix element without
changing any physical ratio. The form below omits it.

$$
\boxed{\;\langle j'm'|T^k_q|jm\rangle = \langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle\,\langle j'\|T^k\|j\rangle\;}
$$

$\langle j'\|T^k\|j\rangle$, the **reduced matrix element**, carries all
the operator-specific physics (radial integrals, coupling strengths); the
Clebsch-Gordan coefficient carries *all* of the angular dependence, for
every $m,m',q$ at once.

</TheoremBox>

### Selection rules for free

$J_z$ conservation forces $\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle=0$ unless
$m'=m+q$ (apply $J_z$ to $|jm\rangle|kq\rangle$ before coupling: it has
eigenvalue $m+q$; the coupled $|j'm'\rangle$ has eigenvalue $m'$; these
can only match if $m'=m+q$, a two-line argument). The triangle
rule from the recursion's construction above forces
$\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle=0$ unless $|j-k|\le j'\le j+k$. **Both
conditions are entirely about which CG coefficient vanishes**, established
above with no reference to any specific operator; by Wigner-Eckart, *any*
rank-$k$ tensor operator's matrix element inherits both selection rules
automatically, with zero additional calculation.

## Selection Rules Without an Integral

<InsightBlock>
  Whether a transition is allowed is a symmetry question, answerable from
  Clebsch-Gordan vanishing conditions alone: no radial integral has to
  be evaluated to know a transition is forbidden.
</InsightBlock>

Hydrogen's electric dipole operator $\vec r$ has three spherical
components that transform exactly as $Y_1^q$, i.e. as a rank-$1$ ($k=1$)
tensor. Wigner-Eckart immediately gives $\Delta m=q\in\{0,\pm1\}$ and
$|l-1|\le l'\le l+1$, i.e. $\Delta l\in\{-1,0,+1\}$, with zero integration.
(Ruling out $\Delta l=0$ specifically needs one more, separate ingredient:
parity. $Y_1^q$ is odd under $\vec r\to-\vec r$, and $Y_l^m$ has parity
$(-1)^l$, so the full matrix element's integrand has parity
$(-1)^{l+l'+1}$; it vanishes unless $l+l'$ is odd, which combined with the
triangle rule above leaves exactly $\Delta l=\pm1$. This parity argument
is a separate ingredient from Wigner-Eckart itself, worth stating honestly
rather than folding silently into "the selection rule.") This is
the electric-dipole selection rule underlying every atomic transition
this platform's Hydrogen Atom course used, now derived from symmetry.

**Confirm $\langle1,0;\tfrac12,-\tfrac12|\tfrac32,-\tfrac12\rangle=\sqrt{2/3}$
directly from the $m$-conservation rule and the table above.**

The table's $|3/2,-1/2\rangle$ row has nonzero coefficients only on

## Reading Proportionality as Equality

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming Wigner-Eckart says a matrix element equals the CG
coefficient.** It says the matrix element is *proportional* to the CG
coefficient, with an operator-and-$j,j'$-dependent (but
$m,m',q$-independent) reduced matrix element absorbing everything else.
Two different rank-$1$ operators between the same $j,j'$ have matrix
elements in the *same ratios* across $m,m',q$, not identical values.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Concluding $\Delta l=\pm1$ from Wigner-Eckart/CG alone.** The
triangle rule alone permits $\Delta l\in\{-1,0,+1\}$ for a rank-1
operator; excluding $\Delta l=0$ specifically requires the separate
parity argument above, not an extra CG vanishing condition.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Apply the recursion relation to $\left|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right\rangle$
   (derived in the worked example above) to derive
   $\left|\tfrac32,-\tfrac12\right\rangle$, and confirm your result
   matches the table.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Apply $J_-=L_-+S_-$ to both sides of

   $$
   \left|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1,0\rangle\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1,1\rangle\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle .
   $$

   Left side, by the single-$j$ ladder formula at $j=\tfrac32$, $m=\tfrac12$:

   $$
   \sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m-1)}=\sqrt{\tfrac{15}{4}+\tfrac14}=\sqrt4=2 ,
   $$

   so the left side is $2\left|\tfrac32,-\tfrac12\right\rangle$.

   Right side, lowering each factor separately. On the first term,
   $L_-|1,0\rangle=\sqrt2\,|1,-1\rangle$ and
   $S_-\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle=\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle$.
   On the second term, $L_-|1,1\rangle=\sqrt2\,|1,0\rangle$ while
   $S_-\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle=0$, since $-\tfrac12$ is
   already the bottom rung. Collecting the two surviving product states:

   $$
   \sqrt{\tfrac23}\sqrt2\;\left|-1,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\left(\sqrt{\tfrac23}+\sqrt{\tfrac13}\sqrt2\right)\left|0,-\tfrac12\right\rangle
   =\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\left|-1,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\tfrac{2\sqrt2}{\sqrt3}\left|0,-\tfrac12\right\rangle .
   $$

   Divide by $2$:

   $$
   \left|\tfrac32,-\tfrac12\right\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1,-1\rangle\left|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right\rangle+\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1,0\rangle\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle .
   $$

   An explicit rebuild of the lowering step returns
   $0.577350$ and $0.816497$, which are $\sqrt{1/3}$ and $\sqrt{2/3}$, and
   these are exactly the two entries the worked example reads off the
   table for the $|3/2,-1/2\rangle$ row. The norm squared is
   $\tfrac13+\tfrac23=1$ to twelve decimals.

   Compare the result to $\left|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\right\rangle$ and a
   symmetry is visible: both interior $j=\tfrac32$ states put the larger
   coefficient $\sqrt{2/3}$ on the product state with $m_1=0$, and the
   two are mirror images under $m\to-m$ with $m_S$ flipped. That is
   forced, since the whole multiplet is generated by lowering from a
   state symmetric under nothing in particular; the mirror symmetry comes
   from the $m\to-m$ symmetry of the ladder coefficients themselves.

   The wrong turn is dividing by the wrong ladder factor, most often
   $\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m+1)}=\sqrt3$, which is the *raising* normalization
   read off by reflex. The coefficients then come out $0.666667$ and
   $0.942809$, and the tell is immediate: their squares sum to
   $\tfrac43$, not $1$. Checking normalization catches this every time,
   which is why it is worth doing even when the algebra felt safe. A
   second wrong turn is applying $J_-$ to only one factor of a product
   state, or forgetting that $S_-$ annihilates
   $\left|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\right\rangle$ and manufacturing a third,
   nonexistent term at $m_2=-\tfrac32$.

   </details>

2. Using $m$-conservation alone, explain why
   $\langle j'm'|T^1_1|jm\rangle$ must vanish unless $m'=m+1$, for *any*
   rank-1 tensor operator $T^1$, not just the dipole operator.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The defining property of a rank-$k$ spherical tensor supplies the
   whole proof in two lines. For $k=1$, $q=1$ the definition reads
   $[J_z,T^1_1]=1\cdot T^1_1$. Sandwich that commutator between the two
   eigenstates and let $J_z$ act on whichever side it faces:

   $$
   \langle j'm'|[J_z,T^1_1]|jm\rangle=(m'-m)\,\langle j'm'|T^1_1|jm\rangle
   =1\cdot\langle j'm'|T^1_1|jm\rangle ,
   $$

   so $(m'-m-1)\,\langle j'm'|T^1_1|jm\rangle=0$. Either $m'=m+1$, or the
   matrix element is zero. There is no third option.

   Nothing in that argument mentions the dipole, a radial integral, a
   wavefunction, or even the value of $j$ and $j'$. The only input is the
   commutator, which is what "rank-1 tensor operator" *means*. Physically,
   $T^1_1$ is built to transform like $|1,+1\rangle$, so it carries one
   unit of $J_z$ into the matrix element, and the bra has to absorb it.
   This is angular-momentum conservation written at the level of
   operators.

   It is equally worth naming what the rule does not say. It gives a
   condition for the element to be *allowed*, never a guarantee that it is
   nonzero when the condition holds. Vanishing can still come from the
   triangle rule on $j,j',k$, or from the reduced matrix element
   $\langle j'\|T^1\|j\rangle$ being zero for that particular operator.
   Selection rules exclude; they do not include.

   The wrong turn is proving it for the dipole by writing the angular
   integral $\int Y_{l'}^{m'*}Y_1^1Y_l^m\,d\Omega$ and observing that the
   azimuthal part $\int_0^{2\pi}e^{i(m+1-m')\phi}d\phi$ vanishes unless
   $m'=m+1$. That derivation is correct and it answers a narrower
   question than the one asked. Worse, it does not generalize: the spin
   operator components $S_{\pm},S_z$ form a perfectly good rank-1
   spherical tensor and there is no spatial wavefunction to integrate
   anywhere in sight, so no product of spherical harmonics exists to
   write down. The commutator proof is not merely tidier, it is the only
   one available in general.

   </details>
3. For $j_1=1,j_2=1$ (two $l=1$ systems combined, e.g. two p-electrons'
   orbital angular momenta), what range of total $j$ does the triangle
   rule permit? List every value.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   The rule is $|j_1-j_2|\le j\le j_1+j_2$ in integer steps, so with
   $j_1=j_2=1$:

   $$
   j\in\{0,\;1,\;2\} .
   $$

   All three, including the lower bound $|1-1|=0$, which is attained
   rather than approached.

   The check that makes this trustworthy rather than recalled is the
   dimension count. The uncoupled space has
   $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)=3\times3=9$ states, and the coupled multiplets
   contribute $2j+1$ each:

   $$
   (2\cdot0+1)+(2\cdot1+1)+(2\cdot2+1)=1+3+5=9 .
   $$

   Coupling is a change of basis, so the totals must agree exactly. Drop
   $j=0$ and you have $8$ states where there were $9$, which is the
   fastest way to notice an omission.

   In spectroscopic language these are the $S$, $P$ and $D$ terms of a
   two-p-electron configuration. There is a further piece of structure
   worth knowing, since the question names two p-electrons specifically:
   for $j_1=j_2=j$ the coupled state has exchange symmetry
   $(-1)^{2j-J}$, so here $J=2$ and $J=0$ are symmetric under swapping
   the two electrons' orbital labels while $J=1$ is antisymmetric.
   Combined with the Pauli principle, a symmetric orbital state must pair
   with the antisymmetric spin singlet and vice versa, which selects
   exactly $^1S_0$, $^3P$ and $^1D_2$ as the allowed terms of a $p^2$
   configuration such as carbon's ground configuration. The triangle rule
   supplies the candidates; antisymmetry prunes them.

   The wrong turn is producing half-integer or non-integer values. Both
   $j_1$ and $j_2$ are integers here, so $j_1+j_2$ is an integer and the
   ladder steps by one, keeping every $j$ an integer; half-integers can
   only appear when an odd number of half-integer angular momenta are
   coupled. A second wrong turn is stopping at $\{1,2\}$ on the grounds
   that $j=0$ means "no angular momentum" and therefore cannot arise from
   two nonzero ones. It arises precisely when the two are antiparallel,
   and the dimension count refuses to balance without it.

   </details>

4. Why does the top state $|j_1+j_2,j_1+j_2\rangle$ need no recursion at
   all, while every other state in every other multiplet does?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because at the very top there is only one product state to choose
   from, so there is nothing left to determine.

   Conservation of $J_z$ restricts the expansion to product states with
   $m_1+m_2=m$. At $m=j_1+j_2$, the largest available values are
   $m_1\le j_1$ and $m_2\le j_2$, so the sum can reach $j_1+j_2$ only by
   saturating both at once: $m_1=j_1$ and $m_2=j_2$, and no other pair
   works. That $m$-subspace is one-dimensional, and a one-dimensional
   space has exactly one unit vector up to phase, so

   $$
   |j_1+j_2,\,j_1+j_2\rangle=|j_1,j_1\rangle|j_2,j_2\rangle
   $$

   with coefficient $1$. There is no superposition to solve for, hence no
   recursion to run.

   One step down the picture changes qualitatively. At $m=j_1+j_2-1$ two
   product states share the value, $(j_1-1,j_2)$ and $(j_1,j_2-1)$, so the
   $m$-subspace is two-dimensional and "which unit vector" becomes a real
   question with a whole circle of candidate answers. The recursion is
   what answers it, and the subspace dimension keeps growing as $m$
   descends, up to $\min(2j_1,2j_2)+1$, so the question only gets harder.
   This is the same counting the lesson's prediction exercise makes
   concrete for $l=1\otimes s=\tfrac12$: one contributing product state at
   $m_j=\tfrac32$, two at $m_j=\tfrac12$.

   Two honest refinements, since the contrast is slightly sharper than
   the question's phrasing suggests. First, the top state of each *lower*
   multiplet is not produced by the recursion either; it is produced by
   orthogonality to the states already built at that $m$, with the
   Condon-Shortley convention fixing its phase. Recursion generates
   states within a multiplet, orthogonalization starts new ones. Second,
   the *bottom* state $|j_1+j_2,-(j_1+j_2)\rangle$ is forced by exactly
   the same uniqueness argument as the top, $m_1=-j_1$ and $m_2=-j_2$
   being the only option, even though in practice the ladder delivers it.

   The wrong turn is explaining the top state's simplicity by saying it
   is "the most aligned" or "the classical configuration," which sounds
   physical but is not an argument. The reason is purely a counting fact
   about how many pairs $(m_1,m_2)$ can sum to a given $m$, and it would
   hold for any operator with the same additive quantum number.

   </details>

## Summary

- The general Clebsch-Gordan recursion,
  $\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m-1)}\,C^{j,m-1}=\sqrt{j_1(j_1+1)-m_1(m_1+1)}\,C^{j,m}_{m_1+1,m_2}+\sqrt{j_2(j_2+1)-m_2(m_2+1)}\,C^{j,m}_{m_1,m_2+1}$,
  plus "peel off the orthogonal complement" to start each new multiplet,
  generates every $j_1\otimes j_2$ coupling coefficient.
- Worked explicitly for $l=1\otimes s=\tfrac12$, verified as exact $\vec
  J^{\,2}$/$J_z$ eigenstates against this platform's own operators, not
  merely quoted from a table.
- Wigner-Eckart, $\langle j'm'|T^k_q|jm\rangle=\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle\langle
  j'\|T^k\|j\rangle$, follows because tensor-operator matrix elements obey
  the identical recursion CG coefficients do; selection rules ($\Delta
  m=q$, the triangle rule) follow immediately, with no integral evaluated.
