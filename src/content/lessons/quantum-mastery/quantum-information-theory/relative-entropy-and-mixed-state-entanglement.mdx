## When Purity Stops Measuring Entanglement

Concurrence: A Two-Qubit Entanglement Measure computed a real
entanglement measure for pure states and then stopped, naming the
general mixed-state Wootters concurrence formula explicitly and refusing
to implement it: it needs eigenvalues of a **non-Hermitian** matrix
product, which this platform's closed-form $2\times2$-Hermitian-only
eigensolver cannot handle in general. This lesson builds the general
formula properly, and finds one properly mixed, structurally special
case where this platform's *existing* eigensolver, unchanged, is
still exactly enough to compute it.

## Klein's Inequality, Concurrence, and Peres-Horodecki

### Quantum relative entropy

<EquationReveal
  terms={[
    { id: "Srel", symbol: "S(ρ‖σ)", gloss: "Quantum relative entropy, a measure of how costly it is to mistake σ for the true state ρ." },
    { id: "rho", symbol: "ρ", gloss: "The true state." },
    { id: "sigma", symbol: "σ", gloss: "The (possibly wrong) reference state being compared against." },
  ]}
>

$$
S(\rho\|\sigma) \equiv \text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho) - \text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\sigma)
$$

</EquationReveal>

the <Term id="quantum-relative-entropy">quantum relative entropy</Term>, a
generalization of the classical Kullback–Leibler divergence measuring how
distinguishable $\sigma$ is from the "true" state $\rho$, in base-2
logarithms to match this platform's von Neumann entropy convention.

### Klein's inequality: $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq0$

<TheoremBox title="Klein's inequality" provenance="derived">

**Hypotheses.** $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are density matrices on the same
finite-dimensional Hilbert space. For $S(\rho\|\sigma)$ to be finite one
further condition is needed: $\text{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\text{supp}(\sigma)$.
Otherwise the quantity is $+\infty$, which is the correct value rather
than an error, and the inequality holds trivially.

**Conclusion.** $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq0$, with equality if and only if
$\rho=\sigma$.

Nothing is assumed about commutation. The proof below handles the
non-commuting case through the doubly stochastic matrix
$c_{ij}=|\langle i|j\rangle|^2$, which is exactly where the
non-commutativity is absorbed; when $\rho$ and $\sigma$ do commute,
$c_{ij}$ is a permutation and the argument collapses to the classical
Kullback–Leibler statement.

</TheoremBox>

**Proof.**

<DerivationSteps>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Diagonalize both states in their own eigenbases, ρ=Σp_i|i⟩⟨i|, σ=Σq_j|j⟩⟨j|. Then Tr(ρ log₂σ) expands using c_ij ≡ |⟨i|j⟩|² ≥ 0, a doubly stochastic matrix (Σⱼc_ij = ⟨i|i⟩ = 1 and Σᵢc_ij = ⟨j|j⟩ = 1, both from completeness of each basis).">
    $$
    S(\rho\|\sigma) = \sum_ip_i\Big[\log_2p_i - \sum_jc_{ij}\log_2q_j\Big]
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="By concavity of log₂ (Jensen's inequality, weights c_ij summing to 1 over j): Σⱼc_ij log₂qⱼ ≤ log₂(Σⱼc_ijqⱼ) ≡ log₂rᵢ. So log₂pᵢ − Σⱼc_ijlog₂qⱼ ≥ log₂pᵢ − log₂rᵢ = log₂(pᵢ/rᵢ), where D_cl(p‖r) is the classical KL divergence between p and the distribution rᵢ ≡ Σⱼc_ijqⱼ.">
    $$
    S(\rho\|\sigma) \geq \sum_ip_i\log_2(p_i/r_i) = D_{cl}(p\|r)
    $$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="r is itself a probability distribution: Σᵢrᵢ = Σᵢⱼc_ijqⱼ = Σⱼqⱼ Σᵢc_ij = Σⱼqⱼ = 1, using doubly-stochasticity again.">
    $\sum_ir_i=\sum_{ij}c_{ij}q_j=\sum_jq_j\sum_ic_{ij}=\sum_jq_j=1$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="D_cl(p‖r) ≥ 0 by a second application of Jensen.">
    $-D_{cl}(p\|r)=\sum_ip_i\log_2(r_i/p_i)\leq\log_2\big(\sum_ip_i\cdot r_i/p_i\big)=\log_2\big(\sum_ir_i\big)=\log_2(1)=0$
  </DerivationStep>
  <DerivationStep annotation="Chaining both steps proves the inequality. Equality throughout requires both Jensen steps to saturate, which forces pᵢ = rᵢ for every i and c_ij to reduce to a permutation matrix, i.e. ρ and σ commute and share eigenvectors with identical eigenvalues, i.e. ρ = σ.">
    $S(\rho\|\sigma)\geq D_{cl}(p\|r)\geq0$ $\blacksquare$
  </DerivationStep>
</DerivationSteps>

<MatrixGrid matrix={rhoBiased} label="rho = diag(0.9, 0.1)" compareTo={sigmaMaxMixed} compareLabel="sigma = I/2" ariaLabel="The two states used in the worked relative-entropy example" />

**Worked example.** For $\rho=\text{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ and $\sigma=I/2$
(both already diagonal in the same basis, so $c_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$ and
the inequality above is already tight, $S=D_{cl}$ exactly):

$$
S(\rho\|\sigma) = 0.9\log_2\frac{0.9}{0.5} + 0.1\log_2\frac{0.1}{0.5} \approx 0.531 \text{ bits}
$$

a strictly positive number, as Klein's inequality requires, computed
directly via `Matrix`/`Complex` arithmetic matching this platform's
existing `vonNeumannEntropy` conventions. (A structural edge case worth
naming: if $\sigma$ has a zero eigenvalue in a direction $\rho$ has
support on (e.g. $\rho=|0\rangle\langle0|$, $\sigma=|+\rangle\langle+|$),
$S(\rho\|\sigma)=+\infty$, since $\log_2(0)=-\infty$ appears with a
nonzero $p_i$ weight; this is the standard, expected behavior of a
divergence, not a computational error.)

### The general (mixed-state) Wootters concurrence

For a pure state, Concurrence: A Two-Qubit Entanglement Measure used
$C=2|ad-bc|$. The general definition, valid for *any* two-qubit state
$\rho$ (mixed or pure):

$$
\tilde\rho \equiv (\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)\,\rho^*\,(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y), \qquad R \equiv \rho\tilde\rho, \qquad C(\rho) \equiv \max\big(0,\ \sqrt{\mu_1}-\sqrt{\mu_2}-\sqrt{\mu_3}-\sqrt{\mu_4}\big)
$$

where $\mu_1\geq\mu_2\geq\mu_3\geq\mu_4\geq0$ are $R$'s eigenvalues. That
they are real and non-negative at all is not obvious, since $R$ is in
general **non-Hermitian**. The reason: $\tilde\rho\geq0$ (it is a unitary
conjugation of $\rho^*$, and complex conjugation preserves positive
semidefiniteness), so with $A=\sqrt\rho$ and $B=\sqrt\rho\,\tilde\rho$ we
have $R=AB$ while $BA=\sqrt\rho\,\tilde\rho\sqrt\rho\geq0$. Since $AB$
and $BA$ always share their nonzero spectrum, $R$'s eigenvalues are those
of a positive semidefinite matrix, with no invertibility assumed
anywhere. This
formula (Wootters, 1998) is *defined* here as stated; the theorem that
it equals the **entanglement of formation** (the true convex-roof
extension of entanglement entropy to mixed states) is a celebrated,
difficult result whose proof is **cited, not re-derived**: it requires
optimizing over every possible pure-state decomposition of $\rho$, a
substantially harder problem than anything built so far
on this platform.

**Reduction to the pure-state formula, derived, not cited.** For
$\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ pure, $\tilde\rho=|\tilde\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|$
for $|\tilde\psi\rangle\equiv(\sigma_y\otimes\sigma_y)|\psi^*\rangle$, so
$R=\rho\tilde\rho=\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle\,|\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|$,
rank 1 with a single nonzero eigenvalue
$\mu_1=|\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle|^2$ (the other three are exactly
$0$). Then $C=\sqrt{\mu_1}=|\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle|$. Direct
symbolic computation for $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$
with fully general complex amplitudes $a,b,c,d$ confirms
$|\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle|=2|ad-bc|$ exactly: the two formulas
agree completely on every pure state, confirming that the general
definition extends the pure-state one; the shared name is not a
coincidence.

### A second, independent test: the Peres-Horodecki criterion

Concurrence is not the only way to ask "is this mixed state entangled?"
There is a second test, discovered the same year the Wootters program
began, that needs no eigensolver gymnastics at all: transpose half the
state and see if it is still a state.

<DefinitionBox title="Partial transpose">

For a two-qubit state $\rho$ with matrix elements
$\rho_{(ij),(kl)}=\langle ij|\rho|kl\rangle$ (first index qubit $A$,
second qubit $B$), the partial transpose over $B$ transposes only the
second index pair:

$$
\big(\rho^{T_B}\big)_{(ij),(kl)} \equiv \rho_{(il),(kj)}
$$

$\rho^{T_B}$ is always Hermitian with trace 1, but it need not be
positive semidefinite. That failure is the test.

</DefinitionBox>

<TheoremBox title="Peres-Horodecki (PPT) criterion" provenance="cited">

If $\rho$ is separable, then $\rho^{T_B}\geq0$ (Peres, Phys. Rev. Lett.
77, 1413 (1996)): a negative eigenvalue of the partial transpose proves
entanglement. For $2\times2$ and $2\times3$ systems the converse also
holds (M., P., and R. Horodecki, Phys. Lett. A 223, 1 (1996)):
$\rho^{T_B}\geq0$ proves separability. So for two qubits, PPT is a
complete entanglement test. Both directions are cited here, not
re-derived; the sufficiency proof uses the theory of positive maps.

</TheoremBox>

**Cross-check on the same Werner state, all four eigenvalues computed.**
In the basis $\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}$, with
$p=0.7$:

$$
\rho_W=\begin{pmatrix}
\tfrac{1-p}{4} & 0 & 0 & 0\\[2pt]
0 & \tfrac{1+p}{4} & -\tfrac{p}{2} & 0\\[2pt]
0 & -\tfrac{p}{2} & \tfrac{1+p}{4} & 0\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1-p}{4}
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
0.075 & 0 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0.425 & -0.35 & 0\\
0 & -0.35 & 0.425 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0 & 0.075
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The only off-diagonal entries sit at $(01,10)$ and $(10,01)$. The partial
transpose swaps the $B$ indices, so it moves those two coherences to the
$(00,11)$ and $(11,00)$ corners and leaves every diagonal entry fixed:

$$
\rho_W^{T_B}=\begin{pmatrix}
\tfrac{1-p}{4} & 0 & 0 & -\tfrac{p}{2}\\[2pt]
0 & \tfrac{1+p}{4} & 0 & 0\\[2pt]
0 & 0 & \tfrac{1+p}{4} & 0\\[2pt]
-\tfrac{p}{2} & 0 & 0 & \tfrac{1-p}{4}
\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}
0.075 & 0 & 0 & -0.35\\
0 & 0.425 & 0 & 0\\
0 & 0 & 0.425 & 0\\
-0.35 & 0 & 0 & 0.075
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Two eigenvalues can be read off the untouched middle diagonal:
$\tfrac{1+p}{4}=0.425$, twice. The corner $2\times2$ block
$\big(\begin{smallmatrix}0.075 & -0.35\\ -0.35 & 0.075\end{smallmatrix}\big)$
has eigenvalues $0.075\pm0.35$, i.e. $0.425$ and $-0.275$. The full
spectrum of $\rho_W^{T_B}$ at $p=0.7$ is therefore

$$
\Big\{\tfrac{1+p}{4},\ \tfrac{1+p}{4},\ \tfrac{1+p}{4},\ \tfrac{1-3p}{4}\Big\}
= \{0.425,\ 0.425,\ 0.425,\ -0.275\}
$$

which sums to $1$, as any partial transpose's spectrum must. One
eigenvalue is negative, so the state is entangled: the same verdict the
concurrence computation reached, by a completely different route. Better
still, the general-$p$ spectrum makes the agreement quantitative. The
lone candidate for negativity is $\tfrac{1-3p}{4}$, which drops below
zero exactly at $p=1/3$: the same threshold where
$C(p)=\max(0,(3p-1)/2)$ switches on. Two independent tests, one derived
from an eigenvalue formula and one from a transpose, draw the
entangled/separable boundary at the identical point. (For this family
the match runs deeper: the negativity, defined as the absolute sum of
$\rho^{T_B}$'s negative eigenvalues, is $(3p-1)/4=0.275$ at $p=0.7$,
half the concurrence $0.55$.)

**Bound entanglement: where PPT stops being the whole story.** The
two-qubit completeness above does not survive in higher dimensions. For
$3\times3$ and $2\times4$ systems, P. Horodecki constructed states that
are entangled yet have positive partial transpose (Phys. Lett. A 232,
333 (1997)), so there PPT remains a one-way test. Such PPT-entangled
states turn out to be *bound entangled*: no amount of
<Term id="locc">local operations and classical communication</Term> can
distill their entanglement back into usable Bell pairs (M., P., and R.
Horodecki, Phys. Rev. Lett. 80, 5239 (1998)). Entanglement you can prove
is present but can never cash out is
a phenomenon with no two-qubit analogue, and it is one reason mixed-state
entanglement theory is still an active research area rather than a closed
chapter.

## Two Questions That Come Apart for Mixed States

<InsightBlock>
  For a pure state, "how mixed is the part I hold?" and "how entangled is
  the whole?" are the same question. For a mixed state they come apart,
  and every difficulty in this lesson traces back to that one split.
</InsightBlock>

Relative entropy quantifies how costly it is to mistake $\sigma$ for the
true state $\rho$, and Klein's inequality is the statement that this cost
is never negative: no wrong model pays.

The concurrence story is harder for a reason worth naming precisely. A
mixed $\rho$ carries two kinds of uncertainty at once, quantum
correlation and classical ignorance about which pure state was prepared,
and entropy of the reduced state cannot tell them apart. A separable
mixture of product states has plenty of reduced-state entropy and no
entanglement at all. The convex roof behind the Wootters formula is what
performs the separation: it minimizes average pure-state entanglement
over *every* decomposition of $\rho$, so any entanglement the number
reports is entanglement no choice of preparation could have explained
away as classical mixing. That minimization is why the general formula
was hard to find and why its equality with entanglement of formation is a
theorem rather than a definition.

## Assuming the Two-Qubit Shortcut Always Applies

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming $R=\rho\tilde\rho$ is Hermitian in general, and that
`eigenvaluesHermitian2x2` therefore always applies.** It happened to be
block-Hermitian for this lesson's Werner-state example, a structural
consequence of that state's high symmetry, not a general fact.
Concurrence: A Two-Qubit Entanglement Measure's stated scope limitation
(no general non-Hermitian eigensolver on this platform) still applies to
an arbitrary mixed two-qubit state.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Forgetting the $\max(0,\cdot)$ clipping in the concurrence formula.**
Without it, $\sqrt{\mu_1}-\sqrt{\mu_2}-\sqrt{\mu_3}-\sqrt{\mu_4}$ can come
out negative (it does for any Werner state with $p<1/3$): the clipping
is not a numerical-safety afterthought; it's the formula correctly
reporting zero entanglement for a state that really is separable.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Verify $S(\rho\|\rho)=0$ directly from the definition, for any valid
   density matrix $\rho$.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Substituting $\sigma=\rho$ into the definition makes the two terms
   literally the same expression:

   $$
   S(\rho\|\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho)=0.
   $$

   No spectral decomposition is needed, but it is worth doing one anyway to
   see that nothing is being swept under the rug in the singular case. With
   $\rho=\sum_ip_i|i\rangle\langle i|$ the overlap matrix is
   $c_{ij}=|\langle i|j\rangle|^2=\delta_{ij}$, so the proof's general
   expression collapses to $\sum_ip_i(\log_2p_i-\log_2p_i)=0$, term by
   term. Any $p_i=0$ contributes $0\log_20=0$ under the standard
   convention, which is not an evasion: the limit
   $x\log_2 x\to0$ as $x\to0^+$ makes it the only continuous choice. And
   the finiteness hypothesis $\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\sigma)$
   is satisfied with room to spare, since the two supports are identical.

   The result is the "if" half of Klein's equality condition, and it is the
   half that gives relative entropy its meaning: the quantity measures the
   cost of using $\sigma$ as a model when the truth is $\rho$, and a
   correct model costs nothing.

   What this emphatically does not make relative entropy is a distance. It
   fails symmetry outright. With $\rho=\mathrm{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ and
   $\sigma=I/2$ from the worked example,

   $$
   S(\rho\|\sigma)=0.531\ \text{bits},\qquad S(\sigma\|\rho)=0.737\ \text{bits},
   $$

   two different numbers for the same pair. It fails the triangle
   inequality too, and it can be $+\infty$ while the two states are
   perfectly ordinary. $S(\rho\|\rho)=0$ is one metric axiom holding by
   accident, not evidence of the others.

   </details>

2. Using Klein's inequality's equality condition, explain why
   $S(\rho\|\sigma)=0$ is only possible when $\rho=\sigma$ exactly, not
   merely when they have the same eigenvalues.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the eigenvectors enter the proof through $c_{ij}=|\langle i|j\rangle|^2$,
   and equality demands that this matrix be a permutation, which is a
   statement about *bases*, not spectra.

   Follow the two Jensen steps backwards. The second one,
   $D_{cl}(p\|r)\geq0$, saturates only when $r_i=p_i$ for every $i$. The
   first one, $\sum_jc_{ij}\log_2q_j\leq\log_2\sum_jc_{ij}q_j$, uses the
   *strict* concavity of $\log_2$, so it saturates only when the $q_j$
   carrying nonzero weight $c_{ij}$ are all equal for each fixed $i$. Put
   together with double stochasticity, that forces each row of $c$ to be
   supported where $\sigma$'s eigenvalue is constant, which makes $c$ a
   permutation (up to relabelling inside degenerate eigenspaces). A
   permutation $c$ says $\rho$'s eigenvectors are $\sigma$'s eigenvectors,
   and $r_i=p_i$ then says the matched eigenvalues agree. Together:
   $\rho=\sigma$.

   So sharing a spectrum is not enough, and the gap is not small. Take
   $\rho=\mathrm{diag}(0.9,0.1)$ in the $z$ basis and
   $\sigma=0.9|{+}\rangle\langle{+}|+0.1|{-}\rangle\langle{-}|$, the *same*
   eigenvalues rotated into the $x$ basis. Both have von Neumann entropy
   $0.469$ bits, so every spectrum-only quantity agrees. But
   $\langle{+}|\rho|{+}\rangle=\langle{-}|\rho|{-}\rangle=\tfrac12$, so

   $$
   \mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log_2\sigma)=\tfrac12\log_2 0.9+\tfrac12\log_2 0.1=-1.737,
   $$

   $$
   S(\rho\|\sigma)=-0.469-(-1.737)=1.268\ \text{bits},
   $$

   nearly two and a half times the divergence from $\rho$ to the
   maximally mixed state. Identical spectra, wildly different states.

   Where the proof felt it: here $c_{ij}=\tfrac12$ for all four entries,
   about as far from a permutation matrix as a $2\times2$ doubly stochastic
   matrix can get, so the first Jensen step is maximally slack. That is the
   general lesson worth carrying: $c_{ij}$ is the only place the two
   eigenbases meet, and it is exactly the term that vanishes from the
   argument when $\rho$ and $\sigma$ commute. Relative entropy is sensitive
   to the mismatch between eigenbases, which is precisely the sensitivity
   that makes it, and not the entropy difference, the right measure of how
   wrong a model is.

   </details>

3. At exactly $p=1/3$, confirm $C(p)=0$ using the closed form
   $C(p)=\max(0,(3p-1)/2)$, and explain physically why this is the exact
   threshold where the Werner state stops being entangled.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $C(1/3)=\max\big(0,(3\cdot\tfrac13-1)/2\big)=\max(0,0)=0$, the one point
   where the clipped and unclipped expressions meet.

   The physical reason is best seen by rewriting the Werner state in the
   Bell basis. Since $I/4$ is an equal mixture of the four Bell
   projectors,

   $$
   \rho_W(p)=\underbrace{\left(p+\frac{1-p}{4}\right)}_{\textstyle F=\frac{1+3p}{4}}|\Psi^-\rangle\langle\Psi^-|
   +\frac{1-p}{4}\Big(|\Psi^+\rangle\langle\Psi^+|+|\Phi^+\rangle\langle\Phi^+|+|\Phi^-\rangle\langle\Phi^-|\Big),
   $$

   so the state is completely described by one number: $F$, its overlap
   with the singlet, with the remaining weight spread evenly over the other
   three Bell states. At $p=1/3$,

   $$
   F=\frac{1+3(1/3)}{4}=\frac12.
   $$

   The threshold is exactly **singlet fidelity one half**, and $\tfrac12$
   is the ceiling any separable state can reach against a maximally
   entangled target. So $F>\tfrac12$ certifies entanglement for any
   two-qubit state, which settles the $p>1/3$ side immediately. The
   converse direction is not true in general, but it is true within this
   family: for a Bell-diagonal state, which is what $\rho_W$ is, the
   largest Bell weight is the whole story, and $F\leq\tfrac12$ leaves the
   singlet no longer dominant enough to survive. The Horodecki converse
   cited in this lesson then guarantees an explicit decomposition of
   $\rho_W$ into product states exists at $p\leq1/3$, since the partial
   transpose is positive there.

   The partial-transpose route reaches the same point from a different
   direction, which is the cross-check worth having. The only eigenvalue of
   $\rho_W^{T_B}$ that can go negative is $(1-3p)/4$, and it changes sign
   at $p=1/3$. Two tests, one built from an eigenvalue formula and one from
   a transpose, agree on the boundary to the digit.

   One thing $p>1/3$ does *not* buy is Bell violation, and this is where
   Werner states earn their name. The CHSH value for $\rho_W$ is
   $2\sqrt2\,p$, which exceeds $2$ only for $p>1/\sqrt2\approx0.707$. The
   lesson's own worked example sits at $p=0.7$: concurrence $0.55$, so
   definitely entangled, yet CHSH value $2\sqrt2(0.7)=1.980<2$, so no
   violation at all. Werner's 1989 point was exactly this, that
   entanglement and Bell nonlocality are different properties and the first
   does not imply the second for mixed states.

   </details>

4. Explain why the pure-state reduction ($R$ rank 1, three of its four
   eigenvalues exactly zero) makes the $\max(0,\cdot)$ clipping
   automatically inactive for every pure state, i.e. why a pure state's
   concurrence is never "clipped."

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the clipping only ever matters when the three subtracted roots
   are competing with the first, and for a pure state they are all
   identically zero.

   The rank-1 structure does it in one line. For $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$,

   $$
   R=\rho\tilde\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|=\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle\;|\psi\rangle\langle\tilde\psi|,
   $$

   an outer product times a scalar, so $\mathrm{rank}(R)\leq1$ and its
   spectrum is $\{\mu_1,0,0,0\}$ with $\mu_1=|\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle|^2$.
   The raw expression is therefore

   $$
   \sqrt{\mu_1}-\sqrt{0}-\sqrt{0}-\sqrt{0}=\sqrt{\mu_1}=|\langle\psi|\tilde\psi\rangle|\geq0,
   $$

   non-negative before the $\max$ is applied. The clipping has nothing to
   remove, and $C=2|ad-bc|$ follows.

   The structural reason behind the algebra is the one to remember. The
   concurrence formula is the closed-form solution of a convex-roof
   minimization over all pure-state decompositions of $\rho$, and the three
   subtracted roots are what that optimization charges for having several
   pure components that partially cancel. A pure state has exactly one
   component, so there is nothing to cancel against and no penalty to
   assess. The clipping is a genuinely mixed-state phenomenon; it fires for
   a Werner state below $p=1/3$ precisely because the classical mixture in
   it can be redistributed into an all-product decomposition, and there is
   no such redistribution available to a pure state.

   A precision worth keeping. "Never clipped" is not "never zero". A pure
   product state such as $|00\rangle$ has $ad-bc=0$, hence $\mu_1=0$, and
   $C=0$ arrives honestly as $\sqrt{\mu_1}$ rather than through the
   $\max$. What cannot happen for a pure state is the situation the
   clipping exists for: the unclipped quantity coming out strictly
   negative, which would be a meaningless amount of entanglement.

   </details>

## Summary

- Quantum relative entropy $S(\rho\|\sigma)=\text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\rho)-\text{Tr}(\rho\log_2\sigma)$
  is non-negative (Klein's inequality), proved here via two applications
  of Jensen's inequality reducing to a classical KL-divergence bound.
- The general Wootters concurrence $C(\rho)=\max(0,\sqrt{\mu_1}-\sqrt{\mu_2}-\sqrt{\mu_3}-\sqrt{\mu_4})$,
  built from $R=\rho\tilde\rho$, is proved to reduce exactly to the
  pure-state formula $2|ad-bc|$ when $\rho$ is pure.
- Computed on an actually mixed Werner state ($p=0.7$, purity
  $\approx0.6175$): $C\approx0.55$, matching the known closed form
  $(3p-1)/2$ exactly, using this platform's *existing* Hermitian
  eigensolver on a structurally special (block-Hermitian) case, closing
  the exact gap Concurrence: A Two-Qubit Entanglement Measure named and
  left open.
