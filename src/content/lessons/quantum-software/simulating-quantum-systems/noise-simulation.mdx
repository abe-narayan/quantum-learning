## Predicting What a Real Device Will Say

A noise simulator predicts what an imperfect physical device will output
before any scarce hardware time gets spent. This lesson builds and
runs one directly, reusing Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics'
Kraus-channel machinery unchanged: ideal gate evolution and a
noise channel, alternating after every gate.

## Interleaving Gates With Channels

### Interleaving ideal evolution with a noise channel

A noisy circuit simulator alternates two steps for every gate: (1) apply
the gate's IDEAL <Term id="unitary-evolution">unitary evolution</Term> to a density matrix
(`evolveDensityMatrix`, $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$), then (2) apply a
<Term id="noise-decoherence">noise channel</Term> (Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics' `applyKrausChannel`)
modeling whatever physical error process (Noise, Decoherence & Scaling's
T1/T2, or others) affects the real device between operations. This
platform's `runNoisyCircuit` implements this two-step loop,
literally two function calls per gate, both already built and tested
elsewhere.

### A concrete demonstration

$H$ then $H$ again is, ideally, the identity ($HH=I$): applied to
$|0\rangle$, it should return cleanly to $|0\rangle$. This platform's
engine confirms it with a trivial (identity) noise channel: `purity=1`,
`P(0)=1`, no deviation at all.

Now interleave a real dephasing channel ($\lambda=0.2$) after each of
those two $H$ gates. Both gates are identical, and both are followed by
the identical noise channel, so the natural expectation is that each one
costs the same amount of purity.

### An honest scope limitation

This platform's `runNoisyCircuit` is deliberately scoped to
single-qubit circuits. Extending it correctly to multiple qubits
requires expanding each gate into a full $2^n\times2^n$ unitary (via
<Term id="tensor-product">tensor products</Term> with identity on every untouched qubit) before applying
it to a multi-qubit density matrix. This is well-understood
machinery, just not needed by anything this course's lessons
demonstrate, an explicit, stated scope choice, not a hidden limitation.

## How Production Noise Models Work

This is how real noisy-simulator software (Qiskit Aer's noise models,
for instance) works at a conceptual level:
combine an ideal circuit description with a noise model, and predict
what the actual (imperfect) output distribution will look like, letting
researchers estimate real-hardware performance without consuming real,
scarce hardware time (Programming Quantum Computers' queue-time
discussion).

## Watching Fidelity Fall, Unevenly

**Does purity decrease monotonically with every additional noisy gate,
or can it stay flat for some steps?**

This platform's own test suite shows purity after 1 noisy $H$ gate
($\approx0.82$) equals purity after 2 noisy $H$ gates (also
$\approx0.82$), so purity does not strictly decrease at every step. But
purity after 3 gates ($\approx0.70$) is strictly less than after 1. The
mechanism is $H$'s specific relationship to the dephasing channel's
axis. Dephasing shrinks the Bloch vector's $x$ and $y$ components and
leaves $z$ alone, and $H$ swaps $x$ with $z$. So the vector alternates:
after gate 1 it lies along $x$, where the channel shortens it; after
gate 2 it lies along $z$, where the channel has nothing to grip; after
gate 3 it is back on $x$ and loses length again. Noise accumulation
isn't monotonic step by step, even though it trends downward overall.

One question the worked example leaves open: is this behavior specific
to dephasing, or would an energy-loss channel act the same way? The
Noise & Decoherence Explorer below exists for this kind of
qualitative question. It applies a channel repeatedly to a fixed state,
with no gates interleaved, so its numbers won't reproduce this lesson's
$\approx0.82$ and $\approx0.70$; treat it as a map of what each channel
type does, and to which states it does it. Start from $|0\rangle$ with
phase scrambling (T2) selected and nothing decays at all: a
computational-basis state has no coherence for dephasing to destroy,
which is why this lesson's demonstration needed its $H$ gates
first, to rotate into a state the noise could act on. Then switch to
energy loss (T1) starting from $|1\rangle$ and watch the same state
decay immediately.

## Assuming Every Gate Degrades the State

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming purity must strictly decrease after every single noisy gate.**
The worked example shows this is FALSE in general. The specific gate
sequence and its relationship to the noise channel's structure matters,
not just the gate count.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming this platform's single-qubit scope is a permanent, general
limitation of noise simulation.** It's a deliberate, stated scope choice
for *this* platform's lessons. Real noise simulators (and a correctly
generalized version of this engine) do handle multi-qubit circuits, via
the tensor-product gate expansion this lesson names but doesn't build.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Describe the two-step loop `runNoisyCircuit` performs for each gate
   in a circuit.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   For every gate in the list, in order:

   **Step 1, ideal unitary evolution.** `evolveDensityMatrix(rho, gate)`
   applies $\rho\to U\rho U^\dagger$. This is the gate exactly as a
   noiseless simulator would apply it, with nothing degraded.

   **Step 2, the noise channel.** `applyKrausChannel(rho, noiseChannel)`
   applies $\rho\to\sum_i K_i\rho K_i^\dagger$, modelling whatever
   physical error the real device accumulates around that operation.

   Then move to the next gate and do both again. That is the entire
   function: two calls per gate, both to machinery built and tested in
   earlier courses, with no new physics anywhere in it.

   The structural point worth extracting is *why the state is a density
   matrix at all*. Every other simulator in this course carries a
   `StateVector`. This one carries a `Matrix`, and it has to, because a
   noise channel maps a pure state to a mixed one and a state vector
   cannot represent a mixed state. There is no amplitude array
   corresponding to "the qubit is $|0\rangle$ half the time and
   $|1\rangle$ half the time, incoherently." So the switch from
   $|\psi\rangle$ to $\rho$ is forced by the presence of noise rather
   than chosen for convenience, and it is what the whole density-matrix
   formalism exists for.

   Two details in the ordering are worth noticing.

   The noise comes *after* the gate, modelling error accrued during and
   around that operation. Nothing forbids a different placement, and real
   noise models often attach different channels to different gate types
   or add idle-time noise to qubits doing nothing. The interleaving
   pattern is the general shape; where exactly each channel sits is part
   of the model.

   And the ideal step remains exactly ideal. The gate is not "applied
   noisily"; a perfect gate is applied and then a separate channel
   degrades the result. That factorization is what makes the noise model
   a swappable component, which is exactly how production tools such as
   Qiskit Aer are built.

   The wrong turn is expecting a noisy simulator to need a different
   physics engine, some approximate gate application that bakes error in.
   It needs a different *state representation* and one extra function
   call per gate.

   </details>

2. Using the worked example's numbers, is "more gates always means lower
   purity" a safe assumption? Justify using the specific 1-vs-2-gate
   result.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **No.** Purity after one noisy $H$ is $0.820000$, and purity after two
   is $0.820000$: identical, to every digit the engine prints. The second
   noisy gate cost nothing.

   **The mechanism is geometric.** Track the Bloch vector, and recall
   that for one qubit purity is $\tfrac12(1+r^2)$ where $r$ is the
   vector's length.

   Start at the north pole, $z=1$, $r=1$, purity 1. The first $H$ swings
   the vector onto the $x$ axis. Dephasing shrinks $x$ and $y$ and leaves
   $z$ untouched, so it finds the vector lying entirely in its own
   contraction plane and shortens it to $x=0.800$, giving
   $\tfrac12(1+0.64)=0.82$.

   The second $H$ swaps $x$ with $z$, putting the vector along the $z$
   axis at $z=0.800$. Dephasing now has nothing to grip: $x$ and $y$ are
   already zero, and $z$ is exactly what it does not touch. Length
   unchanged, purity unchanged at 0.82.

   The third $H$ swings it back onto $x$, dephasing bites again,
   $x=0.640$, and purity falls to $\tfrac12(1+0.4096)=0.7048$.

   The engine's staircase, computed live: purity runs
   $1$, $0.8200$, $0.8200$, $0.7048$, $0.7048$, $0.6311$, $0.6311$ across zero
   through six gates. Down on odd gates, flat on even ones. The trend is
   downward and the step-by-step claim is false.

   **And the failure is worse than "sometimes flat."** Swap dephasing for
   amplitude damping at the same strength and purity after one, two and
   three noisy $H$ gates comes out $0.920000$, $0.935108$, $0.894148$. It
   *rose* at the second gate. Nothing is wrong: amplitude damping's fixed
   point is the pure state $|0\rangle$, so it contracts the Bloch ball
   toward a point on its surface, and a state being dragged toward a pure
   state can become purer. A channel that loses information can still
   increase purity, because purity measures how close the state is to
   *some* pure state, not how much information survives.

   The wrong turn is treating purity as a running total of damage, a
   quantity that only goes one way, like entropy in a closed system. It
   is a property of the current state and nothing else. What actually
   accumulates is the channel's effect on the *information*, and purity
   is an imperfect proxy for that: informative when the geometry
   cooperates, misleading when it does not.

   Practically, the lesson is to be careful with per-step fidelity or
   purity checks as a debugging signal. A step that costs nothing may
   only mean the state was pointing somewhere the channel could not
   reach, on that particular step.

   </details>

3. Why is `runNoisyCircuit` scoped to single-qubit circuits specifically,
   and what would extending it to multiple qubits require?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because nothing in this course needs the multi-qubit version, and
   building it would mean adding machinery no lesson exercises. It is a
   stated scope decision, not a limitation of the formalism.

   **What extending it requires:** each single-qubit gate has to be
   expanded into a full $2^n\times2^n$ unitary, by taking tensor products
   with the identity on every untouched qubit, before it can act on a
   multi-qubit density matrix. The single-qubit Kraus operators need the
   same lifting, so that a channel modelling noise on one qubit acts as
   the identity on the others. Both are standard, well-understood
   constructions.

   Three tempting explanations for the scope are wrong, and each is worth
   dismissing individually because each contains a true statement pointed
   at the wrong target.

   **"Kraus channels are defined for a single system."** They are not.
   The formalism is dimension-agnostic: a channel on any finite
   dimensional system is $\rho\to\sum_i K_i\rho K_i^\dagger$ with
   $\sum_i K_i^\dagger K_i=I$, and nothing in it mentions qubit count.

   **"A multi-qubit density matrix has $4^n$ entries, so even two qubits
   is impractical."** Two qubits is $4^2=16$ complex numbers, 256 bytes.
   Nothing about that is impractical. The cost argument is real but bites
   far later: $4^{20}\approx1.1\times10^{12}$ entries, about 17.6 TB, and
   notice that this is the same figure as a *40-qubit* state vector.
   That equivalence is the useful way to hold it: a density matrix on $n$
   qubits costs what a state vector on $2n$ qubits costs, so noisy
   simulation halves your reachable qubit count.

   **"Correlated noise across qubits has no agreed model."** Correlated
   noise models are standard, and crosstalk in particular is very well
   characterized on real devices. This turns an unbuilt feature into an
   unsettled research question, which it is not.

   The distinction the lesson is drawing, and the reason it says so
   explicitly, is between something that *cannot* be done and something
   that *has not been done here*. A lesson that names exactly what the
   extension would require is describing the second.

   </details>

4. Explain why a noisy simulator is valuable even before running
   anything on real hardware, connecting to Programming Quantum
   Computers' queue-time discussion.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because hardware time is the scarce resource, and a noisy simulator
   answers most of the questions you would otherwise spend it on.

   **Queue time is the direct link.** A device is one machine in one
   building, shared, and a submitted job waits. A design loop that
   iterates against a queue proceeds in days. The same loop against a
   simulator proceeds in seconds, and the whole point of predicting the
   noisy output first is to spend the answer rather than the fridge time
   on finding out.

   Four things a noisy simulator does that neither an ideal simulator nor
   hardware can.

   **It separates the two error sources.** An ideal simulator tells you
   whether the circuit is logically right. Hardware mixes logic errors
   with physical ones and labels nothing. A noisy simulator lets you turn
   the noise model on and off around the same circuit, so a difference is
   attributable by construction.

   **It answers "is this worth submitting?"** Given a device's published
   error rates, the simulator estimates the output distribution before
   any shots are bought. If the predicted signal is already buried,
   nothing is learned by queueing for it.

   **It distinguishes circuits an ideal simulator cannot.** Two
   transpilations of the same logical circuit are identical under
   noiseless simulation, by construction. Under a noise model the shorter
   or better-routed one wins measurably, which is what makes the
   Compilation course's optimizations checkable at all.

   **It answers counterfactuals no device can.** What if $T_1$ were twice
   as long? What if the dominant channel were dephasing rather than
   damping? You cannot dial a real device's noise, and these are exactly
   the questions that decide where engineering effort should go. The same
   ability is what makes zero-noise extrapolation designable, since that
   protocol needs to know how an observable behaves as noise strength is
   varied.

   Two honest limits, so this does not read as a claim that hardware is
   optional.

   A noise model is a model. Real devices have noise that drifts between
   calibrations, that is correlated across qubits, and that is not
   Markovian, and an interleaved single-qubit channel captures none of
   that. Agreement between a simulator and a device is evidence the model
   is adequate for that circuit, not proof it is right.

   And the simulation is more expensive than the ideal one. A density
   matrix costs $4^n$ rather than $2^n$, so switching noise on halves the
   qubit count you can reach. Noisy simulation is a tool for small
   circuits, which is precisely the regime where you are still deciding
   whether the large one is worth running.

   </details>

## Summary

- A noisy circuit simulator interleaves ideal unitary evolution with a
  Kraus-channel noise model after every gate. `runNoisyCircuit`
  implements this directly, reusing `evolveDensityMatrix` and
  `applyKrausChannel` unchanged.
- Demonstrated concretely: $H,H$ (ideally identity) returns exactly to
  $|0\rangle$ with no noise, but only to $P(0)\approx0.9$ with dephasing
  interleaved, a computed fidelity loss.
- Purity doesn't always decrease monotonically gate-by-gate (confirmed
  by direct computation), an honest, checked nuance, not assumed.
