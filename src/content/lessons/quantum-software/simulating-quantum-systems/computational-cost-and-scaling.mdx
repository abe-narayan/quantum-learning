## Feynman's Objection, Costed

In 1981, at a keynote co-organized by MIT and IBM, Feynman put his
finger on the reason a quantum computer might be worth building at all.
His diagnosis: simulating an $n$-particle quantum system on an ordinary
computer seems to require resources that grow like $2^n$, no matter how
clever the algorithm, because the system's state has that many
independent numbers in it. His proposed fix was blunt: "nature isn't
classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature,
you'd better make it quantum mechanical." This lesson is the other side
of that same coin: it puts exact figures on the classical price tag
Feynman was pointing at, using this platform's own engine as the
receipts.

State-Vector Simulation named the technique this course has used since

## Memory and Time, Both Exponential

### Memory cost

Each complex <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> is two double-precision floats (real and
imaginary), 8 bytes each: 16 bytes per amplitude. Total memory:
$16\times2^n$ bytes.

Production simulators often let you drop to single precision, storing
each amplitude as two 32-bit floats for 8 bytes instead of 16. It is
worth knowing what that buys, because the answer is the whole lesson in
one line: $8\times2^{n+1}=16\times2^n$, so halving the bytes per
amplitude buys **exactly one more qubit**. Every figure below shifts one
column and nothing else changes.

### Time cost per gate, and per circuit

Every gate application (Circuit Representation in Code's
`applySingleQubitGate`/`applyCNOT`) touches every amplitude, an $O(2^n)$
operation. A full circuit with `numGates` sequential gates costs roughly
$\text{numGates}\times2^n$ total floating-point operations, computed
directly by this platform's `estimatedGateFlops`. Combined with
Scaling Challenges' compounding-fidelity-error result, this is a second,
independent reason deep circuits are expensive: not just accumulating
physical error on real hardware, but accumulating classical simulation
cost too, if simulating instead of running on hardware.

### Where the wall bites

State-vector simulation is practical up to roughly 30-40 qubits on
serious classical hardware (well beyond a laptop, but within reach of
research computing clusters), and essentially impossible beyond about
50, for any currently existing or plausible near-future classical
computer. This concrete range is why <Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum advantage</Term> experiments
specifically target circuits in or beyond this regime: it's the qubit
count at which *no* classical simulation technique (including the more
sophisticated approaches the rest of this course covers) can
straightforwardly keep up.

The table's bottom row is what makes "essentially impossible" the right
phrase rather than a rhetorical one. 18 PB is not a figure anyone
reaches by fitting more memory to an existing machine. Through the
mid-2020s the largest supercomputers carried total memory measured in
single-digit petabytes, so a 50-qubit state vector exceeds the whole
memory of any one of them, and it buys one state vector with no gate
applied to it yet.

## A Wall No Implementation Can Move

This cost is not a limitation of any particular implementation choice.
It's a direct consequence of quantum mechanics' own structure: an
$n$-qubit state has $2^n$ independent complex degrees of freedom
(Mathematical Foundations' tensor-product dimension counting), and
representing that exactly requires storing all of them. This is, in one
sense, why quantum computers are potentially powerful at all: classical
memory cannot compress this information away for a generic state, the
structural point Feynman was making in 1981, well before there was
hardware capable of testing it directly.

<ResearchConnection title="Choosing a qubit count to sit past the wall" source="Arute et al., Nature 574, 505-510 (2019)" url="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1666-5">
Google's 2019 Sycamore experiment used 53 qubits specifically: that
qubit count was chosen to sit just past what any existing supercomputer
could feasibly state-vector-simulate directly, a concrete instance of
the wall this lesson computes. IBM subsequently argued a cleverer
classical technique (not brute-force state-vector storage) could
approximate the same task using disk space instead of RAM, a real
dispute that illustrates the point above from the other side: pushing
past this specific wall means abandoning exact state-vector simulation
entirely, not just buying a bigger machine.
</ResearchConnection>

## Costing 500 Gates on Thirty-Five Qubits

**A circuit runs 500 gates on 35 qubits. Roughly how many floating-point
operations does exact state-vector simulation require?**

$$
\text{estimatedGateFlops}(35, 500) = 500\times2^{35} \approx 500\times3.44\times10^{10} \approx1.72\times10^{13}
$$

About 17 trillion operations. On hardware performing $\sim10^{12}$
operations per second (a fast modern system), this circuit alone would
take roughly 17 seconds, for JUST 35 qubits and a modest 500 gates,
illustrating how quickly even "reasonable-sounding" circuit sizes push
into serious computational cost.

## Expecting Memory and Time to Fail at Different Sizes

<Callout type="note">
**Treating memory and time cost as separate concerns with different
qubit-count thresholds.** Both scale as $2^n$ from the SAME underlying
fact (every gate touches every amplitude). They cross into
"impractical" territory together, not independently.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming better classical hardware (more RAM, faster CPUs) meaningfully
pushes back the qubit-count wall.** Because the cost is exponential, a
10× hardware improvement buys only $\log_2(10)\approx3.3$ additional
qubits, a real but modest gain against exponential growth, not a
solution to it.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Compute the exact memory (in bytes) for a 25-qubit state-vector
   simulation. Then write that figure as a power of two, and say why the
   same number can be quoted as both 512 MiB and 536.9 MB.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   16\times2^{25} = 16\times33{,}554{,}432 = 536{,}870{,}912 \text{ bytes}.
   $$

   `stateVectorMemoryBytes(25)` returns exactly 536,870,912. That is
   $2^{29}$ bytes, or 512 MiB exactly, which is a nice sanity check in
   itself: $16=2^4$, so $16\times2^{25}=2^{29}$, and every entry in this
   table is an exact power of two.

   Where the 16 comes from: one complex amplitude is two
   double-precision floats, real and imaginary, at 8 bytes each. Nothing
   is stored per qubit; everything is stored per basis state, and there
   are $2^{25}=33{,}554{,}432$ of those.

   Note that this is comfortable. Half a gigabyte for 25 qubits sits well
   inside a laptop, and it is easy to conclude from that that 25 qubits
   is a modest problem. The relevant comparison is what the next few
   qubits cost: 30 qubits is 17.2 GB, and 40 is 17.6 TB. Fifteen more
   qubits than this question's, and the answer has moved from "fits in
   RAM without thinking about it" to "needs a serious cluster."

   The wrong turn is a units one, and it is easy to make in either
   direction. The formula gives *bytes*. Reporting 33.5 million because
   you stopped at the amplitude count is off by the factor of 16, and
   reporting 536.9 MB rather than 512 MiB is not wrong but is a different
   convention: decimal megabytes and binary mebibytes differ by about
   5%, which is worth naming explicitly whenever these figures are
   compared against a machine's advertised memory.

   </details>

2. Using the worked example's methodology, estimate the total
   floating-point operations for a 1000-gate circuit on 30 qubits. Set it
   against the worked example itself (500 gates on 35 qubits) and say
   which of the two inputs, gate count or qubit count, accounts for most
   of the difference.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \text{numGates}\times2^{n} = 1000\times2^{30} = 1000\times1{,}073{,}741{,}824 \approx 1.07\times10^{12}.
   $$

   `estimatedGateFlops(30, 1000)` returns 1,073,741,824,000, about a
   trillion floating-point operations.

   At roughly $10^{12}$ operations per second on a fast modern system,
   that is on the order of a second of compute. Compare the lesson's
   worked example, 500 gates on 35 qubits, which came to
   $1.72\times10^{13}$ and roughly 17 seconds. Double the gates and five
   fewer qubits, and the cost still dropped by a factor of sixteen: the
   extra gates multiplied the work by two, and the five fewer qubits
   divided it by $2^5=32$.

   That asymmetry is the thing to take away. The circuit's *depth* enters
   linearly, so doubling the gate count doubles the work. The *width*
   enters exponentially, so five more qubits multiplies it by 32. A
   simulation that is uncomfortably slow can be rescued by shortening
   the circuit and cannot be rescued by anything at all if the problem
   is the qubit count.

   The reason a single-qubit gate costs $O(2^n)$ rather than $O(1)$ is
   worth restating, since it is the origin of the whole cost model.
   `applySingleQubitGate` pairs up every amplitude with its
   bit-flipped partner and rewrites both, so it reads and writes the
   entire array. Nothing in the array announces which entries happen to
   be zero, which is exactly what the lesson's worked 3-qubit Hadamard
   showed: one gate on one qubit, and all eight slots were touched.

   The wrong turn is treating this as a running-time upper bound to be
   compared against a real profile. It is a leading-order operation
   count, and a real simulator's wall-clock time is usually dominated by
   memory bandwidth rather than arithmetic, since it is streaming
   gigabytes through a pipeline that does two multiplies per element. The
   scaling is right; the constant is not something to bet on.

   </details>

3. If a research group upgrades from a system that can simulate 40
   qubits to one with 1000× more memory, roughly how many additional
   qubits can they now simulate? Reason the way the cautions above do.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **About nine or ten.** Nine for certain.

   Each extra qubit doubles the memory requirement, so a factor of $M$ in
   memory buys $\log_2 M$ qubits, and

   $$
   \log_2 1000 \approx 9.97 .
   $$

   Checking against the engine rather than the logarithm: 40 qubits needs
   17.6 TB, and 49 qubits needs exactly $2^9=512$ times that, which fits
   inside a 1000-fold budget with room to spare. Fifty qubits needs
   $2^{10}=1024$ times, just past it. So nine more qubits is comfortable,
   the tenth is a near miss, and 49 to 50 is the honest answer.

   That is the caution's arithmetic run at a larger scale. The lesson
   notes a 10× hardware improvement buys $\log_2 10\approx3.3$ qubits.
   Multiplying the improvement by a hundred, from 10× to 1000×, only
   triples the number of qubits gained, because the buyer is on the
   wrong side of an exponential. The relationship between resources and
   capability is logarithmic, and logarithms are unimpressed by large
   inputs.

   Put the result next to what it took. A thousandfold memory increase is
   not an upgrade cycle; it is the difference between a workstation and
   one of the largest machines in the world. The prize is nine qubits,
   landing at 49, still below Google's 53-qubit Sycamore experiment,
   which was chosen at that size precisely to sit past this wall.

   The wrong turn is to conclude the wall is therefore a hardware-funding
   problem, solvable by a sufficiently determined institution. Follow the
   arithmetic one step further: another thousandfold on top of the first
   buys nine more, reaching 58. Every additional qubit costs as much as
   everything spent so far. Getting meaningfully past this point means
   abandoning exact state-vector simulation for something that exploits
   structure, which is what Tensor Network Methods does and what IBM's
   response to the Sycamore claim did.

   </details>

4. Explain, using the tensor-product dimension-counting idea from
   Mathematical Foundations, why $2^n$ growth is a fact about quantum
   mechanics itself, not an artifact of how this platform's engine
   happens to be implemented. Then say what that same fact implies about
   why a quantum computer might beat a classical one.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because $2^n$ is the *dimension of the state space*, and dimension is a
   property of the physics, not of any program.

   Mathematical Foundations' counting rule: combining two systems takes
   the tensor product of their state spaces, and tensor products
   multiply dimensions. One qubit lives in a two-dimensional complex
   space. Two qubits live in $\mathbb{C}^2\otimes\mathbb{C}^2$, dimension
   $2\times2=4$. Add another and it is 8. After $n$ qubits the dimension
   is $2^n$, and a basis for a space of that dimension has $2^n$ vectors,
   so a general state needs $2^n$ complex coefficients to specify it.

   The load-bearing word is **general**. A basis has $2^n$ elements, and a
   generic vector has a nonzero component along every one of them. There
   is no smaller set of numbers that determines an arbitrary state,
   because if there were, the space would have had a smaller dimension.
   That is what dimension means.

   So the storage cost is not a choice this engine made. Any exact
   representation of an arbitrary $n$-qubit state, in any language, on
   any machine, using any clever data structure, must be able to
   distinguish $2^n$ independent complex directions. Compression works
   only where a state is not generic, which is a real and important
   escape route and precisely the one Tensor Network Methods takes. It is
   not available in general.

   The physical significance runs the other way round, and this is the
   part worth carrying. That the information *cannot* be compressed away
   for a generic state is exactly why a quantum computer might be able to
   do something a classical one cannot. If $n$ qubits could always be
   described by, say, $n^2$ classical numbers, a classical machine would
   simply track those numbers and there would be no advantage to chase.
   The cost of simulation and the possibility of advantage are two
   readings of one fact, which is what Feynman was pointing at in 1981,
   before any hardware existed to test it.

   Two wrong turns.

   The first is expecting a better data structure to help. Sparse
   storage, for instance, is a genuine optimization for states with many
   zero amplitudes, and generic states do not have many zero amplitudes.
   It saves nothing in the case that sets the wall.

   The second is confusing the count of qubits with the count of degrees
   of freedom. $n$ qubits is $n$ physical objects and $2^n$ complex
   amplitudes, and the whole subject lives in the gap between those two
   numbers. Real hardware pays the first, linearly, in control lines and
   cooling. A classical simulator pays the second.

   </details>

## Summary

- Memory cost is exactly $16\times2^n$ bytes; time cost per circuit is
  roughly $\text{numGates}\times2^n$ operations, both computed directly
  by this platform's `stateVectorMemoryBytes`/`estimatedGateFlops`.
- The practical wall sits around 30-40 qubits (serious clusters) to ~50
  qubits (beyond any existing classical system), a narrow 20-qubit span
  covering "easy" to "impossible."
- This cost is fundamental, coming from the $2^n$ complex amplitudes in
  an $n$-qubit state, the same count Feynman pointed to in 1981. Better
  classical hardware alone cannot fix it.
