## Motivation

In 1981, at a keynote co-organized by MIT and IBM, Feynman put his
finger on the reason a quantum computer might be worth building at all.
His diagnosis: simulating an $n$-particle quantum system on an ordinary
computer seems to require resources that grow like $2^n$, no matter how
clever the algorithm, because the system's state genuinely has that many
independent numbers in it. His proposed fix was blunt — "nature isn't
classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature,
you'd better make it quantum mechanical." This lesson is the other side
of that same coin: it puts exact figures on the classical price tag
Feynman was pointing at, using this platform's own engine as the
receipts.

State-Vector Simulation named the technique this course has used since

## Engineering Development

### Memory cost

Each complex <Term id="amplitude">amplitude</Term> needs two double-precision floats (real and
imaginary), 8 bytes each: 16 bytes per amplitude. Total memory:
$16\times2^n$ bytes.

### Time cost per gate, and per circuit

Every gate application (Circuit Representation in Code's
`applySingleQubitGate`/`applyCNOT`) touches every amplitude, an $O(2^n)$
operation. A full circuit with `numGates` sequential gates costs roughly
$\text{numGates}\times2^n$ total floating-point operations, computed
directly by this platform's `estimatedGateFlops`. Combined with
Scaling Challenges' compounding-fidelity-error result, this is a second,
independent reason deep circuits are expensive: not just accumulating
physical error on real hardware, but accumulating classical simulation
cost too, if simulating instead of running on hardware.

### Where the wall actually bites

State-vector simulation is practical up to roughly 30-40 qubits on
serious classical hardware (well beyond a laptop, but within reach of
research computing clusters), and essentially impossible beyond about
50, for any currently existing or plausible near-future classical
computer. This concrete range is why <Term id="quantum-advantage-supremacy">quantum advantage</Term> experiments
specifically target circuits in or beyond this regime: it's the qubit
count at which *no* classical simulation technique (including the more
sophisticated approaches the rest of this course covers) can
straightforwardly keep up.

## Physical Interpretation

This cost is not a limitation of any particular implementation choice.
It's a direct consequence of quantum mechanics' own structure: an
$n$-qubit state has $2^n$ independent complex degrees of freedom
(Mathematical Foundations' tensor-product dimension counting), and
representing that exactly requires storing all of them. This is, in one
sense, why quantum computers are potentially powerful at all: classical
memory cannot compress this information away for a generic state — the
exact structural point Feynman was making in 1981, well before there
was hardware capable of testing it directly.

<ResearchConnection title="Choosing a qubit count to sit past the wall" source="Arute et al., Nature 574, 505-510 (2019)" url="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1666-5">
Google's 2019 Sycamore experiment used 53 qubits specifically: that
qubit count was chosen to sit just past what any existing supercomputer
could feasibly state-vector-simulate directly, a concrete instance of
the wall this lesson computes. IBM subsequently argued a cleverer
classical technique (not brute-force state-vector storage) could
approximate the same task using disk space instead of RAM, a real
dispute that illustrates the point above from the other side: pushing
past this specific wall means abandoning exact state-vector simulation
entirely, not just buying a bigger machine.
</ResearchConnection>

## Worked Example

**A circuit runs 500 gates on 35 qubits. Roughly how many floating-point
operations does exact state-vector simulation require?**

$$
\text{estimatedGateFlops}(35, 500) = 500\times2^{35} \approx 500\times3.44\times10^{10} \approx1.72\times10^{13}
$$

About 17 trillion operations. On hardware performing $\sim10^{12}$
operations per second (a fast modern system), this circuit alone would
take roughly 17 seconds, for JUST 35 qubits and a modest 500 gates,
illustrating how quickly even "reasonable-sounding" circuit sizes push
into serious computational cost.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="note">
**Treating memory and time cost as separate concerns with different
qubit-count thresholds.** Both scale as $2^n$ from the SAME underlying
fact (every gate touches every amplitude). They cross into
"impractical" territory together, not independently.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming better classical hardware (more RAM, faster CPUs) meaningfully
pushes back the qubit-count wall.** Because the cost is exponential, a
10× hardware improvement buys only $\log_2(10)\approx3.3$ additional
qubits, a real but modest gain against exponential growth, not a
solution to it.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Memory cost is exactly $16\times2^n$ bytes; time cost per circuit is
  roughly $\text{numGates}\times2^n$ operations, both computed directly
  by this platform's `stateVectorMemoryBytes`/`estimatedGateFlops`.
- The practical wall sits around 30-40 qubits (serious clusters) to ~50
  qubits (beyond any existing classical system), a narrow 20-qubit span
  covering "easy" to "impossible."
- This cost is fundamental (from $2^n$ real degrees of freedom in an
  $n$-qubit state, the same count Feynman pointed to in 1981), not an
  implementation artifact fixable by better classical hardware alone.
