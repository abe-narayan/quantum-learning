## Engineering Development

### What state-vector simulation actually is

A **state-vector simulator** represents an $n$-qubit quantum state as a
list of $2^n$ complex amplitudes — this platform's `StateVector` class,
used without exception in every Quantum Computing pillar course.
Applying a gate updates these amplitudes directly via ordinary linear
algebra (`applySingleQubitGate`, `applyCNOT`): exact matrix
multiplication, not an approximation of any kind.

### What it guarantees

A state-vector simulator computes the *exact* quantum state (up to
ordinary floating-point rounding, the same kind of error present in any
numerical computation). Every amplitude, every probability, every
expectation value this platform has ever displayed came from this
technique. That's fundamentally different from *sampling* (Writing
Your First Circuit's `sampleMeasurements`), which only estimates
probabilities statistically by drawing finite outcomes; a state-vector
simulation instead reads the probabilities straight off the amplitudes,
no draws involved.

Watching both side by side on the same circuit makes the distinction
concrete rather than just asserted: the "exact" bars below never move
(they ARE the state-vector answer), while the "sampled" bars are this
page's own live `sampleMeasurements` calls against that exact state,
landing closer to 0.5 as shot count grows, the same statistical
convergence Writing Your First Circuit already demonstrated once, here
made explorable across five different shot counts on a fresh Bell
circuit.

### Simulating vs. being

"Simulating a quantum computer" on a classical machine (this platform's
entire engine) and "being a quantum computer" (Physical Qubit Platforms'
actual hardware) compute the *same* mathematical answer for a given
circuit, but by *completely different physical means*. Nothing about
this platform's engine is somehow "not really" quantum mechanics — the
linear algebra is exact. It's just being carried out by classical
hardware instead of a physical qubit (Simulators vs. Real Hardware's
opposite-failure-modes framing), and that distinction is what makes
this course's cost question (next lesson) a real one, not a
formality.

## Physical Interpretation

Every numerical claim this entire platform has ever verified, Grover's
algorithm's success probability, the Bell state's entanglement, the
hydrogen atom's energy levels via a truncated basis, rests on this
exact technique, used without ever being named as such until now. This
lesson's contribution is entirely conceptual: recognizing the common
thread, not adding new capability.

## Worked Example

**Is running `runCircuit` on this platform's `QuantumCircuit` "real
quantum computing," or "just simulation"?**

It's state-vector simulation: exact quantum-mechanical calculation,
computed on ordinary classical hardware. It isn't running on an actual
quantum device (Physical Qubit Platforms), so it carries none of real
hardware's physical noise (Noise, Decoherence & Scaling). It also isn't
an approximation of the underlying physics — the amplitudes it computes
are what an ideal, noiseless quantum computer would produce for the
same circuit, down to floating-point precision.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Thinking "simulation" means "approximation."** State-vector simulation
computes EXACT amplitudes (up to floating-point precision). The
approximation, when it exists, is in techniques like Tensor Network
Methods (a later lesson in this course), not in the state-vector
approach itself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming this platform's engine is "not really" doing quantum
mechanics because it runs on classical hardware.** The linear algebra is
exact quantum mechanics; only the substrate executing it (classical bits
and transistors, vs. Physical Qubit Platforms' actual qubits) differs.
</Callout>

## Summary

- State-vector simulation represents an $n$-qubit state as $2^n$ exact
  complex amplitudes, updated via exact linear algebra, precisely this
  platform's `StateVector` engine, used throughout every prior course.
- It computes exact probabilities (up to floating-point precision),
  fundamentally different from statistical sampling.
- It is exact quantum-mechanical calculation executed on
  classical hardware, not an approximation, but also not physically
  "being" a quantum computer.
