## The Engine Behind Every Result So Far

Every circuit this platform has ever executed was run by a state-vector
simulator, the technique this course is named for. Naming it raises the
question the rest of the course spends its time on: if this method is
exact, and it has been exact for every lesson so far, why does anyone
need another one?

## One Amplitude Array, Watched Through a Gate

### What state-vector simulation is

A **state-vector simulator** represents an $n$-qubit <Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term> as a
list of $2^n$ complex <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term>, this platform's `StateVector` class,
used without exception in every Quantum Computing track course.
Applying a gate updates these amplitudes directly via ordinary linear
algebra (`applySingleQubitGate`, `applyCNOT`): exact matrix
multiplication, with no approximation anywhere in it.

Written out, the whole technique is four boxes wide, with the second one
repeated once per gate. Nothing in it is quantum: it is an array of
complex numbers, a matrix multiply, and a random draw at the end.

  ariaLabel="The state-vector simulation pipeline: allocate two-to-the-n amplitudes, apply the next gate's matrix to them, repeat for every gate, then square the amplitudes to get probabilities and draw shots from them"
  steps={[
    { label: "Allocate", detail: "2ⁿ complex amplitudes, all but one zero" },

The second box is where every cost in this course comes from: it touches
every one of those $2^n$ amplitudes, and $2^n$ is the number that
doubles with each qubit you add.

### The amplitude array, watched through one gate

"A list of $2^n$ complex amplitudes" is concrete enough to write out in
full for a small register. A 3-qubit register is an array of exactly
$2^3=8$ complex numbers, one slot per basis state. Freshly initialized
in $\lvert 000\rangle$, the array is:

| Slot | Basis state | Amplitude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $\lvert 000\rangle$ | $1$ |
| 1 | $\lvert 001\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 2 | $\lvert 010\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 3 | $\lvert 011\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 4 | $\lvert 100\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 5 | $\lvert 101\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 6 | $\lvert 110\rangle$ | $0$ |
| 7 | $\lvert 111\rangle$ | $0$ |

Now apply a single Hadamard to the first qubit (qubit 0, the leftmost
bit of each basis label, in this platform's convention). Mechanically,
this is what `applySingleQubitGate` does: it walks the array and pairs
every slot whose qubit-0 bit is $0$ with the slot four positions later,
its qubit-0-flipped partner. Four pairs mix: slots $(0,4)$, $(1,5)$,
$(2,6)$, and $(3,7)$. Each pair $(a_0, a_1)$ is replaced by
$((a_0+a_1)/\sqrt{2},\ (a_0-a_1)/\sqrt{2})$, the Hadamard's own matrix
acting inside that pair. Only the $(0,4)$ pair holds anything nonzero,
so the array becomes:

| Slot | Basis state | Mixes with slot | Amplitude after $H$ on qubit 0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $\lvert 000\rangle$ | 4 | $1/\sqrt{2}$ |
| 1 | $\lvert 001\rangle$ | 5 | $0$ |
| 2 | $\lvert 010\rangle$ | 6 | $0$ |
| 3 | $\lvert 011\rangle$ | 7 | $0$ |
| 4 | $\lvert 100\rangle$ | 0 | $1/\sqrt{2}$ |
| 5 | $\lvert 101\rangle$ | 1 | $0$ |
| 6 | $\lvert 110\rangle$ | 2 | $0$ |
| 7 | $\lvert 111\rangle$ | 3 | $0$ |

Notice what it cost. One gate, on one qubit, touched every one of the
8 amplitudes: all four pairs were read and rewritten, because nothing
in the array announces in advance which slots happen to be zero. That
is this course's entire cost story in miniature. A gate on a single
qubit is an operation over the whole $2^n$-entry array, which is
why the next lesson's scaling analysis comes out exponential
in the qubit count.

### What it guarantees

A state-vector simulator computes the *exact* quantum state (up to
ordinary floating-point rounding, the same kind of error present in any
numerical computation). Every amplitude, every probability, every
<Term id="expectation-value">expectation value</Term> this platform has ever displayed came from this
technique. That's different in kind from *sampling* (Writing
Your First Circuit's `sampleMeasurements`), which only estimates
probabilities statistically by drawing finite outcomes; a state-vector
simulation instead reads the probabilities straight off the amplitudes,
no draws involved.

Watching both side by side on the same circuit makes the distinction
concrete: the "exact" bars below never move
(they ARE the state-vector answer), while the "sampled" bars are this
page's own live `sampleMeasurements` calls against that exact state,
landing closer to 0.5 as shot count grows, the same statistical
convergence Writing Your First Circuit already demonstrated once, here
made explorable across five different shot counts on a fresh Bell
circuit.

### Simulating vs. being

"Simulating a quantum computer" on a classical machine (this platform's
entire engine) and "being a quantum computer" (Physical Qubit Platforms'
actual hardware) compute the *same* mathematical answer for a given
circuit, but by *completely different physical means*. Nothing about
this platform's engine is somehow "not really" quantum mechanics: the
linear algebra is exact. It's just being carried out by classical
hardware instead of a physical qubit (Simulators vs. Real Hardware's
opposite-failure-modes framing), and that distinction is what makes
this course's cost question (next lesson) a real one, not a
formality.

## Simulating Against Being

Every numerical claim this entire platform has ever verified, Grover's
algorithm's success probability, the Bell state's entanglement, the
hydrogen atom's energy levels via a truncated basis, rests on this
one technique, used without ever being named as such until now. This
lesson's contribution is entirely conceptual: recognizing the common
thread, not adding new capability.

## Is This Quantum Computing?

**Is running `runCircuit` on this platform's `QuantumCircuit` "real
quantum computing," or "just simulation"?**

It's state-vector simulation: exact quantum-mechanical calculation,
computed on ordinary classical hardware. It isn't running on an actual
quantum device (Physical Qubit Platforms), so it carries none of real
hardware's physical noise (Noise, Decoherence & Scaling). It also isn't
an approximation of the underlying physics. The amplitudes it computes
are what an ideal, noiseless quantum computer would produce for the
same circuit, down to floating-point precision.

## Confusing Exact With Approximate

<Callout type="note">
**Thinking "simulation" means "approximation."** State-vector simulation
computes EXACT amplitudes (up to floating-point precision). The
approximation, when it exists, is in techniques like Tensor Network
Methods (a later lesson in this course), not in the state-vector
approach itself.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming this platform's engine is "not really" doing quantum
mechanics because it runs on classical hardware.** The linear algebra is
exact quantum mechanics; only the substrate executing it (classical bits
and transistors, vs. Physical Qubit Platforms' actual qubits) differs.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. In one sentence, define what a state-vector simulator computes and
   what precision it achieves.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   A state-vector simulator holds an $n$-qubit state as an array of
   $2^n$ complex amplitudes and updates it by exact matrix
   multiplication, so it computes the state, and every probability and
   expectation value read off it, exactly, to within ordinary
   floating-point rounding.

   The clause worth defending is "exactly." It is a strong claim, so here
   is its scope.

   **What is exact:** the linear algebra. Applying a gate is a genuine
   matrix multiplication over the amplitude array, with no truncation, no
   sampling, no basis cut-off and no perturbative expansion anywhere.
   The amplitudes that come out are the amplitudes quantum mechanics
   specifies.

   **What the error actually is:** IEEE double-precision rounding, about
   one part in $10^{16}$ per operation. You can see the whole error
   budget in the engine's own trailing digits. A Bell circuit returns
   probabilities of $0.5000000000000001$; $HZH$ on $|0\rangle$ returns
   $[0,\ 1.0000000000000004]$. Those final digits *are* the
   inexactness, and there is nothing else.

   **What "exact" does not mean.** It computes what an *ideal, noiseless*
   quantum computer would produce for that circuit. It is not modelling
   any particular device, and it contains none of the $T_1$, $T_2$,
   crosstalk or readout error a real machine has, unless a noise model is
   deliberately added, which is what the Noise Simulation lesson does.
   Exactness is about faithfulness to the mathematics, not about
   faithfulness to a piece of hardware.

   The wrong turn is the word "simulation" itself, which in most of
   science implies approximation. Here it does not. The approximations in
   this course arrive later, with tensor networks, and they arrive as a
   deliberate trade of exactness for tractability.

   </details>

2. Contrast state-vector simulation with shot-based sampling
   (`sampleMeasurements`): which one gives EXACT probabilities, and
   which only estimates them?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   `runCircuit` is exact; `sampleMeasurements` estimates.

   **The test that settles it in one question:** would a second call with
   identical inputs return identical numbers? `runCircuit` is
   deterministic, so yes, bit for bit. `sampleMeasurements` draws random
   outcomes, so no, and the difference between two calls is the estimate's
   error made visible.

   **What each actually does.** `runCircuit` multiplies matrices through
   to the final amplitude array and reads probabilities off it by
   squaring magnitudes. Nothing is drawn; the Bell circuit's
   $1/\sqrt2$ amplitudes give $0.5$ directly.
   `sampleMeasurements` starts from that same exact distribution and then
   draws individual outcomes from it, one shot at a time, so what it
   returns is a frequency, carrying shot noise that shrinks only as
   $1/\sqrt{n}$.

   Three wrong turns, each of which gets close and then slips.

   **"Both are exact, since the sampler reads its frequencies off the
   same amplitudes `runCircuit` computed."** It notices the shared
   starting point and misses the draw that follows. The amplitudes are
   the sampler's *input*; its output is a random variable.

   **"Both estimate, because `runCircuit`'s repeated matrix
   multiplications accumulate rounding error."** This promotes
   floating-point rounding into statistical error, and the two are not
   comparable in either kind or size. Rounding is deterministic, so it is
   a bias rather than noise, and at 1000 shots it is around fourteen
   orders of magnitude smaller: shot noise contributes about 1.6% to an
   estimated probability, rounding about $10^{-16}$.

   **"`sampleMeasurements` is exact and `runCircuit` estimates by
   truncating small amplitudes."** It reverses the two roles and invents
   a truncation the engine does not perform. Nothing is thresholded away;
   the worked H-on-a-3-qubit-register example above rewrites all eight
   slots, zeros included, precisely because nothing announces in advance
   which ones are zero.

   The reason the contrast matters beyond bookkeeping is that only one of
   the two is available on hardware. A device can only ever give you the
   sampled version, because measurement returns outcomes rather than
   amplitudes. The exact column exists solely because a classical machine
   is holding the numbers.

   </details>

3. Is a state-vector simulation running on a classical computer "doing
   real quantum mechanics"? Explain your answer precisely, then name the
   measurable consequence that makes your answer more than a matter of
   wording.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Yes to the mathematics, no to the physics**, and the gap between
   those two answers is not a quibble. It is measurable, and it is what
   the rest of this course is about.

   **Yes to the mathematics.** The state lives in the same Hilbert space,
   evolves by the same unitary operators, and yields outcomes by the same
   Born rule. Nothing is approximated, nothing is a model of quantum
   mechanics standing in for the thing itself. The amplitudes computed
   are the amplitudes an ideal quantum computer would have, to
   floating-point precision. Calling this "not really quantum mechanics"
   would require pointing at a step where the physics was replaced by
   something else, and there is no such step.

   **No to the physics.** The substrate is classical: transistors,
   ordinary memory, ordinary arithmetic. Nothing inside the machine is in
   superposition or entangled with anything. The amplitudes are numbers
   written down in an array, the way a weather model's temperatures are
   numbers rather than weather.

   **Why the distinction is operational rather than philosophical.**
   Because storing the state explicitly costs something, and being the
   state does not. A classical machine must hold all $2^n$ amplitudes, so
   it pays memory that doubles with every qubit, and stops somewhere in
   the forties. A real quantum computer holds the same state while
   storing none of those numbers anywhere, which is exactly why it does
   not pay that cost, and, in the same breath, exactly why it cannot show
   the amplitudes to anyone.

   That is the whole argument for building the hardware. If simulating
   and being were the same thing, there would be no cost gap and no
   reason for the machines to exist. The gap is the subject of the next
   lesson, and it is real, computed, and exponential.

   Two wrong turns pull in opposite directions and are worth naming
   together.

   Downward: "it is a simulation, so it is an approximation of quantum
   mechanics." It is not. The approximation in this course is tensor
   networks, and it is introduced deliberately.

   Upward: "the linear algebra is exact, so this is quantum computing and
   hardware is redundant." Also no. Exactness is what makes it expensive,
   and expensive at an exponential rate.

   </details>

4. Name three specific prior-course results (from anywhere in this
   platform) that were computed via state-vector simulation, even though
   the term wasn't used at the time.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Three, with the numbers the engine returns:

   **Grover's success probability** (Quantum Algorithms I). `runGrover`
   allocates the uniform superposition over $2^n$ amplitudes and applies
   an oracle and a diffusion operator to the whole array once per
   iteration. On $n=3$ with one marked item, `optimalGroverIterations`
   gives 2, and the marked outcome's probability comes back as
   $0.9453125$.

   **The Bell state's entanglement** (Entanglement & Measurement). Build
   `h(0).cnot(0,1)`, run it, and hand the resulting amplitude array to
   `entanglementEntropy`, which returns $0.9999999999999999$, one full
   ebit, with `concurrenceOfPureState` giving $1.0000000000000002$. Both
   are functions of the amplitudes; there is nothing else for them to
   read.

   **The GHZ state's probabilities** (Writing Your First Circuit).
   `runCircuit` on `.h(0).cnot(0,1).cnot(1,2)` returns
   $[0.5,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0.5]$, eight amplitudes squared, six
   of them exactly zero.

   Others qualify equally: $HZH$ on $|0\rangle$ giving
   $[0,\ 1.0000000000000004]$, Shor's period-finding peaks after the
   quantum Fourier transform, and any Bloch-sphere coordinate this
   platform has ever plotted.

   **The test that decides membership**, which is more useful than the
   list, is whether a vector of amplitudes had to be built, evolved by a
   sequence of operators, and then read for probabilities. Plenty of
   numbers in this platform look like quantum results and fail that test:

   $\gcd(7,15)$ from the classical post-processing step of Shor's
   algorithm is Euclid's algorithm on two integers, sitting inside a
   quantum algorithm without being a quantum computation;

   the thermal photon occupation $\bar n$ from Cryogenic Systems is one
   closed-form expression evaluated at a given $\omega$ and $T$;

   a trapped-ion gate budget is a coherence time divided by a gate time,
   characterizing a quantum device rather than simulating one.

   The wrong turn is treating "appears in a quantum context" as the
   criterion. A great deal of ordinary classical arithmetic lives inside
   quantum algorithms and quantum engineering, and none of it touches a
   state vector.

   </details>

## Summary

- State-vector simulation represents an $n$-qubit state as $2^n$ exact
  complex amplitudes, updated via exact linear algebra, precisely this
  platform's `StateVector` engine, used throughout every prior course.
- It computes exact probabilities (up to floating-point precision),
  different in kind from statistical sampling.
- It is exact quantum-mechanical calculation executed on
  classical hardware, not an approximation, but also not physically
  "being" a quantum computer.
