## From Workflow to Working Code

The previous lessons described the build-run-read workflow; this one
performs it, start to finish. It builds a 3-qubit GHZ circuit, runs it
exactly, then samples it the way real hardware would, so both kinds of
output this platform's engine can produce, exact amplitudes and
shot-based samples, appear end to end below.

## Walkthrough

generalization of the Bell state) is built with:

```

circuit.h(0).cnot(0, 1).cnot(1, 2);
```

### Step 3: Sample it like real hardware would

```
const counts = sampleMeasurements(state, 5000);
```

Real hardware (and honest simulators) don't hand back exact amplitudes.
They report a finite number of individual measurement shots
(Qubit Readout Techniques). Running this platform's `sampleMeasurements`
with 5000 shots gives counts close to (but not exactly) a 2500/2500
split between "000" and "111". The run rendered on this page landed on
{ghzCount000} and {ghzCount111}. The deviation from a perfectly even
split is ordinary **statistical (shot) noise**, not a bug. It shrinks (relatively) as
shot count grows, following the standard $1/\sqrt{\text{shots}}$
statistical scaling any repeated-sampling estimate obeys.

Here's one live run of that at 5000 shots: the exact probability is
$0.5$ for each outcome by construction; the sampled fraction is this
page's own `sampleMeasurements` call, jittering
around 0.5 by whatever this particular run of 5000 shots happened to
land on.

## Where Exact Probabilities Meet Finite Shots

The gap between Step 2's exact probabilities and Step 3's sampled counts
*is* the central practical fact of running anything on real quantum
hardware: you never directly observe $|\psi\rangle$'s <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term>, only
statistics accumulated over many runs, always with <Term id="shot-noise-standard-error">finite-shot noise</Term> on
top of whatever hardware error (Noise, Decoherence & Scaling) is also
present.

## How Far Should a Count Drift

**Step 3 sampled this GHZ circuit 5000 times and got a `000` count that
missed 2500. How far from 2500 should the count be allowed to drift
before it stops being ordinary shot noise? Treat it as a binomial
sampling process.**

For $p=0.5$, $n=5000$: standard deviation
$\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}=\sqrt{5000\times0.25}=\sqrt{1250}\approx35.4$.
A 95% range is roughly $\pm2\sigma\approx\pm71$ around the expected
mean of 2500, so counts between about 2429 and 2571 are expected 95% of
the time, purely from statistical shot noise, with a perfect,
noiseless simulator and an exact 50/50 underlying probability. Check
this page's own run ({ghzCount000} and {ghzCount111}) against that
window: a count inside it is the sampler behaving exactly as it should,
not a circuit that needs debugging.

## Blaming Noise for Sampling

<Callout type="warning">
**Expecting sampled counts to exactly match the underlying probabilities.**
Even with a perfect, noiseless <Term id="quantum-state">quantum state</Term>, finite-shot sampling
always shows statistical fluctuation. The worked example's $\pm71$-count
range at 5000 shots is real and expected, not evidence of a broken
circuit.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
**Confusing shot noise (statistical, present even for a perfect
simulator) with hardware error (physical, from Noise, Decoherence &
Scaling's actual error sources).** Both contribute to real hardware
results; only shot noise is present in this lesson's idealized
`sampleMeasurements` example.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Write out (conceptually) the three `GateInstruction`s the GHZ
   circuit's `.h(0).cnot(0,1).cnot(1,2)` chain produces.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   An H instruction on qubit 0; a CNOT instruction with control 0 and
   target 1; a CNOT instruction with control 1 and target 2. In the
   engine's own words:

   `[{"gate":"H","targets":[0]},{"gate":"CNOT","targets":[0,1]},{"gate":"CNOT","targets":[1,2]}]`

   Three things in that list repay a second look.

   **The second CNOT's control is qubit 1, not qubit 0.** It is a chain,
   not a fan-out from a single control. That works because after the
   first CNOT, qubit 1 is already perfectly correlated with qubit 0, so
   conditioning on qubit 1 conditions on qubit 0 too, and the correlation
   propagates down the line.

   **A fan-out would also work, and is not what was written.** Building
   `.h(0).cnot(0,1).cnot(0,2)` produces the same GHZ state. The chain
   version is preferred because every gate in it acts on physically
   adjacent qubits, so it needs only nearest-neighbour connectivity,
   while the fan-out version needs qubit 0 coupled to qubit 2 as well.
   On a linear chip that difference is the SWAP overhead the Compilation
   course computes. The instruction list is where such a choice is
   visible, and where a router can act on it.

   **Nothing has run.** These are three data objects sitting in an array.
   The state does not exist until `runCircuit`, at which point
   `state.probabilities()` returns exactly
   $[0.5,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0.5]$.

   The wrong turn is writing the CNOT's two qubits as an unordered pair.
   `targets` is positional: entry zero is the control and entry one is
   the target. `{gate:"CNOT", targets:[1,0]}` is a different gate, and on
   this circuit it would not build a GHZ state at all.

   </details>

2. Using the same $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$ formula, estimate the standard
   deviation for 10,000 shots instead of 5000. Does statistical noise
   grow or shrink (in absolute count) with more shots? What about as a
   FRACTION of total shots?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   \sigma = \sqrt{np(1-p)} = \sqrt{10000\times0.5\times0.5} = \sqrt{2500} = 50 \text{ counts}.
   $$

   **In absolute count, noise grows.** From 35.4 counts at 5000 shots to
   50 at 10,000. Doubling the shots multiplied $\sigma$ by
   $\sqrt2\approx1.414$, which is exactly what $\sigma\propto\sqrt n$
   predicts.

   **As a fraction of the total, noise shrinks.**

   $$
   \frac{35.4}{5000} = 0.708\%, \qquad \frac{50}{10{,}000} = 0.500\%.
   $$

   Down by the same factor of $\sqrt2$, because
   $\sigma/n\propto1/\sqrt n$.

   Both statements are true at once, and the apparent tension is the
   whole point of the question. The count wanders further from its
   expected value as you take more shots; the *estimate of the
   probability* gets better. Nothing is contradictory, because they are
   ratios of different things.

   The practical consequence is the one worth memorizing, and it is
   discouraging. Precision improves as $1/\sqrt n$, so halving your error
   bar costs four times the shots, and a tenfold improvement costs a
   hundredfold. Going from 0.7% to 0.07% would take five million shots
   rather than five thousand. This is why the sampling cost, and not the
   circuit depth, is what stalls a great deal of real variational work,
   and it is a statistical fact rather than a hardware limitation: a
   perfect quantum computer obeys the same scaling.

   The wrong turn is answering only one half. "More shots means less
   noise" is the intuition everyone has, and it is right about the
   fraction and wrong about the count. Look at the shot-count slider
   above: the captioned raw counts swing by more at 100,000 shots than at
   100, while the bars visibly tighten onto the 0.5 line.

   </details>

3. Why does real hardware always report sampled counts, never exact
   amplitudes directly?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because a measurement returns one classical outcome, and an amplitude
   is not an outcome of anything.

   The measurement postulate says a measurement yields one eigenvalue of
   the measured observable, with a probability set by the state. There is
   no observable whose measured value is "the amplitude of $|000\rangle$."
   A single run of the GHZ circuit gives you the three-bit string `000`
   or the three-bit string `111`, and nothing else, and afterwards the
   superposition is gone.

   From there, everything follows.

   **Magnitudes need repetition.** $|\alpha|^2$ is a probability, and
   estimating a probability from samples is the only route to it, with
   the $1/\sqrt n$ tax question 2 just computed.

   **Phases need different circuits entirely.** A relative phase is
   invisible to a measurement in the computational basis, because it does
   not change any of those probabilities. It shows up only through
   interference, so learning it means rotating the basis before measuring
   and running the whole thing again. Reconstructing a full state that
   way is tomography, and the number of measurement settings it needs
   grows exponentially with the qubit count.

   **You cannot dodge this by copying the state.** The no-cloning theorem
   forbids duplicating an unknown state, so measuring "the same" state in
   several bases at once is not available. Each run gives you exactly one
   basis and exactly one outcome.

   **And there would be too many of them anyway.** A 50-qubit state has
   $2^{50}$ amplitudes. Even granting a magic amplitude-reading
   instrument, that is not a quantity of numbers a device could report.

   The clean inversion to carry away is about the simulator, not the
   hardware. `runCircuit` can hand back exact amplitudes only because it
   is *holding them*, as ordinary classical numbers in an array. That is
   the same reason it costs $2^n$ memory, which is the wall the
   Simulating Quantum Systems course runs into. Real hardware never
   stores those numbers anywhere, which is why it is not
   subject to the memory wall and also why it cannot show you its state.
   The two facts are one fact.

   The wrong turn is treating this as a temporary engineering shortfall,
   as though a better readout circuit might one day report amplitudes
   directly. Readout fidelity can improve without limit and this does not
   change, because the constraint is the measurement postulate rather
   than the instrument.

   </details>

4. If a sampled experiment gave 4000 "000" counts and 1000 "111" counts
   out of 5000 shots (badly off from the expected 50/50 split), would
   this most likely indicate shot noise alone, or something else?
   Explain your reasoning using the worked example's expected range,
   then use which outcomes did and did not appear to name the most
   likely specific cause.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Not shot noise. Not remotely.**

   The worked example gives $\sigma=\sqrt{5000\times0.25}\approx35.4$
   counts around an expected 2500, so ordinary sampling lands between
   about 2429 and 2571 roughly 95% of the time. The observed count misses
   by 1500, which is

   $$
   \frac{1500}{35.4} \approx 42\ \sigma .
   $$

   A 42-sigma excursion has a probability somewhere around $10^{-390}$.
   No number of experiments anyone will ever run makes that a live
   possibility. Something is different about the experiment, and the
   useful question is what.

   **Now the diagnostic part, which the numbers answer surprisingly
   precisely.** Notice what did *not* happen: no counts appeared in
   `001`, `010`, `011`, `100`, `101` or `110`. The state is still
   perfectly correlated, all three qubits agreeing on every single shot.
   Only the *weighting* between the two surviving outcomes is wrong.

   That rules out most of the usual suspects. A miswired CNOT chain would
   break the correlation and scatter counts into the other six strings. A
   readout error on one qubit would flip one bit and produce strings like
   `011` or `110`. Relaxation during readout would do the same. All of
   those show up as *new outcomes*, and there are none.

   What fits exactly is an underlying probability of 0.8 rather than 0.5
   on `000`. That is what you get if the first gate is not a Hadamard but
   a rotation through the wrong angle: an $R_y(\theta)$ with
   $\cos^2(\theta/2)=0.8$, so $\theta\approx0.927$ rad instead of the
   $\pi/2$ a Hadamard delivers. The two CNOTs then faithfully copy that
   unbalanced superposition onto the other two qubits, and you get a
   perfectly entangled, perfectly correlated, perfectly wrong GHZ state.
   Check it: at $p=0.8$ and $n=5000$, the expected count is 4000 with
   $\sigma=\sqrt{5000\times0.8\times0.2}\approx28$. The observation sits
   dead on the mean.

   So the answer is a miscalibrated or misspecified first gate, and the
   evidence is not merely that 42 sigma is too far but that the *pattern*
   of the deviation points at one specific cause.

   The wrong turn is stopping at "that's outside the $\pm71$ window, so
   something is wrong." True, and it leaves you no better off. The window
   tells you to keep looking; which outcomes are missing tells you where.

   </details>

## Summary

- Building a circuit (`.h(0).cnot(0,1).cnot(1,2)`) creates instructions
  as data; `runCircuit` executes them, giving exact GHZ-state

  shot-based counts with statistical fluctuation around the
  exact probabilities.
- At 5000 shots on a perfect 50/50 state, roughly $\pm71$ counts of
  deviation is expected 95% of the time from shot noise alone.
