## Motivation

Writing Your First Circuit showed shot noise alone, using a perfect,
noiseless simulator. This closing lesson adds back everything a
simulator conveniently strips away, real hardware's actual physical
error, practical access constraints, while also being honest about
where simulators themselves hit a very different wall.

## Engineering Development

### What real hardware adds on top of shot noise

Every physical error source Noise, Decoherence & Scaling covered
(T1/T2 decay, crosstalk, readout error, compounding multi-gate error)
sits on TOP of the pure statistical shot noise Writing Your First
Circuit already demonstrated. A real device's reported counts deviate
from the ideal probabilities by more than shot noise alone predicts; the
excess deviation is a genuine, measurable signature of physical error.

### Practical constraints unique to real hardware

- **Queue time**: real quantum hardware is a shared, scarce resource;
  jobs often wait in a queue before running, unlike a simulator running
  immediately on local (or cloud classical) compute.
- **Qubit count ceiling**: a specific device has a fixed number of
  physical qubits (Physical Qubit Platforms); a simulator's qubit count
  is limited differently (next section).
- **Connectivity constraints**: real hardware often only supports gates
  between specific qubit pairs (Physical Qubit Platforms' connectivity
  discussion), requiring extra SWAP gates (Compilation & Hybrid
  Algorithms) a simulator doesn't need.

### Where simulators hit their own wall

An ideal, noiseless simulator represents a state as a list of $2^n$
complex amplitudes (this platform's own `StateVector`), doubling with
every additional qubit. At $n=30$ qubits, that's over a billion
amplitudes; well before $n=50$, the memory requirement exceeds any
existing classical computer's capacity. This is the exact reason exact
state-vector simulation cannot substitute for real hardware at large
qubit counts, a genuinely different limitation from anything on the
"real hardware" side of this comparison, developed fully in Simulating
Quantum Systems' Computational Cost & Scaling lesson.

## Physical Interpretation

Simulators and real hardware fail in *opposite* ways: a simulator is
perfectly accurate (no physical error) but limited by classical memory,
scaling exponentially badly with qubit count; real hardware has no such
memory limit (a physical qubit doesn't cost exponentially more per
additional qubit, at least not for this reason) but suffers genuine,
unavoidable physical error and limited access. Neither is simply
"better." The right choice depends on the specific development stage
and question being asked.

## Worked Example

**A researcher is debugging a new 4-qubit circuit's logic. Should they
use a simulator or real hardware?**

Simulator: at only 4 qubits ($2^4=16$ amplitudes), exact simulation is
trivially cheap, gives exact (not sampled) results for easy debugging,
runs instantly with no queue, and has zero physical error to confound
whether a bug is in the circuit LOGIC or in hardware noise, exactly
what's needed at this development stage. Real hardware becomes the
right target only later, once the logic is verified and the actual
question shifts to "how does this perform under real physical
conditions."

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming real hardware is always the "more real" or "better" choice.**
For debugging circuit logic specifically, a noiseless simulator is
strictly more useful: it isolates logic bugs from hardware noise
entirely, something real hardware cannot do.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
**Assuming simulators can scale to arbitrarily many qubits just by using
a bigger classical computer.** The $2^n$ memory cost means even a
substantial hardware upgrade buys only a few more qubits of simulation
capacity. Exponential growth overwhelms any realistic linear resource
increase.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Real hardware adds genuine physical error (on top of pure shot noise)
  plus practical constraints (queue time, fixed qubit count,
  connectivity) that a simulator doesn't face.
- An ideal simulator instead faces exponential memory cost ($2^n$
  amplitudes), a hard wall well before large qubit counts.
- Simulators and hardware fail in opposite ways; the right choice
  depends on development stage: simulators for isolating logic bugs at
  small qubit count, hardware for evaluating real physical performance.
