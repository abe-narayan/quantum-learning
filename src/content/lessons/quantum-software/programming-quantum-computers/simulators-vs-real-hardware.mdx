noiseless simulator. This closing lesson puts back what that simulator
left out (physical error, and the practical constraints of getting time

## What Hardware Adds, and Where Simulators Stop

### What real hardware adds on top of shot noise

Every physical error source Noise, Decoherence & Scaling covered
(<Term id="t1-t2-coherence-times">T1/T2 decay</Term>, <Term id="crosstalk">crosstalk</Term>, readout error, compounding multi-gate error)
sits on TOP of the pure statistical shot noise Writing Your First
Circuit already demonstrated. A real device's reported counts deviate
from the ideal probabilities by more than shot noise alone predicts; the
excess deviation is a measurable signature of physical error, not
something a noiseless simulator will ever show you.

### Practical constraints unique to real hardware

- **Queue time**: real quantum hardware is a shared, scarce resource;
  jobs often wait in a queue before running, unlike a simulator running
  immediately on local (or cloud classical) compute.
- **Qubit count ceiling**: a specific device has a fixed number of
  physical qubits (Physical Qubit Platforms); a simulator's qubit count
  is limited differently (next section).
- **Connectivity constraints**: real hardware often only supports gates
  between specific qubit pairs (Physical Qubit Platforms' connectivity
  discussion), requiring extra <Term id="qubit-routing-swap-overhead">SWAP gates</Term> (Compilation & Hybrid
  Algorithms) a simulator doesn't need.

### Where simulators hit their own wall

An ideal, noiseless simulator represents a state as a list of $2^n$
complex <Term id="amplitude">amplitudes</Term> (this platform's own `StateVector`), doubling with
every additional qubit. At $n=30$ qubits, that's over a billion
amplitudes; well before $n=50$, the memory requirement exceeds any
existing classical computer's capacity. That is why exact state-vector
simulation cannot stand in for real hardware at large qubit counts, a
different kind of limitation from anything on the "real hardware" side of
this comparison, developed in full in Simulating Quantum Systems'
Computational Cost & Scaling lesson.

## Two Tools That Fail in Opposite Directions

Simulators and real hardware fail in *opposite* ways: a simulator is
perfectly accurate (no physical error) but limited by classical memory,
scaling exponentially badly with qubit count; real hardware has no such
memory limit (a physical qubit doesn't cost exponentially more per
additional qubit, at least not for this reason) but suffers unavoidable
physical error and limited access. Neither is simply "better." The
right choice depends on the specific development stage and question
being asked.

## Choosing the Target for a Given Job

**A researcher is debugging a new 4-qubit circuit's logic. Should they
use a simulator or real hardware?**

Simulator. At 4 qubits the state is 16 amplitudes ($2^4$), so exact
simulation costs nothing worth measuring. It returns the ideal
probabilities rather than sampled counts, it runs immediately with no
queue, and, the decisive point at this stage, it contributes no physical
error of its own, so any deviation from what the circuit should do is a
bug in the circuit. Hardware becomes the right target later,
once the logic is settled and the question changes to how the circuit
holds up under real physical conditions.

## Debugging Logic Against Noise

<Callout type="note">
**Assuming real hardware is always the "more real" or "better" choice.**
For debugging circuit logic specifically, a noiseless simulator is
strictly more useful: it isolates logic bugs from hardware noise
entirely, something real hardware cannot do.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming simulators can scale to arbitrarily many qubits just by using
a bigger classical computer.** The $2^n$ memory cost means even a
substantial hardware upgrade buys only a few more qubits of simulation
capacity. Exponential growth overwhelms any realistic linear resource
increase.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. List three practical constraints real hardware has that a local
   simulator does not.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Queue time.** A quantum computer is one physical machine in one
   building, shared among everyone with access, so a job waits before it
   runs. A simulator is a process you start, and it starts now.

   **A fixed qubit-count ceiling.** A device has the number of physical
   qubits it was built with, and no amount of patience adds one. A
   simulator's limit is memory, which is a different kind of wall and one
   you can push on by renting a bigger machine, at least for a few more
   qubits.

   **Connectivity constraints.** Real hardware typically supports
   two-qubit gates only between specific physical pairs, so a circuit
   asking for a gate between distant qubits has to be routed with extra
   SWAP gates before it can run. A simulator applies a CNOT between any
   two indices at the same cost.

   Several more belong on the list if you want them. Physical error on
   top of shot noise, which the rest of this lesson is about. Cost, since
   hardware access is often metered per shot. Calibration drift, so a
   circuit submitted today and run after a queue may meet a differently
   calibrated device. And the loss of introspection: on a simulator you
   can print the state after every gate, and on hardware you cannot look
   at an intermediate state without destroying it.

   The wrong turn is putting "it is noisy" as all three entries in
   different words. Noise is one constraint, and the three above are
   logistical and architectural rather than physical. A perfectly
   noiseless quantum computer would still have a queue, a fixed qubit
   count, and a connectivity graph.

   </details>

2. How many complex amplitudes does an exact state-vector simulation of
   a 20-qubit circuit require? (Express as a power of 2, and roughly in
   scientific notation.) Then state the memory that implies, and what
   ten more qubits would cost.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576 \approx 1.05\times10^{6} \text{ complex amplitudes.}
   $$

   `stateVectorAmplitudeCount(20)` returns exactly 1,048,576.

   In memory that is about 16.8 MB, at 16 bytes for a complex number
   stored as two 64-bit floats, which any laptop holds without noticing.

   The reason for $2^n$ rather than anything smaller is worth restating,
   because it is the fact the whole Simulating Quantum Systems course
   turns on. A general $n$-qubit state assigns an independent complex
   amplitude to every one of the $2^n$ computational basis strings, and
   there are $2^n$ of those because each qubit contributes a binary
   choice. The count is a property of quantum mechanics, not of this
   engine.

   And a million amplitudes for twenty qubits is the *comfortable* end of
   the curve. Ten more qubits multiplies it by $2^{10}$: at $n=30$ it is
   $1.07\times10^{9}$ amplitudes and about 17.2 GB, past a typical
   laptop. Twenty more beyond that gives about 18.0 PB at $n=50$.

   The wrong turn is reading "20 qubits" as a small problem because the
   number 20 is small. In an exponential the input is the exponent, and
   intuitions calibrated on the base do not transfer. The useful habit is
   to convert every qubit count into its amplitude count before reasoning
   about whether something is feasible.

   </details>

3. For the worked example's 4-qubit debugging scenario, explain
   specifically why hardware noise would make it HARDER, not easier, to
   isolate a circuit-logic bug. Then say what simulating 4 qubits
   actually costs, and what that settles about the tradeoff.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because it breaks the inference the debugging depends on.

   On a noiseless simulator, the reasoning is a clean implication: the
   only thing that could make the output differ from what the circuit
   should produce is the circuit. Deviation means bug. That single
   implication is the entire value of a simulator at this stage, and it
   is not a convenience, it is what makes the observation informative at
   all.

   On hardware the implication becomes a disjunction: deviation means bug
   *or* noise, and the returned counts do not say which. Separating them
   requires characterizing the device's error independently, or comparing
   against a simulator, which is the tool you declined to use.

   Three specific ways the ambiguity bites.

   **Noise masks a bug.** A small logic error, a rotation slightly off or
   a gate on the wrong qubit, shifts the distribution by an amount that
   can sit comfortably inside the noise floor. On a simulator the same
   error is visible immediately, because there is no floor.

   **Noise mimics a bug.** Readout error and decoherence put counts into
   outcomes that should have probability zero. That is exactly the
   signature a miswired gate produces, so the most useful diagnostic
   available, which outcomes appeared that should not have, stops
   discriminating.

   **Results stop being reproducible.** Hardware error drifts between
   calibrations, so the same circuit submitted twice gives measurably
   different distributions. Debugging is the practice of changing one
   thing and seeing what changes, and it needs everything else to hold
   still.

   There is also a tooling loss. On a simulator you can read the state
   after every gate and see exactly where the circuit first departs from
   expectation. On hardware, measuring an intermediate state destroys it,
   so the equivalent means running a separate truncated circuit for each
   prefix, and each of those returns sampled statistics rather than a
   state.

   And nothing is being paid for the simulator's advantages. At 4 qubits
   the state is 16 amplitudes, 256 bytes. There is no cost, no queue, and
   no scale argument on the other side.

   The wrong turn is the appeal to realism: hardware is the real thing,
   so surely testing on it is more honest. It is more honest about a
   different question. "Does my circuit compute what I meant?" and "does
   my circuit survive this device?" are two questions, and answering the
   second before the first means debugging two unknowns at once.

   </details>

4. Explain why simulators and real hardware are described as failing in
   "opposite ways," rather than one simply being more limited than the
   other overall.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because each one's limitation is the direct cost of the other one's
   strength, and the two limits sit on different axes.

   **A simulator is exact and pays in memory.** It does honest linear
   algebra, so its answer is right to floating-point precision and no
   physical error enters anywhere. Being exact is precisely what makes it
   expensive: representing a general $n$-qubit state requires holding all
   $2^n$ amplitudes, so the binding limit is *width*. Somewhere in the
   forties of qubits it stops, and no realistic hardware upgrade moves
   that far, since a thousandfold more memory buys just under ten more
   qubits.

   **Hardware has no memory wall and pays in error.** It does not store
   amplitudes anywhere; it *is* the state. That is why an extra qubit
   costs one more qubit rather than doubling anything. And being a
   physical system means being coupled to a physical environment, so
   every gate is a real operation with a real error rate, and the binding
   limit is *depth against error rate*, the $p^N$ compounding of the
   hardware course.

   So the two strengths and the two weaknesses are the same two facts
   read twice. The simulator is exact because it holds the numbers, and
   expensive for the same reason. The device is cheap in memory because
   it holds nothing, and noisy for the same reason.

   Three ways this question is commonly answered wrongly, each of which
   collapses that structure.

   Reversing which one is exact: "simulators approximate to stay fast,
   hardware is exact because it is the physics." An exact state-vector
   simulator approximates nothing, and hardware is subject to $T_1$,
   $T_2$, crosstalk and readout error.

   Collapsing the two limits into one: "both run out at qubit count."
   Qubit count binds the simulator. What binds the device is how deep a
   circuit it can run before error accumulates, and a device with plenty
   of qubits can be useless at depth.

   Or attributing the wrong failure to each: floating-point rounding is
   deterministic and vastly smaller than shot noise, and hardware never
   pays a $2^n$ storage cost.

   One qualification, so the contrast is not overdrawn. "No memory limit"
   is scoped to memory. Adding physical qubits still costs control lines,
   cooling power and fridge space, which the Scaling Challenges lesson
   works out in detail. That cost is linear rather than exponential, and
   linear is not free.

   The practical upshot is that neither substitutes for the other, and
   the honest workflow uses both: the simulator to establish that the
   circuit is right, the device to find out whether it survives.

   </details>

## Summary

- Real hardware adds physical error (on top of pure shot noise)
  plus practical constraints (queue time, fixed qubit count,
  connectivity) that a simulator doesn't face.
- An ideal simulator instead faces exponential memory cost ($2^n$
  amplitudes), a hard wall well before large qubit counts.
- Simulators and hardware fail in opposite ways; the right choice
  depends on development stage: simulators for isolating logic bugs at
  small qubit count, hardware for evaluating real physical performance.
