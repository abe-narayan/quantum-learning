## What to Do Before Correction Is Affordable

Quantum SDKs Overview
said outright that a real production SDK's feature set includes "error
mitigation," one of the things this platform's own deliberately minimal
`QuantumCircuit`/`runCircuit` pattern doesn't build, and nothing since
has picked that thread back up. Quantum Error Correction & Fault
Tolerance built a different, related idea (repairing the quantum state
itself with extra qubits), and the NISQ capstone said a hybrid
algorithm's classical loop can "partially compensate for imperfect
quantum execution," without ever saying how. Both leave the same real,
standard NISQ-era technique unnamed, and both leave the opening question
standing: how does a number extracted from noisy runs end up more
trustworthy than any of the runs it came from?

## Zero-Noise Extrapolation and Readout Unfolding

### Mitigation is not correction

Error correction (built across the entire previous course) repairs the
*quantum state* itself: extra ancilla qubits, syndrome measurement, and
an explicit recovery operation restore $|\psi\rangle$ before the
computation continues, at a large physical-qubit overhead
(Fault-Tolerant Thresholds' whole subject). **<Term id="quantum-error-mitigation">Error mitigation</Term>** asks a
narrower question: for a hybrid algorithm like <Term id="vqe">VQE</Term> or QAOA, what
actually gets used at the end is not the quantum state at all but a
handful of *<Term id="expectation-value">expectation values</Term>*, $\langle H\rangle$, computed from
measurement statistics. Error mitigation accepts that every individual
run is noisy, and instead corrects the **statistics computed from many
noisy runs**, using no extra qubits at all, just extra circuit
executions and classical post-processing.

### Zero-noise extrapolation, derived

The idea: deliberately run the SAME circuit at several different,
known, amplified noise levels, measure the observable at each, and
extrapolate the trend back to the (unreachable) zero-noise point.

Model the noise as Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics' depolarizing
channel, parameter $p$ per "noise unit":

$$
\varepsilon(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2}
$$

For an observable $O$ with $\text{Tr}(O)=0$ (**traceless**: true of
every individual Pauli, and hence of any Pauli-string Hamiltonian this
platform's VQE/QAOA lessons use), the maximally-mixed piece contributes
nothing:

$$
\langle O\rangle_{\varepsilon} = \text{Tr}(O\,\varepsilon(\rho)) = (1-p)\,\text{Tr}(O\rho) + p\,\frac{\text{Tr}(O)}{2} = (1-p)\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
$$

Applying the channel $n$ times in a row (Noise Simulation's
`applyChannelRepeatedly`, reused here unchanged) compounds this factor:

$$
\langle O\rangle(n) = (1-p)^n\,\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
$$

**Digital zero-noise extrapolation** realizes different integer values
of $n$ (the "noise level" $\lambda$) on real hardware via **unitary
folding**: replacing each gate $G$ with $G\,G^\dagger\,G$ leaves the
IDEAL action unchanged exactly ($G G^\dagger G = G(G^\dagger G) = G\cdot I=G$,
since every gate is unitary) while tripling how many times the
physical device's per-gate noise gets applied. Folding once gives
$\lambda=3$; not folding gives $\lambda=1$; the ideal, unreachable
target is $\lambda=0$.

For small $p\lambda$, $(1-p)^\lambda \approx 1-p\lambda$: **linear in**
$\lambda$. Two measurements at $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=3$ pin down that
line and extrapolate it to $\lambda=0$:

$$
\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}} \approx \langle O\rangle(0) = \frac{3\langle O\rangle(1) - \langle O\rangle(3)}{2} = \frac32\langle O\rangle(1) - \frac12\langle O\rangle(3)
$$

(the standard two-point linear-extrapolation formula, $y_0 = y_1 -
x_1\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$, evaluated at $x_1=1,x_2=3$.)

<Callout type="note">
This uses $\text{Tr}(O)=0$ specifically. For an observable with nonzero
trace, the depolarizing channel drives $\langle O\rangle$ toward
$\text{Tr}(O)/2$, not zero, and the same linear-extrapolation algebra
still applies, just with an extra constant term to solve for.
</Callout>

### Readout error mitigation

A second, independent error source: even a perfectly-prepared state can
be **misread** at measurement time (Qubit Readout Techniques). Model
this with a **confusion matrix** $M$, built once by calibration
(prepare $|0\rangle$, measure many times, record how often "1" is
reported instead; repeat for $|1\rangle$):

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix}P(\text{read }0\mid\text{true }0) & P(\text{read }0\mid\text{true }1)\\ P(\text{read }1\mid\text{true }0) & P(\text{read }1\mid\text{true }1)\end{pmatrix}
$$

A perfect readout is $M=I$. Real superconducting-qubit readout is
typically **asymmetric** (Qubit Readout Techniques, T1 & T2
Decoherence): a $|1\rangle$ is more likely to be misread as $0$ (it can
decay to $|0\rangle$ during the measurement pulse) than a $|0\rangle$ is
to be misread as $1$. Whatever raw distribution the device reports,
$\vec p_{\text{meas}}$, relates to the true distribution by
$\vec p_{\text{meas}} = M\vec p_{\text{true}}$, so **inverting $M$
recovers the true distribution from the raw one**:

$$
\vec p_{\text{true}} = M^{-1}\vec p_{\text{meas}}
$$

## Fixing the Average, Never the Run

Both techniques share the same shape: accept that any single circuit
execution is noisy, then use EXTRA classical work (more circuit
repetitions at controlled noise levels, or a one-time calibration matrix
inversion) to pull a less-biased number out of many noisy ones. Neither
technique changes what the quantum device physically does on any given
run; both operate entirely on the classical side of the hybrid loop
Hybrid Quantum-Classical Workflows already named. That is the
"classical optimizer can partially compensate" sentence the NISQ
capstone left unexplained, cashed out.

## Worked Example: Correcting a measured distribution

**A device's calibration gives**
$M=\begin{pmatrix}0.98&0.15\\0.02&0.85\end{pmatrix}$ **(a
15%-of-the-time $1\to0$ misread against only 2% the other way). A
circuit's raw, uncorrected measurement gives
$\vec p_{\text{meas}}=(0.20,0.80)$. Recover the true distribution.**

The asymmetry here is realistic in *shape*, and it is the shape T1 decay
during the readout window produces: a $|1\rangle$ has somewhere to fall
and a $|0\rangle$ does not. The magnitude is deliberately exaggerated so
the correction is visible in three digits. Averaged over the two states
this matrix is about 91.5% readout fidelity, worse than the 95-99% range
Qubit Readout Techniques quotes as representative. On a device inside
that range the arithmetic below is identical and the correction is a few
points rather than fourteen.

<MatrixGrid
  matrix={romMatrix}
  label="Confusion matrix M (rows = reported outcome, columns = true state)"
  ariaLabel="Confusion matrix M: 0.98 and 0.15 in the top row, 0.02 and 0.85 in the bottom row, showing a 15% one-to-zero misread against only a 2% zero-to-one misread"
/>

First, $\det M = (0.98)(0.85)-(0.15)(0.02) = 0.833-0.003 = 0.830$, so

$$
M^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.85&-0.15\\-0.02&0.98\end{pmatrix}
$$

Then:

$$
\vec p_{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.85&-0.15\\-0.02&0.98\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.20\\0.80\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.170-0.120\\-0.004+0.784\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.050\\0.780\end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix}0.060\\0.940\end{pmatrix}
$$

  bars={romBars}
  maxValue={1}
  ariaLabel="Bar chart comparing the raw measured distribution, P(0) 0.20 and P(1) 0.80, against the readout-corrected distribution, P(0) 0.06 and P(1) 0.94, with the corrected pair highlighted"
/>

**Check:** $M\vec p_{\text{true}} = (0.98\times0.060+0.15\times0.940,\ 0.02\times0.060+0.85\times0.940) \approx (0.0588+0.1410,\ 0.0012+0.7990) = (0.200,\ 0.800)$,
reproducing $\vec p_{\text{meas}}$ to the three digits shown here, and
exactly when the unrounded $\vec p_{\text{true}}$ is used, confirming
the inversion.
The correction is substantial: the raw circuit looked like $80\%$
$|1\rangle$, but the readout-corrected estimate is closer to $94\%$
$|1\rangle$. The extra $14$ points were entirely the asymmetric
misread rate, not the circuit's real behavior, and a VQE cost function
computed from the raw (uncorrected) $80\%$ would be measurably biased.

## Mistaking Mitigation for Cheap Correction

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating error mitigation as a cheaper substitute for error
correction that happens to also work at scale.** It doesn't. The
threshold theorem (Fault-Tolerant Thresholds) gives EXPONENTIAL error
suppression at POLYNOMIAL physical-qubit overhead, once below threshold.
Error mitigation gives no such scaling: extending zero-noise
extrapolation or probabilistic error cancellation (a related, cited-not-derived
technique using a quasi-probability decomposition of the noise instead
of a linear fit) to deeper, noisier circuits requires exponentially MORE
circuit repetitions to keep the statistical estimate's variance under
control, a proven fundamental limit (Takagi et al. 2022;
Quek et al. 2024), not an engineering gap that better algorithms will close.
Mitigation is a useful NISQ-era tool with no qubit overhead;
it is not a scalable alternative to fault tolerance.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the two-point extrapolation formula is exact.** It's the
FIRST-ORDER (linear) approximation to a curve that's actually
exponential, $(1-p)^\lambda$. This lesson's own numbers show a
nonzero residual ($\approx0.37\%$) even in a clean, noiseless-simulation
test case; on real hardware, with additional noise sources beyond a
single depolarizing channel, the residual is generally larger still.
"Extrapolated" means "a corrected estimate with reduced bias," not "the
exact answer."
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. Using $\langle O\rangle(\lambda)=(1-p)^\lambda\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}$
   with $p=0.1$, compute $\langle O\rangle(1)$ and $\langle O\rangle(3)$,
   then apply the two-point extrapolation formula. How does the
   residual error compare to this lesson's $p=0.05$ case, and why does
   it grow with $p$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   With $\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}=1$:

   $$
   \langle O\rangle(1) = 0.9, \qquad \langle O\rangle(3) = 0.9^3 = 0.729,
   $$

   $$
   \langle O\rangle(0) \approx \tfrac32(0.9) - \tfrac12(0.729) = 1.35 - 0.3645 = 0.9855 .
   $$

   A residual error of $0.0145$, or **1.45%**, against the lesson's
   $0.36875\%$ at $p=0.05$. Both closed forms are confirmed by pushing
   the real depolarizing channel through `applyChannelRepeatedly`, which
   returns $\langle Z\rangle=0.9000000000$ and $0.7290000000$ at
   $\lambda=1$ and $3$, matching $(1-p)^\lambda$ to ten digits.

   **The residual grew by a factor of 3.93, so doubling $p$ roughly
   quadrupled it.** That is not a coincidence, and the algebra gives it
   exactly. Substitute the model into the formula:

   $$
   \tfrac32(1-p) - \tfrac12(1-p)^3 = \tfrac32 - \tfrac32 p - \tfrac12\left(1 - 3p + 3p^2 - p^3\right) = 1 - \tfrac32 p^2 + \tfrac12 p^3 .
   $$

   So the residual is exactly

   $$
   1 - \langle O\rangle(0) = \tfrac32 p^2 - \tfrac12 p^3 .
   $$

   Check it: at $p=0.05$ that is $0.00375-0.0000625=0.00368750$, and at
   $p=0.1$ it is $0.015-0.0005=0.01450000$. Both agree with the direct
   computation to every digit.

   **Why it grows.** The leading term is $\tfrac32p^2$, quadratic, so
   doubling $p$ quadruples the residual to leading order, and the small
   cubic correction is why the observed ratio is 3.93 rather than exactly
   4. Notice what the $p^2$ says: the linear term cancelled completely.
   That is the whole point of extrapolating. The raw $\lambda=1$
   measurement is wrong by $p$, a first-order error, and the two-point
   fit removes that entirely, leaving only the curvature of
   $(1-p)^\lambda$ that a straight line cannot follow. At $p=0.1$ the
   raw measurement is 10% off and the mitigated estimate is 1.45% off, a
   sevenfold improvement from two measurements instead of one.

   The direction of the error is also fixed rather than random.
   $(1-p)^\lambda$ is convex, curving upward, so a chord through two
   points on it lies *above* the curve between them and *below* it when
   extended backwards. The estimate therefore always lands short of the
   true value, never over. That is a bias, and a known-sign one, which is
   why adding a third point at $\lambda=5$ and fitting a quadratic
   shrinks it further.

   The wrong turn is calling the extrapolated number the answer. It is a
   corrected estimate with the first-order bias removed, and this
   lesson's own Common Mistakes box says so. On real hardware, where the
   noise is not a single clean depolarizing channel, the residual is
   larger still.

   </details>

2. Why does zero-noise extrapolation's derivation specifically require
   $\text{Tr}(O)=0$? What would change in the derivation for an
   observable with $\text{Tr}(O)\ne0$?

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because the tracelessness is what makes the noise a *pure shrink*,
   with no offset, which is what turns repeated application into a clean
   geometric decay.

   Work the single step. With
   $\varepsilon(\rho)=(1-p)\rho+p\,I/2$,

   $$
   \langle O\rangle_\varepsilon = \operatorname{Tr}(O\varepsilon(\rho)) = (1-p)\operatorname{Tr}(O\rho) + p\,\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(O)}{2} .
   $$

   With $\operatorname{Tr}(O)=0$ the second term vanishes and every
   application multiplies the expectation by the same factor $(1-p)$.
   Compounding is then immediate: $\langle O\rangle(\lambda) =
   (1-p)^\lambda\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}$, one parameter, decaying
   to zero, which is exactly the model the two-point formula inverts.

   **With $\operatorname{Tr}(O)\ne0$**, write $c=\operatorname{Tr}(O)/2$.
   The step becomes affine rather than linear:

   $$
   \langle O\rangle \mapsto (1-p)\langle O\rangle + pc ,
   $$

   a map whose fixed point is $c$ rather than 0. Iterating gives

   $$
   \langle O\rangle(\lambda) = (1-p)^\lambda\left(\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}} - c\right) + c .
   $$

   Same exponential, now decaying toward $c$ instead of toward zero. The
   depolarizing channel drives the state to $I/2$, and $\operatorname{Tr}
   (O\,I/2)=c$ is simply what $O$ reads on the maximally mixed state.

   **What changes in practice: less than it looks.** The extrapolation
   still works, because the shape is still exponential in $\lambda$;
   there is just a known constant to account for. And $c$ *is* known:
   $\operatorname{Tr}(O)/2$ is computable from the observable alone,
   with no experiment. So the cleanest fix is to subtract it, extrapolate
   $\langle O\rangle-c$ exactly as before, and add it back. Failing to
   account for it is what breaks things: the two-point formula assumes
   the decay heads to zero, so applied to an observable with an offset it
   extrapolates the wrong line and is biased even at first order.

   **Why the condition is usually satisfied anyway.** Every non-identity
   Pauli matrix is traceless, and a tensor product of Pauli operators is
   traceless as long as it is not the identity string. A Hamiltonian
   written as a sum of Pauli strings is therefore traceless unless it
   carries an explicit identity term, and an identity term is a constant
   energy offset that can be pulled out and added back at the end. That
   is why the lesson can say the condition is "true of every individual
   Pauli, and hence of any Pauli-string Hamiltonian" this platform's VQE
   and QAOA lessons use.

   The wrong turn is treating the tracelessness as a limitation on which
   observables can be mitigated. It is a condition on which *model* to
   fit, and the general case just has one more known constant in it.

   </details>

3. For the readout confusion matrix
   $M=\begin{pmatrix}0.98&0.15\\0.02&0.85\end{pmatrix}$, what raw
   measured distribution would a TRUE distribution of exactly
   $(1,0)$ (perfect $|0\rangle$) produce? (Hint: this is $M$ applied
   directly to $(1,0)^T$, no inversion needed.)

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   M\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.98\\0.02\end{pmatrix} .
   $$

   A perfectly prepared $|0\rangle$ is reported as "0" 98% of the time
   and as "1" 2% of the time.

   Multiplying by $(1,0)^T$ just selects the first column, so the
   arithmetic is trivial. What the question is really checking is that
   you read the matrix the right way round, so it is worth being explicit
   about the layout. Rows are the reported outcome; columns are the true
   state. So column 0 is the conditional distribution of readings given a
   true $|0\rangle$, and column 1 is the same given a true $|1\rangle$.
   Both columns sum to 1, as conditional distributions must: $0.98+0.02$
   and $0.15+0.85$.

   That column-stochastic structure is also the built-in check on the
   most common mistake here, which is reading the first *row* and
   answering $(0.98,\,0.15)$. Those two numbers sum to 1.13, which is not
   a probability distribution, and noticing that catches the error before
   it propagates. The second common mistake is reaching for $M^{-1}$
   because the lesson's worked example did. Inversion goes the other way,
   from a measured distribution back to a true one; here the true
   distribution is given and the measured one is what is wanted.

   Two things worth reading off the answer.

   **This is exactly what calibration measures.** The way $M$ is built is
   to prepare $|0\rangle$ many times and record the reported outcomes,
   which hands you column 0 directly, then repeat with $|1\rangle$ for
   column 1. So this question is not a hypothetical; it is a description
   of the calibration experiment.

   **The asymmetry is physical.** A $|1\rangle$ is misread 15% of the
   time, seven and a half times more often than a $|0\rangle$'s 2%,
   because during the several hundred nanoseconds of readout integration
   a qubit in $|1\rangle$ can relax toward $|0\rangle$, and there is no
   comparable route upward. That asymmetry is why the correction in the
   lesson's worked example is large, moving 80% to 94%, and why simply
   subtracting a flat error rate would not have worked.

   </details>

4. Explain, in your own words, why "more circuit repetitions, no extra
   qubits" (mitigation) and "more physical qubits, exponential
   suppression" (correction) are different resource trades, not two
   versions of the same idea.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because they repair different objects, spend different resources, and
   scale in opposite shapes.

   **Different objects.** Error correction repairs the *quantum state*.
   Ancilla qubits measure syndromes, a recovery operation is applied, and
   the register is genuinely restored, so the computation can continue on
   corrected data. Error mitigation repairs *a number*. It accepts that
   every single run is exactly as noisy as it was, and uses classical
   post-processing over many such runs to produce a less biased estimate
   of an expectation value. After a full zero-noise-extrapolation
   protocol finishes, no state the device prepared on any single run was
   any cleaner than before.

   **Different resources.** Correction spends physical qubits, on the
   order of hundreds to thousands per logical qubit, plus continuous
   syndrome measurement and real-time classical decoding. Mitigation
   spends circuit executions and classical arithmetic, and no qubits at
   all. That is why mitigation is usable today on hardware that cannot
   support a single logical qubit.

   **Opposite scaling shapes, which is the decisive difference.** Below
   the threshold, error correction gives *exponential* suppression of the
   logical error rate in the code distance at *polynomial* physical-qubit
   overhead. Buying more qubits buys accuracy very cheaply in the
   exponent. Mitigation has no such curve. Keeping the variance of the
   estimate under control as circuits get deeper and noisier requires
   exponentially more repetitions, and that is a proven fundamental
   limit rather than a gap better algorithms will close.

   So the two are not points on one spectrum with correction at the
   expensive end. One converts qubits into accuracy at a favourable
   exponential rate; the other converts shots into accuracy at an
   unfavourable one. Neither substitutes for the other: mitigation cannot
   make a deep circuit work, because the sampling cost explodes exactly
   where you would need it, and correction cannot be run today, because
   the qubit counts do not exist.

   One further restriction on mitigation is worth carrying, because it
   is easy to miss and it is structural. Mitigation only helps when the
   thing you want *is* an expectation value averaged over many runs. VQE
   and QAOA qualify, which is why the technique belongs to the NISQ era
   specifically. An algorithm whose output is a particular measured bit
   string, Shor's period-finding for instance, has no expectation value
   to de-bias: you need the run itself to be right, and only correction
   delivers that.

   The wrong turn is the one the lesson names outright: reading
   mitigation as cheap error correction that happens to also work at
   scale. It is a genuinely useful tool with no qubit overhead, and it is
   not a path to fault tolerance.

   </details>

## Summary

- Error mitigation statistically corrects EXPECTATION VALUES computed
  from many noisy runs; it never repairs any single run's quantum
  state, unlike error correction.
- Zero-noise extrapolation: for a traceless observable under a
  depolarizing channel, $\langle O\rangle(\lambda)=(1-p)^\lambda\langle
  O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}$, confirmed here against this platform's own
  `applyChannelRepeatedly`; a two-point linear fit
  ($\frac32\langle O\rangle(1)-\frac12\langle O\rangle(3)$) extrapolates
  to an estimate close to, but not exactly at, the true zero-noise
  value.
- Readout error mitigation inverts a calibrated confusion matrix,
  $\vec p_{\text{true}}=M^{-1}\vec p_{\text{meas}}$, worked numerically
  above to a substantial, verified correction ($80\%\to94\%$).
- Mitigation's fundamental limit: sampling overhead grows (provably,
  eventually exponentially) with circuit noise, the opposite tradeoff
  shape from error correction's polynomial-overhead, exponential-suppression
  threshold theorem. Mitigation earns its place in the NISQ era; it does
  not substitute for fault tolerance at scale.
