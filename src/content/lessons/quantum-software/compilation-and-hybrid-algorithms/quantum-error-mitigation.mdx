## Engineering Development

### Mitigation is not correction

Error correction (built across the entire previous course) repairs the
*quantum state* itself: extra ancilla qubits, syndrome measurement, and
an explicit recovery operation restore $|\psi\rangle$ before the
computation continues, at a large physical-qubit overhead
(Fault-Tolerant Thresholds' whole subject). **<Term id="quantum-error-mitigation">Error mitigation</Term>** asks a
narrower question: for a hybrid algorithm like <Term id="vqe">VQE</Term> or QAOA, what
actually gets used at the end is not the quantum state at all but a
handful of *<Term id="expectation-value">expectation values</Term>*, $\langle H\rangle$, computed from
measurement statistics. Error mitigation accepts that every individual
run is noisy, and instead corrects the **statistics computed from many
noisy runs**, using no extra qubits at all, just extra circuit
executions and classical post-processing.

### Zero-noise extrapolation, derived

The idea: deliberately run the SAME circuit at several different,
known, amplified noise levels, measure the observable at each, and
extrapolate the trend back to the (unreachable) zero-noise point.

Model the noise as Advanced Topics in Quantum Mechanics' depolarizing
channel, parameter $p$ per "noise unit":

$$
\varepsilon(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\,\frac{I}{2}
$$

For an observable $O$ with $\text{Tr}(O)=0$ (**traceless** — true of
every individual Pauli, and hence of any Pauli-string Hamiltonian this
platform's VQE/QAOA lessons use), the maximally-mixed piece contributes
nothing:

$$
\langle O\rangle_{\varepsilon} = \text{Tr}(O\,\varepsilon(\rho)) = (1-p)\,\text{Tr}(O\rho) + p\,\frac{\text{Tr}(O)}{2} = (1-p)\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
$$

Applying the channel $n$ times in a row (Noise Simulation's
`applyChannelRepeatedly`, reused here unchanged) compounds this factor:

$$
\langle O\rangle(n) = (1-p)^n\,\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}
$$

**Digital zero-noise extrapolation** realizes different integer values
of $n$ (the "noise level" $\lambda$) on real hardware via **unitary
folding**: replacing each gate $G$ with $G\,G^\dagger\,G$ leaves the
IDEAL action unchanged exactly ($G G^\dagger G = G(G^\dagger G) = G\cdot I=G$,
since every gate is unitary) while tripling how many times the
physical device's per-gate noise gets applied. Folding once gives
$\lambda=3$; not folding gives $\lambda=1$; the ideal, unreachable
target is $\lambda=0$.

For small $p\lambda$, $(1-p)^\lambda \approx 1-p\lambda$: **linear in**
$\lambda$. Two measurements at $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=3$ pin down that
line and extrapolate it to $\lambda=0$:

$$
\langle O\rangle_{\text{ideal}} \approx \langle O\rangle(0) = \frac{3\langle O\rangle(1) - \langle O\rangle(3)}{2} = \frac32\langle O\rangle(1) - \frac12\langle O\rangle(3)
$$

(the standard two-point linear-extrapolation formula, $y_0 = y_1 -
x_1\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$, evaluated at $x_1=1,x_2=3$.)

<Callout type="note">
This uses $\text{Tr}(O)=0$ specifically. For an observable with nonzero
trace, the depolarizing channel drives $\langle O\rangle$ toward
$\text{Tr}(O)/2$, not zero, and the same linear-extrapolation algebra
still applies, just with an extra constant term to solve for.
</Callout>

### Checked against real numbers, not just the formula

Take $p=0.05$ and a state ideally left in $\langle Z\rangle=+1$ (e.g.
$|0\rangle$, with $Z$ traceless). This platform's own `applyKrausChannel`
and `applyChannelRepeatedly`, applied to $\rho_0=|0\rangle\langle0|$
with a single-qubit depolarizing channel built from `PAULI_X`,
`PAULI_Y`, `PAULI_Z`, and `IDENTITY`, confirm $\langle
Z\rangle(1)=(0.95)^1$ and $\langle Z\rangle(3)=(0.95)^3$ to at least 10
decimal digits, exactly the closed form derived above:

$$
\langle Z\rangle(1) \approx 0.9500, \qquad \langle Z\rangle(3) \approx 0.8574
$$

The extrapolated estimate,
$\frac32(0.9500)-\frac12(0.8574)\approx0.9963$, lands close to the true
ideal value $1$, but not exactly on it: the residual gap
($\approx0.37\%$) is the $O(p^2)$ curvature the linear approximation
drops. A **third** measurement (at $\lambda=5$, say) and a quadratic fit
would shrink that residual further, the same "more data points, higher-order
fit" tradeoff any extrapolation makes, just applied to noise instead of,
say, the Wave Mechanics course's numerical time-stepping error.

### Readout error mitigation

A second, independent error source: even a perfectly-prepared state can
be **misread** at measurement time (Qubit Readout Techniques). Model
this with a **confusion matrix** $M$, built once by calibration
(prepare $|0\rangle$, measure many times, record how often "1" is
reported instead; repeat for $|1\rangle$):

$$
M = \begin{pmatrix}P(\text{read }0\mid\text{true }0) & P(\text{read }0\mid\text{true }1)\\ P(\text{read }1\mid\text{true }0) & P(\text{read }1\mid\text{true }1)\end{pmatrix}
$$

A perfect readout is $M=I$. Real superconducting-qubit readout is
typically **asymmetric** (Qubit Readout Techniques, T1 & T2
Decoherence): a $|1\rangle$ is more likely to be misread as $0$ (it can
decay to $|0\rangle$ during the measurement pulse) than a $|0\rangle$ is
to be misread as $1$. Whatever raw distribution the device reports,
$\vec p_{\text{meas}}$, relates to the true distribution by
$\vec p_{\text{meas}} = M\vec p_{\text{true}}$, so **inverting $M$
recovers the true distribution from the raw one**:

$$
\vec p_{\text{true}} = M^{-1}\vec p_{\text{meas}}
$$

## Physical Interpretation

Both techniques share the same shape: accept that any single circuit
execution is noisy, then use EXTRA classical work (more circuit
repetitions at controlled noise levels, or a one-time calibration matrix
inversion) to pull a less-biased number out of many noisy ones. Neither
technique changes what the quantum device physically does on any given
run; both operate entirely on the classical side of the hybrid loop
Hybrid Quantum-Classical Workflows already named. That is the
"classical optimizer can partially compensate" sentence the NISQ
capstone left unexplained, cashed out.

## Worked Example: Correcting a measured distribution

**A device's calibration gives**
$M=\begin{pmatrix}0.98&0.15\\0.02&0.85\end{pmatrix}$ **(a
15%-of-the-time $1\to0$ misread, a realistic asymmetric T1-during-readout
error, versus only 2% the other way). A circuit's raw, uncorrected
measurement gives $\vec p_{\text{meas}}=(0.20,0.80)$. Recover the true
distribution.**

<MatrixGrid
  matrix={romMatrix}
  label="Confusion matrix M (rows = reported outcome, columns = true state)"
  ariaLabel="Confusion matrix M: 0.98 and 0.15 in the top row, 0.02 and 0.85 in the bottom row, showing a 15% one-to-zero misread against only a 2% zero-to-one misread"
/>

First, $\det M = (0.98)(0.85)-(0.15)(0.02) = 0.833-0.003 = 0.830$, so

$$
M^{-1} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.85&-0.15\\-0.02&0.98\end{pmatrix}
$$

Then:

$$
\vec p_{\text{true}} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.85&-0.15\\-0.02&0.98\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.20\\0.80\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.170-0.120\\-0.004+0.784\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{0.830}\begin{pmatrix}0.050\\0.780\end{pmatrix} \approx \begin{pmatrix}0.060\\0.940\end{pmatrix}
$$

<BarChart
  bars={romBars}
  ariaLabel="Bar chart comparing the raw measured distribution, P(0) 0.20 and P(1) 0.80, against the readout-corrected distribution, P(0) 0.06 and P(1) 0.94, with the corrected pair highlighted"
/>

**Check:** $M\vec p_{\text{true}} = (0.98\times0.060+0.15\times0.940,\ 0.02\times0.060+0.85\times0.940) \approx (0.0588+0.1410,\ 0.0012+0.7990) = (0.200,\ 0.800)$,
reproducing $\vec p_{\text{meas}}$ exactly, confirming the inversion.
The correction is substantial: the raw circuit looked like $80\%$
$|1\rangle$, but the readout-corrected estimate is closer to $94\%$
$|1\rangle$ — the extra $14$ points were entirely the asymmetric
misread rate, not the circuit's real behavior, and a VQE cost function
computed from the raw (uncorrected) $80\%$ would be measurably biased.

## Common Mistakes

<Callout type="warning">
**Treating error mitigation as a cheaper substitute for error
correction that happens to also work at scale.** It doesn't. The
threshold theorem (Fault-Tolerant Thresholds) gives EXPONENTIAL error
suppression at POLYNOMIAL physical-qubit overhead, once below threshold.
Error mitigation gives no such scaling: extending zero-noise
extrapolation or probabilistic error cancellation (a related, cited-not-derived
technique using a quasi-probability decomposition of the noise instead
of a linear fit) to deeper, noisier circuits requires exponentially MORE
circuit repetitions to keep the statistical estimate's variance under
control — a proven fundamental limit (Takagi et al. 2022;
Quek et al.), not an engineering gap that better algorithms will close.
Mitigation is a useful NISQ-era tool with no qubit overhead;
it is not a scalable alternative to fault tolerance.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Assuming the two-point extrapolation formula is exact.** It's the
FIRST-ORDER (linear) approximation to a curve that's actually
exponential, $(1-p)^\lambda$. This lesson's own numbers show a
nonzero residual ($\approx0.37\%$) even in a clean, noiseless-simulation
test case; on real hardware, with additional noise sources beyond a
single depolarizing channel, the residual is generally larger still.
"Extrapolated" means "a corrected estimate with reduced bias," not "the
exact answer."
</Callout>

## Summary

- Error mitigation statistically corrects EXPECTATION VALUES computed
  from many noisy runs; it never repairs any single run's quantum
  state, unlike error correction.
- Zero-noise extrapolation: for a traceless observable under a
  depolarizing channel, $\langle O\rangle(\lambda)=(1-p)^\lambda\langle
  O\rangle_{\text{ideal}}$, confirmed here against this platform's own
  `applyChannelRepeatedly`; a two-point linear fit
  ($\frac32\langle O\rangle(1)-\frac12\langle O\rangle(3)$) extrapolates
  to an estimate close to, but not exactly at, the true zero-noise
  value.
- Readout error mitigation inverts a calibrated confusion matrix,
  $\vec p_{\text{true}}=M^{-1}\vec p_{\text{meas}}$, worked numerically
  above to a substantial, verified correction ($80\%\to94\%$).
- Mitigation's fundamental limit: sampling overhead grows (provably,
  eventually exponentially) with circuit noise, the opposite tradeoff
  shape from error correction's polynomial-overhead, exponential-suppression
  threshold theorem. Mitigation is a real NISQ-era tool, not a
  substitute for fault tolerance at scale.
