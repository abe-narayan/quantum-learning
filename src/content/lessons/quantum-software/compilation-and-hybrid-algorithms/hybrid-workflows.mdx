## VQE and QAOA as Software, Not Physics

Quantum Algorithms II built <Term id="vqe">VQE</Term> and QAOA as physics:
minimize $\langle\psi(\vec\theta)|H|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ over
parameters $\vec\theta$. This lesson looks at the same algorithms from a
software angle: what does running one look like, step by step,
and what does this course's compilation machinery cost when it repeats
hundreds of times?

## Tuning by Ear

Think of the loop like tuning a guitar by ear: pluck a string (quantum),
listen for how far off it is (classical), turn the peg a little
(classical), pluck again. Each individual pluck is fast. What makes the
process expensive is repetition: the peg-turning, listening, and
re-plucking cycle runs hundreds of times before the string settles, and
every single pluck pays this course's full compilation cost again from
scratch. The slider further down makes that multiplication concrete.

## The Loop, and Where Its Cost Lives

### The general hybrid loop

Every variational algorithm follows the same repeated pattern:

1. **Classical**: propose parameters $\vec\theta$ (initially a guess,
   later informed by previous iterations).
2. **Quantum**: build and run a circuit implementing the parametrized
   ansatz $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$, going through transpilation
   (previous lessons) and returning measurement statistics.
3. **Classical**: compute the cost function (e.g. $\langle H\rangle$)
   from those measurement results, and decide the next $\vec\theta$
   (Quantum Algorithms II's `runVqe` coordinate-wise search, or a
   gradient-based method).
4. **Repeat** until convergence.

This is what `runVqe` and `runQaoa` (Quantum Algorithms II)
already implement, just described here as a general software pattern
rather than a physics algorithm, the *same* structure Quantum SDKs
Overview named as PennyLane's specific area of strength. Drawn as a
loop, the classical-quantum handoff (and where it repeats from) is
easier to see at a glance than it was in the list above:

### Why compilation overhead compounds here

Every prior lesson's compilation concern, transpilation's <Term id="qubit-routing-swap-overhead">SWAP
overhead</Term>, gate decomposition into native rotations, noise (Noise,
Decoherence & Scaling), applies once PER ITERATION of this loop, not
once total. A VQE run with 100 optimization iterations transpiles and
executes its ansatz circuit 100 separate times; any per-circuit
overhead (SWAP gates, native-gate translation, statistical <Term id="shot-noise-standard-error">shot noise</Term>)
is paid 100 times over, not once. That's often the dominant cost
driver for hybrid algorithms, distinct from a single-shot
circuit's one-time compilation cost.

### What runs where

The quantum device does exactly one job: prepare $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$
and report measurement statistics. *Every other step*, proposing new
parameters, computing the cost function from measurement results,
deciding convergence, runs on ordinary classical hardware. This
division is why these are called "hybrid" algorithms: neither purely
classical nor purely quantum, but a division of labor between the two,
with the quantum device doing only the one piece (native
quantum-mechanical evaluation) classical computers cannot do
efficiently.

## Why the Loop Shape Explains the Runtime

Recognizing this loop structure explains a practical fact about
NISQ-era algorithms (Noise, Decoherence & Scaling): their total runtime
is often dominated by the NUMBER OF ITERATIONS times per-iteration
overhead, not by any single circuit's raw gate count. That's why
minimizing per-circuit compilation and execution overhead (this
course's entire subject) matters disproportionately for hybrid
algorithms.

## Counting the Cost of One Iteration

**A VQE run needs 200 iterations; each iteration's ansatz circuit needs
6 SWAP gates for connectivity (Quantum Compilation & Transpilation) on
top of its native circuit. How many total SWAP gates does the full
optimization run require?**

$$
200 \times 6 = 1200 \text{ SWAP gates total}
$$

A substantial number, even though each individual iteration's
overhead (6 SWAPs) sounds modest. This directly motivates
connectivity-aware ansatz design (choosing which qubits interact to
minimize per-iteration SWAP count) as a high-leverage optimization
for hybrid algorithms in particular.

**Now multiply out the full pipeline for the same run, not just its
SWAP gates.** Estimating one expectation value $\langle H\rangle$
takes more than one circuit execution: each Pauli term in the
Hamiltonian needs its own measurement setting, and each setting needs
enough shots to beat down statistical shot noise. Take a modest
example: 2,000 shots per Pauli term, and a Hamiltonian with 5 Pauli
terms. One cost evaluation is then $5\times2{,}000=10{,}000$ circuit
executions, and one evaluation per iteration across the same 200
iterations gives

$$
200 \times 5 \times 2{,}000 = 2{,}000{,}000 \text{ circuit executions}
$$

for the full optimization run. Two million executions, to estimate one
ground-state energy, from a loop whose every individual piece sounded
modest.

Two million executions is a lot of jobs to put through a shared cloud
device's queue, which raises an obvious scheduling question:

Shot count still isn't the worst of it on real cloud hardware. The
loop is *sequential*: the optimizer cannot propose iteration 43's
parameters until iteration 42's measurement results come back, so each
parameter update is a separate job submitted to the device's queue
(Simulators vs. Real Hardware's queue-time constraint). Wall-clock
time for the run is then roughly 200 times the queue wait per job, and
on a shared device that queue wait routinely dwarfs the circuit's
execution time. This is why hybrid algorithms feel so much
slower in practice than their raw circuit counts suggest: the queue is
paid once per iteration, and iterations cannot be batched away.

## Treating the Classical Half as Plumbing

<Callout type="note">
**Treating VQE/QAOA's classical optimizer as a minor implementation
detail.** It's an equal partner in the algorithm's structure. The
quantum device does ONE well-defined job (state preparation and
measurement); everything else, including the decision of what
to try next, is ordinary classical computation.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning">
**Underestimating per-iteration overhead because a single iteration's
cost looks small.** As the worked example shows, modest per-iteration
overhead multiplies substantially across the many iterations a real
optimization run needs.
</Callout>

## Practice Questions

1. List the four steps of the general hybrid loop, in order.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   1. **Classical:** propose parameters $\vec\theta$, initially a guess
      and later informed by previous iterations.
   2. **Quantum:** build and run a circuit implementing the parametrized
      ansatz $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$, through transpilation, and
      return measurement statistics.
   3. **Classical:** compute the cost function, typically $\langle
      H\rangle$, from those measurement results, and decide the next
      $\vec\theta$.
   4. **Repeat** until convergence.

   Three properties of that list carry the rest of the lesson, and are
   worth reading off it deliberately.

   **What crosses each boundary is classical.** Going down, a list of
   real numbers, the parameters. Coming up, a list of measurement counts.
   No quantum state ever leaves the device, which is why the loop can be
   split across a laptop and a machine in another country at all.

   **Three of the four steps are classical.** The device's entire job is
   step 2. Proposing, evaluating and deciding are ordinary numerical
   computing, which is what "hybrid" is naming, and question 3 is about
   why that division falls where it does.

   **The loop is strictly sequential.** Step 1 of iteration $k+1$ needs
   step 3 of iteration $k$. Iteration 43's circuit does not exist until
   iteration 42's results come back, so the iterations cannot be
   submitted together, batched, or run in parallel. That single
   structural fact is why a shared device's queue wait is paid once per
   iteration rather than once per run, and it is the cost that cannot be
   negotiated down by accepting less precision.

   The wrong turn is reading the loop as "a quantum algorithm with some
   classical glue around it." The classical optimizer is not plumbing; it
   is the part that decides what the algorithm does next, and the quality
   of the answer depends on it as much as on the device.

   </details>

2. For a VQE run with 500 iterations and 4 SWAP gates of overhead per
   iteration, compute the total SWAP-gate count across the full run.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   $$
   500 \times 4 = 2{,}000 \text{ SWAP gates.}
   $$

   And since each SWAP compiles to 3 CNOTs, that is $6{,}000$ extra
   two-qubit gates, executed on the noisiest operation the device has,
   to route a circuit whose logical content never changed.

   The point of the multiplication is where the four came from. It is a
   *per-circuit* cost: four SWAPs is what routing this ansatz onto this
   chip's connectivity costs, once. In a single-shot circuit you would
   pay it once and stop thinking about it. In a loop you pay it on every
   iteration, because every iteration submits a fresh circuit with new
   parameter values, and transpilation happens again from scratch.

   **The fuller bill is larger still, and multiplies the same way.** One
   cost evaluation is not one circuit execution. Each Pauli term in the
   Hamiltonian needs its own measurement setting, and each setting needs
   enough shots to beat down statistical noise. Take the lesson's modest
   figures, 2,000 shots per term and 5 terms:

   $$
   500 \times 5 \times 2{,}000 = 5{,}000{,}000 \text{ circuit executions}
   $$

   for the run. Five million executions to estimate one ground-state
   energy, and 2,000 SWAP gates is the routing line of that same bill.

   The wrong turn is treating four SWAPs as small and therefore
   negligible. Every quantity in a hybrid algorithm's cost is small per
   iteration; that is what makes it a NISQ algorithm. The costs are
   multiplied by the iteration count, and the iteration count is set by
   how hard the optimization is, not by anything the circuit designer
   controls. Which is why connectivity-aware ansatz design is
   high leverage here specifically: shaving the four to two halves a
   thousand two-qubit gates across the run.

   </details>

3. Which specific step in the hybrid loop is the *only* one that must run
   on an actual quantum device, and why can't classical hardware perform
   it efficiently instead? Then state the honest limit of that "can't"
   for the ansätze actually used in practice.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   **Step 2:** preparing the ansatz state $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ and
   measuring it.

   The way to see it is to walk the loop asking what data each step
   touches. Steps 1, 3 and 4 handle lists of real numbers: parameters
   going out, counts coming back, a cost value, a convergence test.
   Ordinary classical machines do all of that routinely, and nothing
   about it is quantum. Step 2 is the only one whose input is a
   parameterized circuit and whose output is a sample from that circuit's
   output distribution.

   **Why a classical machine struggles.** Doing step 2 classically means
   representing the state, and a general $n$-qubit state needs $2^n$
   complex amplitudes, the wall the Simulating Quantum Systems course
   computes exactly. Beyond roughly fifty qubits there is no machine to
   hold it.

   **The honest form of that claim**, since "cannot do it efficiently"
   is doing heavy lifting. What is established is that no efficient
   classical algorithm is *known* for sampling from a general
   parameterized quantum circuit's output, and there are
   complexity-theoretic reasons to believe none exists. What is not
   established is that the *specific* ansätze used in practice are hard.
   Low-depth circuits on sparse interaction graphs are often exactly the
   states tensor networks handle well, which is why classical simulation
   has repeatedly caught up with variational demonstrations, and why no
   NISQ variational advantage has been demonstrated. The right statement
   is that step 2 is the only step with any *prospect* of being
   classically hard, and whether a given ansatz realizes that prospect is
   a live question.

   Three tempting alternatives, each of which puts the quantum work in
   the wrong place.

   **"Proposing the next parameters, because the search space is
   exponential."** It is a continuous optimization over a handful of
   angles, and classical optimizers do that for a living. A large search
   space is not a quantum one.

   **"Computing the cost, because the expectation value sums over $2^n$
   amplitudes."** The $2^n$ sum is performed by the device, physically,
   in the act of preparing and measuring the state. What comes back to
   the classical side is a table of counts, and averaging counts is
   arithmetic over a few numbers.

   **"Checking convergence, because the estimates are noisy."** Comparing
   two noisy numbers is still classical arithmetic. Noise in the input
   does not make a computation quantum.

   </details>

4. Explain why PennyLane's differentiable-programming design (Quantum
   SDKs Overview) is particularly well-suited to STEP 3 of this lesson's
   general loop specifically. Then say where the gradient is actually
   computed, and what that costs.

   <details className="answer-reveal">
   <summary>Answer</summary>

   Because step 3's second half, "decide the next $\vec\theta$," is
   exactly where a gradient is wanted, and PennyLane's design is that the
   gradient falls out of the framework rather than being assembled.

   Step 3 has two halves and they are unequal. Computing $\langle
   H\rangle$ from measurement counts is a weighted average, and any
   framework does it. Deciding the next parameters is an optimization
   step, and a gradient-based optimizer needs $\partial\langle H\rangle/
   \partial\theta_i$ for every parameter. PennyLane makes a circuit an
   ordinary differentiable function inside a familiar autodiff
   framework, so writing that optimizer is writing the same code you
   would write for any differentiable model, and composing quantum and
   classical layers into one trainable object is the default rather than
   a project.

   **One clarification that makes the fit sharper rather than vaguer.**
   The gradient is not computed analytically inside the classical step.
   The parameter-shift rule evaluates it by running the circuit again at
   shifted parameter values, so obtaining a gradient reaches back into
   step 2 and multiplies the circuit executions in question 2's
   arithmetic by roughly two per parameter. PennyLane's contribution is
   that this bookkeeping, which circuits to run, at which shifts, and how
   to combine the results, happens behind the differentiation call
   instead of in the user's code. So it makes step 3 ergonomic by
   automating work that lands in step 2.

   Three overstatements to avoid, each of which turns an ergonomics claim
   into something false.

   **Not exclusivity.** The parameter-shift rule is general and every
   major SDK implements it. Any of them can run this loop.

   **Not exactness.** Parameter-shift is exact wherever it applies, in
   PennyLane and everywhere else. It is not a finite-difference
   approximation, and it carries no step-size tradeoff.

   **Not relocation of the optimizer.** The optimizer stays classical and
   the round trip to the device stays. Nothing about differentiable
   programming removes the sequential structure of question 1, or the
   queue wait paid on every iteration, which on a shared cloud device
   usually dominates the wall-clock time regardless of which SDK wrote
   the circuit.

   </details>

## Summary

- Every variational algorithm follows the same loop: classical parameter
  proposal, quantum circuit execution, classical cost evaluation,
  repeat, what `runVqe`/`runQaoa` already implement.
- Compilation overhead (SWAP gates, native-gate decomposition) applies
  PER ITERATION, compounding substantially across a full optimization
  run, often the dominant cost for hybrid algorithms.
- The quantum device's role is narrowly scoped to state preparation and
  measurement; every other step is ordinary classical computation.
